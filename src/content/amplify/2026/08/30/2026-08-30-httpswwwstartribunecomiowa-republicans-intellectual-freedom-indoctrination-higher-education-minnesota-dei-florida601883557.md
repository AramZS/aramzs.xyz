<p>In 2025, Iowa Republicans <a href="https://www.desmoinesregister.com/story/news/politics/2025/06/12/governor-kim-reynolds-signs-law-requiring-university-of-iowa-create-intellectual-freedom-center/84120327007/">passed a state law</a> requiring the University of Iowa to create a new “Center for Intellectual Freedom.” Students, they argued, were being oppressed by all that diversity, equity and inclusion, and surely would be clamoring for such freedom.</p>
<p>Taxpayer money poured in, and then poured right out again, including a huge speaking fee to conservative influencer Christopher Rufo (who indirectly <a href="https://www.startribune.com/mn-somali-fraud-money-terrorism-evidence/601532958">helped bring ICE to Minnesota</a>) at an<a href="https://www.thegazette.com/news/emails-ui-not-supporting-center-for-intellectual-freedom/article_8a069174-382d-44a9-9872-c6d21aaa5184.html"> $87,000 inaugural event</a> last December. What didn’t<a href="https://www.desmoinesregister.com/story/news/education/2026/04/22/university-of-iowa-center-for-intellectual-freedom-begins/89192875007"> pour in was students</a>.</p>
<p>But don’t worry. Now Iowa Republicans have<a href="https://www.desmoinesregister.com/story/news/politics/2026/06/05/iowa-center-intellectual-freedom-civics-courses-required-2028/90405293007/"> passed new legislation</a> requiring all UI students to attend courses with lectures on keen topics like “Why Capitalism Rocks!” and “The Midterm Election of 1994, the ‘Republican revolution’ and the ‘Contract with America.’”</p>
<p>In other words, in Iowa, this “freedom” is now mandatory.</p>
<p>It’s a difficult time for those of us who care about higher education in this country. In states governed by Republicans, the assault on public colleges and universities keeps ratcheting up as lawmakers and their lackeys assert ever-greater control over course content.</p>
<p>But at the same time, Democratic states have not particularly covered themselves in glory when it comes to higher ed. There’s no counter-movement yet in blue states that offers a robust counter-vision for higher education — that it might, first of all, be actually good for society, or that it might provide the kind of education it seems students actually want.</p>

<p>But there could be. And should be. In other words, the struggles in Iowa present Minnesota with an opportunity to advertise across the border, and an opportunity to start really pushing back at the attacks from the right.</p>
<p>Right-wing attacks on higher education aren’t new, but they have not only intensified, they’ve gotten smarter, attacking curriculum requirements, course content and accreditation. For example, Florida recently declared an<a href="https://www.miamiherald.com/news/politics-government/state-politics/article315198031.html"> introductory sociology class ineligible</a> for core curriculum credit, meaning that Gov. Ron DeSantis and his cronies can claim they aren’t censoring anyone, while removing the incentive for students to study a discipline that teaches about structural inequality and how to address it.</p>
<p>After years of whining about meritocracy, Republican governments are pouring tax dollars into hiring mostly mediocre conservative academics in<a href="https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/feb/26/conservative-civic-centers-public-colleges"> states all around the country</a>. Then in some cases, like Iowa, they are making their courses mandatory when students don’t want to go. In Texas, worst of all, professors’ syllabi are being scrutinized for teaching students forbidden content on race, gender, sexuality and other topics that frighten Republicans.</p>
<p>I called Dominique Baker, associate professor of education and public policy at the University of Delaware, because<a href="https://www.insidehighered.com/author/dominique-j-baker"> she’s been writing about</a> the shape and stakes for American higher education, and asked her how she understood this “red-state vision” for higher ed. She said that while there have always been attacks around the edges, things are worse now. Texas, for example, was<a href="https://www.texastribune.org/2026/06/04/texas-dream-act-federal-appeals-lawsuit/"> the first state to offer in-state tuition</a> to undocumented immigrants, but state officials and the Trump administration combined efforts to kill that policy.</p>
<p>“In general,” Baker said, conservative states have a vision for colleges and universities “around job creation and indoctrination.” And they have become savvier, learning how institutions are really organized and “how to hit higher ed where it hurts.”</p>
<p>At the same time, she said, “you can’t work at indoctrinating other people and not indoctrinate [yourself]. I really do think they were shocked at how few people signed up for their civic centers. They genuinely believe students hate the class options available to them and are dying for these new revisionist versions of history,” and they don’t know what to do when only a handful of students show up.</p>

<p>This lack of understanding ought to open the way for blue-state colleges and universities to invest in a true liberal arts and sciences education that encourages students to explore themselves and the world, and to develop a habit of mind that supports lifelong learning and flexibility in encountering the ever-changing struggles of the modern age.</p>
<p>Instead, Baker told me, “the blue-state vision for higher ed right now, in general, [is] job creation only. At its most fundamental, most Democratic administrations of states are thinking that the No. 1 priority of college is around the labor market.” That’s good for what Baker calls their “bipartisanship fetish,” because if both sides agree it’s all about jobs, it feels like there’s a chance for collaboration.</p>
<p>But there’s another way. Instead of focusing on the money an individual makes through college, we can consider what’s good for society. That means some people are going to need to choose to become teachers or social workers and thus earn less income. If we rely on the current model of judging schools based on the debt-earnings ratio alone, Baker noted, “going into teaching will harm their school by doing good for their community.” Instead, she said, we should “orient the institution toward one that helps to serve the public and the greater good.” And students, both she and I think, want that — if they can afford it.</p>
<p>As the new academic year begins, and as I welcome students both new and returning, here’s what I see are the stakes: Minnesota (and other states like us) have a chance to compete for students both within our borders and across them. College students don’t like what Republican lawmakers are selling when it comes to college.</p>
<p>Here’s an opportunity to move past the job-training consensus that has left our universities stripped threadbare and too driven by trends and vibes (I’ll yell about AI and higher ed another day). But I promise you, students want real educations and will come for them, rather than be forced to listen to a CEO tell them that capitalism rocks.</p>
