<p><em><strong>Annie Lowrey is a staff writer at The Atlantic, where she writes about the economy and politics. Her new book, <a href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/0063442558?lv=shuf&amp;channelId=500&amp;plpRedirect=mhFallback">The Time Tax: How the Government Wastes Our Time and How to Fix It</a>, does a masterful job at explaining the administrative burdens that people encounter when they interact with the safety net. She connects policy and people, right from the opening chapter, where she writes:</strong></em></p>
<blockquote><p>To make sure that the safety net catches us, to ensure that our social insurance programs insure us, and to exercise our rights as citizens, we toil and labor and try. We work as our own health- care administrators. Our own tax professionals. Our own social workers. Our own legal advocates, long- term- care policy brokers, and permit expediters. Here in the United States, with our deep- seated skepticism of bureaucracy, we nevertheless spend a lot of time mired in bureaucracy, acting as our own bureaucrats.</p><p>In my two decades as a reporter, I have mostly understood this situation as a fact of life. Government programs exist; people have to figure them out. Laws and regulations exist; people have to obey them. That is how it goes. But at some point, I started thinking about these pervasive administrative hassles as a time tax: a levy of paperwork, effort, delay, and aggravation imposed on Americans in exchange for rights and benefits that are putatively theirs.</p></blockquote>
<p>In a piece at the <a href="https://lithub.com/how-american-bureaucracy-literally-steals-years-from-your-life/">Literary Hub</a>, she puts some numbers on the scale of this problem — how much time and money is spent dealing with burdens, and how much of it is shifted to citizens:</p>
<blockquote><p>Washington currently estimates that Americans spend 12 billion hours a year filling out government forms, meaning the average person spends six 8-hour workdays filing their taxes, signing up for Medicaid, renewing their driver’s licenses, and applying for government loans. At prevailing wages, this uncompensated labor is worth $430 billion a year. If Washington were to create a Department of Bureaucracy to take on the paperwork it shunts to citizens, the agency would require 5.9 million employees, making it four times the size of the active-duty military. The DOB’s payroll would be larger than that of every other federal department combined.</p></blockquote>
<p><em><strong>Annie interviewed Pam Herd and I about our research on administrative burdens in 2021 for a widely influential piece for <a href="https://www.theatlantic.com/politics/archive/2021/07/how-government-learned-waste-your-time-tax/619568/">The Atlantic</a>. So I was excited to turn the tables and ask Annie some questions about her new book.</strong></em></p>
<p><em><strong>Don: What are the main themes of the book you want people to understand?</strong></em></p>
<p>Annie: First, the three hours you spend in line at the DMV, the struggle your neighbor faces in securing long-term care for an elderly parent, the four hours of phone calls it takes to sort out a provider-network issue, the twelve hours it requires to fill out the IRS 1040: These are not isolated issues. They’re related issues. Maybe they’re even one issue.</p>
<p>Second, we should be mad about it! We’re handing the government $8 trillion a year. We’re getting crummy, humiliating, incompetent, expensive, laborious, offensive, outmoded, and inefficient service delivery in return. I understand the many structural barriers to improving state capacity, reforming government bureaucracy, and stamping out administrative burdens, as well as the forces weakening feedback loops in this particular arena. Nevertheless. We should be angry, and the government should do better.</p>

<p><em><strong>When your original piece “The Time Tax” was published in The Atlantic in 2021 it had a real impact. The term made its way into the popular discourse. It popped up in federal documents and in state executive orders. But you write about a lot of topics. What made you think “this is the one I want to sink years of my life writing a book about?”</strong></em></p>
<p>The issue was unusual in that it was salient to just about every American and yet still somehow obscure. I’d describe the concept of time taxes or administrative burdens when I was out doing interviews, and end up collecting a half-dozen stories—awful, depressing, funny, enraging stories, from people from all walks of life—every time.</p>
<p>The reporting felt gratifying, and surely that helped convince me to write the book. Plus, many agencies had already made or were interested in making policy changes to reduce burdens—there’s so much more we could do, and that made it feel like a project worth committing to as well.</p>
<p><em><strong>As an academic, I study this topic by looking at policy interventions and data. You actually talk to the people directly affected! What did you learn from those conversations? What surprised you the most?</strong></em></p>
<p>The sheer range of problems people encountered always shocked me. The hassle of figuring out how to send a fax from your smartphone. (Why does anyone have to send anything by fax anymore?) The challenge of answering a seemingly straightforward question, like who lives in your household. (What is a household anyway? What if you’re constantly moving, or friends and family members are forever bouncing in and out of the place where you’re living?) The conundrum of having disabling medical symptoms without having a diagnosis. (How are you supposed to get diagnosed if you can’t see the doctor?)</p>
<p><em><strong>What are the solutions to reducing the time tax? What can we do as individuals? What should policymakers be doing?</strong></em></p>
<p>Oh gosh. For individuals, complain. Complain to the taxpayer advocate, your mayor, your senator. Complain to the head of your local voting board. Complain to your Medicaid administrator. Engage! Karen for good! Rage away!</p>
<p>As for policymakers, they have a million options. Maybe that’s intimidating—so many problems to fix. Maybe that’s inspiring—so many fixes to implement.</p>
<p>Collect uniform, holistic data on administrative burdens. Require states, departments, and offices to collect and disseminate churn, procedural-denial, and uptake numbers—better yet, make them responsible for eliminating procedural denials, reducing churn, and increasing participation rates. Extend re-enrollment periods. Get rid of asset tests. Get rid of work-reporting requirements. Harmonize income requirements across programs. Create cross-enrollment options. Create no-wrong-door systems. Expand categorical-eligibility initiatives. Federalize whatever you can federalize. Pass some kind of Paperwork Reduction Act Reduction Act, and reform the Administrative Procedure Act. Empower civil servants to achieve outcomes, not tick off boxes. Get the IRS to prefill tax forms. Replace TANF with an expanded CTC. Make more programs tax programs. Make tax programs visible to recipients. Reform public-service hiring practices. Require agencies to use agile development approaches. Increase government data-sharing optionality. Listen to frontline civil servants—they have so many good ideas!</p>
<p><em><strong>Politically, the world has changed since you first started writing the book. The Trump administration has taken a very different approach to frictions than the Biden administration, most obviously with new work reporting requirements for Medicaid. Does your book offer a preview of what life will be like as more and more of these policies are put in place?</strong></em></p>
<p>Unfortunately, I think so. We’re imposing work-reporting requirements on more than 20 million people. We’re asking 63-year-olds to submit timesheets in exchange for nutrition aid, and homeless individuals to volunteer in exchange for insurance coverage. Who knows how many hours people will spend complying with the work-reporting requirements—which, in Medicaid at least, nobody expects to actually increase employment rates. Things will be much worse for the people who don’t realize they have to comply with them, or can’t comply with them.</p>
<p>People are going to die. Health clinics are going to shut down. The unsheltered homeless population is going to increase. Food pantries are already struggling with rising demand.</p>
<p><em><strong>One paradox I’ve always experienced is that as individuals, everyone complains about red tape. But collectively, there is no political movement to fix it. Why do you think that is? Is it possible to create a politics to reduce the time tax?</strong></em></p>
<p>I agree that administrative burdens don’t really fire people up and get them to the polls. They’re regressive; they’re distributed across programs and applied at different levels of government; they’re fussy and finicky and hard to understand; they’re subtle, turning means into ends and ends into means. They aren’t understood as a unitary issue. They’re low-salience. There’s a reason that most politicians don’t bother to do this kind of stuff.</p>
<p>But administrative burdens are about to get much more prominent, tangible, and common, thanks to HR1. And I wonder if the law won’t end up opening up the door to comprehensive reform. Maybe we’d benefit from reframing administrative burdens as illegitimate, government-imposed red tape, rather than anti-fraud, pro-work measures. Maybe we should demand politicians give us the same consideration they give small businesses, at least when it comes to uncertainty and compliance costs.</p>
<p><em><strong>What is the question I should have asked?</strong></em></p>
<p>Not so much a question for me, but a question I have for you and your readers: Americans’ understanding of class, race, and the root causes of poverty have changed significantly over the past ten to fifteen years. Has that changed our tolerance for <a href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/perspectives-on-politics/article/administrative-burdens-mass-political-effects-how-the-administration-of-medicaid-and-elections-shapes-mass-voter-turnout/FC4784E6DC61FB303ED38F1995EBA485">administrative burdens</a>? How much, and how so?</p>
<p><em><strong>We do have some prior research that suggests that people <a href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/puar.13853">support burden reductions in order to ensure access</a> and are less supportive of adding burdens <a href="https://donmoynihan.substack.com/p/what-does-the-public-actually-think?utm_source=publication-search">to reduce fraud</a> than politicians seem to exist. But the big question Annie asks holds: have Americans become so sick of paying the time tax that it has created an untapped constituency that some enterprising politician can appeal to?</strong></em></p>
