<div><article><figure><figcaption><span><div data-server-side-rendered="true"><p>Amazon founder Jeff Bezos, Donald Trump Jr., Google CEO Sundar Pichai, Tesla CEO Elon Musk, Usha Vance, Interior Secretary nominee Doug Burgum and Vice President-elect JD Vance applaud during the inauguration of U.S. President-elect Donald Trump in the U.S. Capitol Rotunda in January 2025 in Washington, DC. </p></div></span><span><span>Pool/Getty Images</span></span></figcaption></figure><div data-block-type="text" data-dropcap="false"><p>It's Mark Zuckerberg who I find particularly perplexing.</p></div><div data-block-type="text" data-dropcap="false"><p>A few years ago — meaning even just since the pandemic — Zuckerberg announced that his company, Facebook, would be pivoting to a new focus. The future, he claimed, was one in which people interacted with one another in virtual reality spaces, the "metaverse." He demonstrated clunky looking environments in which legless people held little meetings, <a data-link="native" href="https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2021/oct/28/facebook-mark-zuckerberg-meta-metaverse">telling the world</a> that "the metaverse will be the successor to the mobile internet." He renamed his company "Meta." He spent <a data-link="native" href="https://www.wsj.com/tech/ai/meta-plans-to-shift-spending-away-from-the-metaverse-d0ac3b7f">about $80 billion</a>.</p></div><div><div><p>Article continues below this ad</p></div><p data-block-type="ad" data-large-id="Inline-1" data-small-id="A300" data-unit="hnp-ctinsider.com/article_dtc"></p></div><p></p><p></p><div data-block-type="text" data-dropcap="false"><p>And — predictably, in my opinion — it was a flop. The metaverse was doomed by its inescapable lameness. Zuckerberg had made an incredibly big bet, and lost.</p></div><div data-block-type="text" data-dropcap="false"><p>So where is he now? Running Meta, a company now focused intently on artificial intelligence. If he paid a price for being spectacularly wrong, it's not obvious from the outside. If there were any repercussions, they don't appear to have been significant.</p></div><div data-block-type="text" data-dropcap="false"><p>It sounds familiar, doesn't it? An intensely wealthy person breezily avoiding any negative effects from their own mistakes? Zuckerberg's metaverse serves as a relatively innocuous and relatively expensive example of the age of impunity in which we now exist, one in which money — and the promise of making money for others — serves as a get-out-of-everything-free card.</p></div><div><div><p>Article continues below this ad</p></div><p data-block-type="ad" data-large-id="Inline-2" data-small-id="NTV7ARD" data-unit="hnp-ctinsider.com/article_dtc"></p></div><div data-block-type="text" data-dropcap="false"><p>The premier example here, of course, is President Donald Trump. Trump has taken the idea to what must be its extreme, advocating and earning sweeping immunity for his actions as president <a data-link="native" href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/politics/2024/07/01/supreme-court-trump-immunity">from the Supreme Court</a>. His first term in office was pockmarked with disasters, including his vilification of medical experts during the pandemic and, of course, his efforts to subvert the 2020 election. He spirited away classified documents, triggering a Justice Department search of his home. And then … he was back in the White House.</p></div><div data-block-type="text" data-dropcap="false"><p>Since his inauguration last year, he's embraced the idea that he can act with impunity. He's withheld appropriated spending, <a data-link="native" href="https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/trump-administration/trump-administration-concedes-canceled-research-grants-blue-states-rcna589129">mostly from states</a> that <a data-link="native" href="https://www.politico.com/news/2026/07/29/fema-democratic-disaster-requests-trump-01013938">didn't vote for him</a>, in violation of the separation of powers. He launched a war in Iran that seems not to have been quite as breezy a success as he imagined. He orchestrated an agreement from the IRS not to poke around in his finances, the political equivalent of wearing a T-shirt that reads, "Please stop asking if I'm a criminal."</p></div><div data-block-type="text" data-dropcap="false"><p>For all of his obvious deviations from his constitutionally assigned role and despite his <a data-link="native" href="https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/polls/donald-trump-approval-rating-polls.html">deepening unpopularity</a>, the people who could do something about his actions — particularly the Republican-led Congress — have opted not to. In fact, they've often opted to <em>aid</em> him, making the bet that there is more to be gained from allying with Trump than challenging him. They are investors in his efforts as surely as are shareholders in Meta: each figures that holding on a bit longer is the safer, more lucrative play.</p></div><div data-block-type="text" data-dropcap="false"><p>As much as Trump represents this moment of impunity, though, it's important to recognize that he is a beneficiary of it more than its creator. Perhaps the clearest point of origin for the idea that a certain amount of wealth could shield you from facing repercussions for your actions was the recession that began nearly 20 years ago. Banks that made historically terrible, economy-crushing bets had the dirt brushed off their knees and were gently put back on their feet by the federal government.</p></div><div><div><p>Article continues below this ad</p></div><p data-block-type="ad" data-large-id="Inline-3" data-small-id="Inline-1" data-unit="hnp-ctinsider.com/article_dtc"></p></div><div data-block-type="text" data-dropcap="false"><p>In fact, in the wake of those bailouts, the government began shrugging at white-collar crime in general. The IRS <a data-link="native" href="https://www.cbo.gov/publication/56467">conducted fewer audits</a>. Fewer white-collar criminals faced prosecution. That is true nationally and it is true in Connecticut. This decline has occurred <a data-link="native" href="https://tracreports.org/reports/760/">despite the rise of identity theft</a> as an increasing proportion of white-collar criminality.</p></div><div data-block-type="text" data-dropcap="false"><p>The net worths of the wealthiest Americans boomed. In 2015, Elon Musk was worth <a data-link="native" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_World%27s_Billionaires_2015">about $12 billion</a>. Today, he's worth <a data-link="native" href="https://www.bloomberg.com/billionaires/">about 60 times that</a>. His net worth has jumped $100 billion this year alone.</p></div><div data-block-type="text" data-dropcap="false"><p>By the time Trump was running for office again in 2024, Musk could take a small fraction of his net worth (a sum totaling hundreds of millions of dollars) and dedicate it to trying to get his friend elected. He offered up pocket change, seven-figure checks, to likely Republican voters, triggering probes in <a data-link="native" href="https://apnews.com/article/musk-million-sweepstakes-lottery-pennsylvania-krasner-a84854e6397ac1440ffb54b13facacf2">Pennsylvania</a> and, later, <a data-link="native" href="https://www.jurist.org/news/2026/07/wisconsin-elections-panel-finds-probable-cause-that-elon-musk-violated-election-bribery-law/">Wisconsin</a>. But his investment paid off: he was given sweeping authority to slash government spending once Trump returned to office, with <a data-link="native" href="https://healthpolicy-watch.news/the-human-cost-one-year-after-the-us-took-a-chainsaw-to-global-health/">disastrous effects on global health</a>.</p></div><div><div><p>Article continues below this ad</p></div><p data-block-type="ad" data-large-id="Inline-4" data-small-id="Inline-2" data-unit="hnp-ctinsider.com/article_dtc"></p></div><div data-block-type="text" data-dropcap="false"><p>Non-billionaire Americans would be justified in feeling cynical, or even in making the same bet that Republican legislators and Meta investors have. If no one is paying a cost for ignoring the rules, why should I play by them? If someone else figured out a shortcut, why don't I simply follow them down that path?</p></div><div data-block-type="text" data-dropcap="false"><p>This impunity is not simply the fault of bad actors running rampant. It is also the fault of those enablers, the millions of people who have decided that the minor inconvenience of speaking out against antisocial or criminal behavior isn't worth paying a minimal cost. Why be the one guy on Wall Street playing by the old rules of accountability? Very few of the people benefiting from your shamelessness are going to call you out on it — unless there’s some significant countervailing, moral or monetary benefit from doing so. At this point, it seems as though there isn’t.</p></div><div data-block-type="text" data-dropcap="false"><p>In fact, one of the sharpest ironies of the moment is that Trump's power derives from people who support him because of a deep-seated fury at a perceived group of elites, people who succeed where they haven't. After a decrease following 9/11, cynicism about leadership in the United States has been growing — paving the way for leaders willing to dive headfirst into the swamp of self-interest and corruption.</p></div><div><div><p>Article continues below this ad</p></div><p data-block-type="ad" data-large-id="Inline-5" data-small-id="Inline-3" data-unit="hnp-ctinsider.com/article_dtc"></p></div><div data-block-type="text" data-dropcap="false"><p>The idea that wealthy people — and particularly wealthy tech interests — face few repercussions for their actions permeates our culture. The backlash against data centers is obviously in part because it feels to many Americans like a mandate from those protected classes. This large building will be placed in your community, straining your power supply, because we need it and you can't do anything about it. But this is also one of the few places where average people can push back, so they do.</p></div><div data-block-type="text" data-dropcap="false"><p>They appear to be uncompelled by the idea that <a data-link="native" href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/07/27/technology/meta-data-center-louisiana.html">Meta needs such facilities</a> for its focus on <s>the metaverse</s> AI. Perhaps Zuckerberg ought to do more podcasts where he explains that corporate America <a data-link="native" href="https://fortune.com/2025/01/13/zuckerberg-says-companies-need-more-masculine-energy/">needs to lean into "masculine energy</a>" or attend another event at the Trump White House. Surely aligning oneself publicly with the avatar for corporate and political impunity ought to instill trust in the people.</p></div><p></p><p></p><p></p><p></p><p></p><p></p><p></p><p></p><p></p><p></p><p></p><p></p></article></div>
