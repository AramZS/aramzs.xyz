<p><em>What does it mean to be a canonical band? Is it one of those “household name”-style groups where most people who have heard music know about? Is it an artist who changed the art of music, the way we listen to music, or the music business forever? It’s all of those things, but the Gories are none of those things. But the Gories are a canonical band in that they have created a legacy that has stood the test of time; they have influenced multiple generations of bands in their little corner of the music scene. </em></p>
<p><em>They have about 100 times as many fans now as they did when they were first active between 1986-1992 and more find them every day, particularly because they’re one of the iconic garage punk bands of any era.</em></p>
<p><em>There was no other band I could have possibly chosen as the first artist in my new series, the Trick Bag Canon. Whether or not a 40-something-year-old person having a “favorite band” is embarrassing, the Gories are unequivocally my favorite band. I explain that in exhausting detail in this first installment in the Trick Bag Canon, why exactly they’re so influential.</em></p>
<p><em>The fastest way to give a publication an identity is to establish a canon, and because the identity of Trick Bag is the identity of Martin Douglas, then it’s best if I formalized my own personal canon. I wouldn’t call that an act of self-importance, especially because no one is forcing you to read this newsletter. I think of it more as giving flowers to the artists who have influenced my tastes in music, who give me a reason to continue writing about music, to continue to find the wonder in music.</em></p>

<p>“We figured we’d have a 45 [seven-inch] record before the whole thing was over and that would have been our musical legacy,” underground rock ‘n roll icon Mick Collins <a href="https://turnit-down.blogspot.com/2013/04/mick-collins-interview-gories-dirtbombs.html">told veteran rock journalist and DJ Rich Tupica</a> in a 2009 interview about Detroit trio the Gories. “I don’t know how it kept going from there.”</p>
<p>The realest of the real punk rock bands were founded on low expectations. A desperately small percentage of punk groups are guided by delusions of grandeur—awards, arena tours, even the idea of having a well-regarded body of work. But then again, who am I to presume the motivations of others? Maybe Turnstile knew they were going to pivot from contemporary DMV hardcore to shameless 311 retreads and win a Grammy for it this whole time.</p>
<p>By Collins’s estimation, the Gories seriously and legitimately broke up three times during their initial run as a band. They didn’t start playing to anything near sizable crowds until their reunion tour with fellow garage punk legends the Oblivians, for two weeks across Europe during Barack Obama’s first year in the Oval Office. They recorded three LPs and a smattering of singles in basements and bedrooms with mostly cheap gear (co-singer/guitarist Danny Kroha had a Fender Jaguar that cost, according to Collins, “more than my guitar and Peg[gy O’Neill, drummer]’s drums put together”).</p>
<p>With slender means, the Gories still became an influential—<em>essential</em> in my personal opinion—band for multiple generations of punk bands. Not just bands, either; they’ve been a stylistic and aesthetic influence for at least one punk rock writer’s style, tastes, humor, and general worldview. I can’t and obviously don’t want to speak for anybody else, but when I first heard the Gories, I immediately knew I found the band I had been waiting for my whole life.</p>
<div class="rw-embed-wrapper"><embed src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/WRGwq_nIEQc" type="video/mp4"/></div>
<p>Mick Collins doesn’t remember a time in his life before listening to rock ‘n roll music. In the previously referenced Tupica interview, he mentioned hearing Chuck Berry and Little Richard before he could walk. The Collins family record collection has ascended to punk folklore at this point; Mick’s father was a mechanic who regularly serviced the vehicle of the man who ran Detroit’s largest record distributor, and he would often receive records for free. His older sisters maintained what became a giant record collection until that responsibility fell on him.</p>
<p>In high school, Collins played trombone in jazz band, which kickstarted his life as a musician in earnest. By the time he became a young adult, he was a mod, a fledgling DJ (perhaps even a hobbyist in that realm), and playing in bands (first in a group called the U-Boats, then a guitarless punk band called the Floor Tasters). The mod scene in Detroit was a tight-knit community—not in that everybody necessarily got along, but certainly everybody knew each other—and that’s how Collins met another musician in the scene by the name of Danny Kroha.</p>
<p>“I had been in a couple bands, but [...] Danny was the one in a real band,” Collins told <a href="https://riotfest.org/2021/07/20/the-gories-history/">Dustin Krcatovich for Riot Fest’s website</a>. “Those were bands that had never gotten any further from the garage, but Danny was playing actual gigs.”</p>
<p>When Danny Kroha was twelve years old, he had a clock radio on which he’d hear gospel and sixties rock music. He remembers loving bands like the Yardbirds, the Animals, and of course the Kinks and the Rolling Stones. Once Kroha found out who actually wrote a lot of those songs the “British Invasion” bands were playing—Bo Diddley, Howlin’ Wolf, and Wolf’s longtime musical rival Muddy Waters—he essentially learned that the British Invasion was simply English bands who stylized American music in their own image and sold it back to Americans. He dug his hands into the roots of the rock music he loved; he traced the genre back to its source.</p>
<p>During <a href="https://youtu.be/F_M-sDhlliI?si=q0FH88e-znkMzCTr">an hourlong episode on a short-lived YouTube docuseries titled </a><em><a href="https://youtu.be/F_M-sDhlliI?si=q0FH88e-znkMzCTr">Detroit Punks</a></em>, Peggy O’Neill said she first saw Kroha when she was a senior in high school. Each member of the Gories said sixties counterculture was what was cool in the eighties, and they all were deep into mod culture at the time. O’Neill discovered the Who and found a book about the scene, and then one day saw Kroha cruising down the street on a Vespa scooter and wearing a parka.</p>
<p>Some time later, Kroha noticed O’Neill at mod parties in the Detroit area—Collins spun records at some of these parties, and was introduced to Kroha by a mutual friend—and one day worked up the nerve to approach her. It didn’t take long for the two to start dating.</p>
<p>Collins, Kroha, and O’Neill had two pastimes they enjoyed doing most, often both at once: drinking and listening to records. They’d frequently listen to garage comps and 45s described as “primitive,” but essentially were different versions of the same boring stuff, all bowl cuts and paisley shirts and Rickenbackers—which Collins has succinctly described more than once as bullshit.</p>
<p>A sixties garage comp called <em>Scum of the Earth</em> once came up on the turntable and Kroha and Collins had an epiphany: “We can play better than this and we can’t even play!,” said the latter on <em>Detroit Punks</em>. Then, the famed <em><a href="https://www.discogs.com/release/2570880-Various-Back-From-The-Grave-Volume-One">Back from the Grave</a></em> came out and they realized they could have been one of the bands on that full-length.</p>
<p>There they decided they were going to become the most primitive band to ever exist.</p>
<p>Primitivism helps when you deliberately choose an instrument you don’t know how to play. Collins previously played drums; he chose guitar for this new band. Kroha was the vocalist for a Detroit band called the Onset and played guitar poorly; he also chose guitar. Collins tried bass but switched to guitar because Kroha couldn’t play riffs and solos quite as well—he once joked that between the two of them, they had the skill set of one decent guitarist.</p>
<p>O’Neill says she was not interested in being a band, but Kroha had eventually convinced her after wearing her down for weeks. She ended up playing a kit that only had two working floor toms—no snare, no cymbals, and the kick drum was hollowed out and presumably only there for looks—that she bought from the drummer of the Onset for $50.</p>
<p>They named themselves the Gories after a deep-cut episode of <em>Gidget</em>, where Sally Field’s title character forms a surf rock band for her school’s talent show. Collins joked on <em>Detroit Punks</em> that there was another talent show band he wanted to call his group: the Horribles.</p>
<div class="rw-embed-wrapper"><embed src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/XBNNnyLK3iU" type="video/mp4"/></div>
<p>As bands do, the Gories played many shows locally in the Detroit area—every two weeks, Collins estimated—and was approached to put out their debut LP, <em>Houserockin’</em>. Though some view the album as undercooked and undernourishing rock music, the album carves an alternate timeline in rock ‘n roll where the original punks studied the originators of the genre and its immediate forebears in the Southern and Midwestern rhythm and blues scenes.</p>
<p>To wit: “Boogie Chillun” is an electric, groove-forward take on John Lee Hooker’s original that’s so addictive, it’s easy not to notice the entire song was built on a one-bar riff. The Gories honor Link Wray, the godfather of punk rock guitar, by playing his version of “Hidden Charms” rather than the one that made the song famous, written by Willie Dixon and performed by Howlin’ Wolf. (Though Wolf is easily my favorite bandleader from the golden era of blues, <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=erBWj29Weuo">Link Wray’s version of “Hidden Charms”</a> is one of the greatest songs ever made if you like blistering, nasty rock ‘n roll. Which I have to assume you do if you’re reading this feature.) Wolf does get his own tribute on <em>Houserockin’</em>, as the Gories play a rollicking version of “You’ll Be Mine,” and Collins approximates his signature vocals very nicely.</p>
<p>Maybe people would still be talking about the Gories if they were the punkest rhythm &amp; blues cover band in ‘80s Detroit, but we’ll never know because their <em>Houserockin’</em> originals are equally exceptional. “I Think I’ve Had It” is a soulful barnburner which serves as empirical proof that Black people were the inventors and should be considered the true arbiters of rock ‘n roll music. The song structure is perfect, and Collins hollers and wails like he’s been overserved moonshine at a Mississippi juke joint. “Sister Ann” is a magnificent tune about finding salvation in a <em>Weekly World News</em> ad.</p>
<p>“Sovereignty Flight” and opener “Feral” are vicious-sounding tunes that prove even though these songs were written in the late-’80s, that “punk” was most certainly a thing for many years before the Ramones played their first show in London. Both “I’ll Go” and the aforementioned “Feral” were heavily inspired by the Sonics; case in point, the original garage punk band.</p>
<p><em>Houserockin’</em> manages the rare feat of simultaneously honoring tradition and making an unruly fucking mess of it. With their first LP, the Gories inserted themselves into the pantheon of rock ‘n roll by offering a version of the style that was never diluted by commercial interests or professionalism.</p>
<div class="rw-embed-wrapper"><embed src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/wbk4YbllvXY" type="video/mp4"/></div>
<p>By their second album, the Gories traded a Detroit quonset hut with a dirt floor for the then-garage studio of renowned Memphis recording man Doug Easley. The awesomely-titled <em>I Know You Fine But How You Doin’</em> was rather famously produced by Alex Chilton, the big star of Big Star hearing the Gories through a mutual friend and instantly deciding that he wanted to cut an album with them. Chilton reportedly noted the Gories were doing for rhythm &amp; blues what the Cramps were doing for rockabilly, which is high praise indeed.</p>
<p>I’m reluctant to use the word “polished” to describe <em>I Know You Fine</em> as opposed to <em>Houserockin’</em>, because any manner of production would sound sleek next to the Gories’ debut full-length, which was obviously by some design. On <em>Detroit Punks</em>, Collins talks about the band moving the mics six feet away whenever the engineer wasn’t looking or inside the windowless control room. <em>I Know You Fine</em>…sounds like it was produced!</p>
<p>That’s not a slight whatsoever on Chilton; there are some interesting things going on here. The saxophone squeals and honks on “Chick-Inn,” the effects on the harmonica blowing through “Detroit Breakdown” makes the song sound almost psychedelic, same with the treated guitar work on “Six Cold Feet,” one of the few Gories ballads in existence.</p>
<p>Otherwise, the Gories ain’t fixing what ain’t broke—and if it <em>is</em> broke, it’s nothing a little duct tape won’t patch up. The B.B. King and Suicide covers are top notch (“Early in the Morning” and “Ghost Rider,” respectively), as is the band’s second John Lee Hooker cover in as many LPs, “Let Your Daddy Ride.” Two of the band’s best-loved songs are here, including “Nitroglycerine,” garage punk’s greatest ode to toxic romance.</p>
<p>And then there’s “Thunderbird ESQ,” forever memorializing the gas station staple flavored wine, serving as the alcoholic’s choice for a convenience store run along with Night Train and MD 20/20. As the Gories were enthusiastic, prolific drinkers, it may have been the call of destiny to write a crowd-pleasing classic about getting shitfaced on the cheapest, strongest stuff you can find.</p>
<p>The secret to the art of most great bands is simply that the pieces just fit. Collins was endowed with the voice of a bluesman; Kroha has a sneer right in step with those <em>Back from the Grave</em> bands the Gories felt a kinship with. I already wrote about how their combined talents equated to one decent guitarist. Collins and Kroha have both praised O’Neill by saying she was “just good enough”—she had great rhythm and could keep time without incident, but she wasn’t <em>so</em> good that her musicianship outshined or embarrassed her bandmates. Collectively, the Gories were the perfect storm, uniquely suited for their stripped-down, punked up, 100 proof-poisoned flavor of rhythm &amp; blues.</p>
<p><em>I Know You Fine But How You Doin’</em> ends with yet another signature Collins tune, “View from Here,” a lyric-driven banger with a rumbling tempo (and drums) courtesy of O’Neill. Its second verse in particular is a marvel—as well as evidence to why it’s so baffling that more people don’t praise Collins as a songwriter and lyricist. The verse opens with, “I go to heaven but it might be closed / If I get to heaven, I’ll probably be all alone / There might be nobody home.”</p>
<div class="rw-embed-wrapper"><embed src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/oCme0No9aVs" type="video/mp4"/></div>
<p>Collins says the Gories have broken up after every LP release. He says that after the release of <em>I Know You Fine But How You Doin’</em>, the band broke up without remembering they promised singles to a few different labels: Estrus, In the Red, and Sub Pop, to name a few. The latter, for the Seattle label’s cult classic singles club, they recorded a cover of Spinal Tap’s “Give Me Some Money” on the A-side, which fits the band’s sense of humor perfectly (“Your face is okay / But your purse is too tight”).</p>
<p>But then the band was approached by Crypt Records, who offered them a European tour if they cut one more LP. The members of the Gories, Collins especially, weren’t going to fall for it. Then Crypt came through with the plane tickets, the Gories rode on trains and vans all around Europe, played to rooms of about 50 people, and came home and recorded their final LP, <em>Outta Here</em>, on a four-track.</p>
<div class="rw-embed-wrapper"><embed src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/h070oDTRQ8U" type="video/mp4"/></div>
<p>A handful of those aforementioned singles made <em>Outta Here</em>: “Telepathic” from <em>Here Be the Gories</em> (In the Red, 1991); “Idol With the Golden Head,” the B-side of their 1992 Estrus single; the instrumental “Ichiban,” the B-side of their awesome single “To Find Out” (Giant Claw, 1992). The first is a booming strut, a Kroha-penned banger about wishing his lady was a mindreader, and the second sounds like Link Wray if he were too stoned to be so intense.</p>
<p>“Idol With the Golden Head” is the best of the three; a call-and-response stunner with typical Gories minimalism, originally performed by vocal-driven, Los Angeles R&amp;B group the Coasters. This feels like a good place to emphasize the mod roots of the band. Before it was associated with polyester outfits and expensive haircuts, the mod scene (short for “modernist”) was essentially made up of British kids who were obsessed with Black American music—primarily jazz 45s at first, and then rhythm &amp; blues.</p>
<p>Kroha identified the period of mod culture the Gories patterned themselves after to <a href="https://narcmagazine.com/interview-the-gories/">Lee Fisher for </a><em><a href="https://narcmagazine.com/interview-the-gories/">Narc Magazine</a></em>: “Mod means a lot of different things to a lot of different people. To us, it meant the original mod sound of R&amp;B records from the years 1960-’64. We were into the idea of mod music as exemplified by the DJ Guy Stevens, who did those Sue Story comps in the early sixties.” He goes on to cite freakbeat, raw blues from from Chess and Sun Records, and early John Lee Hooker and raw Detroit blues as examples.</p>
<p>“I think that’s an aspect of mod that has been forgotten or neglected,” Kroha continues. “Unfortunately, the mod sound is now associated with a bunch of crappy eighties power pop bands. Not trying to insult the Jam here, but sub-Jam stuff. Out of the music I listened to in my twenties, I don’t listen to the Jam anymore, but I still love and listen regularly to early James Brown, Ike and Tina [Turner], Chuck Berry, Bo Diddley, and all kinds of fifties and sixties R&amp;B.”</p>
<p>As a songwriter, Kroha was the perfect foil for Collins, not only adding that <em>Back from the Grave</em> sneer I mentioned earlier, but also adding a huge dollop of extra punk rock flavor to his bandmate’s out-of-time bluesman aura. One of the handful of originals on <em>Outta Here</em> is the Kroha-led “Rat’s Nest,” a punk garage rock tune about having to clean up the alley behind his literal garage and fantasizing about catching up with the juvenile delinquents causing the unholy disarray. The song pairs nicely with the blaring, Collins-penned “48 Hours.”</p>
<div class="rw-embed-wrapper"><embed src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/0Z7H0tFtENY" type="video/mp4"/></div>
<p>Covers have been part and parcel to the rhythm &amp; blues tradition since before rhythm sections were added in the first place. R&amp;B bandleaders and groups routinely played other people’s songs live and on recordings. Covers—or “versions,” depending if you want to consider revised lyrics or how old-school you want to get—have been part of the blues tradition for over a century, and prior to longplayers being the standard musical form (not to mention royalties and copyrights and the importance of artists selling original music), there were often just as many covers as original songs on albums (sometimes more).</p>
<p>Before they opted to assign themselves the publishing rights and outright steal songs written by Willie Dixon and others, the Rolling Stones’ debut only had one song written by the famous Jagger-Richards partnership. Nearly half of the songs on the Beatles’ proper debut <em>Please Please Me</em> were written by someone other than Lennon and/or McCartney. And those are merely the two most famous examples of this particular trend.</p>
<p>Similarly to the Beatles’ first album, the Gories’ last album spotlights six covers out of the LP’s 14 tracks. (A note: The original LP features a cover of “Hey Bo Diddley!” and a version of a now-obscure 45 by the Jesters of Newport, “Stormy,” which were replaced on the <em>Outta Here</em> reissues by “Idol With the Golden Head” and “Ichiban.”)</p>
<p>Given the exceptional tastes of the Gories, the covers here are absolutely delectable. Their version of Earl King’s “Trick Bag” is so good, <a href="https://trickbag.substack.com/p/a-trick-bag-manifesto">this very newsletter was named after it</a>. Their take on Bo Diddley’s “Crawdad” is instantly recognizable to a certain stripe of garage rock fan. Between “Boogie Chillun”—one of the tippy-top highlights of <em>Houserockin’</em>—and <em>Outta Here</em>’s Side 2 kickoff, “I Got Eyes For You,” John Lee Hooker fits the Gories like the proverbial hand in glove. There must be something in the Detroit River that’s not getting filtered out.</p>
<p>Unsurprisingly, the originals on <em>Outta Here</em> are among the very best songs the Gories have written; after years of refining their style and practicing their craft by playing shows to the same Detroit showgoers, the band had found their sound and could play off the cuff with ease, making it feel like playing in a punk band is the most natural thing in the world.</p>
<p>Opener “He’s Doin’ It” is arguably Kroha’s finest moment in front of the Gories; the song has a wild urgency that suits his wail perfectly. The album’s title track comes very close to that honorific, as it is the platonic ideal of Kroha’s snotty style paired with an irresistible boogie.</p>
<p>Collins-led instrumental “Omologato,” speaking of boogie, is magnificent. “Can’t Catch Up With You” not only has an outstanding rolling pace, but a great lyrical narrative, too; the narrator runs all over the city—doesn’t matter which, they all have jails and liquor stores—to find whoever he’s looking for.</p>
<p>Both Kroha and Collins have mentioned in interviews that the band listened to the Sonics for inspiration before recording <em>Outta Here</em>, and “Drowning” stands out as one of the best Gories songs ever particularly because it sounds like the greatest Sonics song the eternally influential Tacoma band never recorded. Both the Sonics and Gories drew from a deep well of rhythm &amp; blues and used blaring, overdriven guitars and primitive drums to blast their inspiration into the world. “Drowning” contains a flawless beat from O’Neill, awesome backing vocals from Kroha, and an insistent, infectious shout from Collins—who caps one of his all-time best performances with a pitch-perfect Gerry Rosalie howl.</p>
<p>It’s an ideal tribute from one of the greatest garage punk bands ever to the band that practically invented the entire subgenre.</p>

<p>The period after <em>Outta Here</em> and the European tour they were promised was the final breakup of the band for many years. Sometimes people who spend so much time together grow to dislike each other. Collins and Kroha knew that if they wanted to remain friends at all, they’d have to break up the band. Meanwhile, Collins and O’Neill didn’t speak for nearly two decades.</p>
<p>But the band reunited in 2009 for a European tour with their generation-defining peers the Oblivians and toured North America a year later. (Word on the street was they were asked by Coachella to reunite at the 2008 festival, but they couldn’t get their shit together in time.) Since then, they have played sporadic live shows to enthusiastic crowds and lifelong fans—including yours truly when they played beloved Memphis festival Gonerfest in 2023.</p>
<div class="rw-embed-wrapper"><embed src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/Mjjgik4PD4w" type="video/mp4"/></div>
<p>In 2013, in a move of crucial history preservation, Third Man Records—the label owned in part by Jack White, perhaps the world’s most famous Gories fan—released the Gories’ only sanctioned live record, <em>The Shaw Tapes: Live in Detroit 5/27/88</em>. At times, live albums are at best a curio for completists and at worst a shameless cash grab, but quite a few live album become essential documents in showcasing a band’s sound, and even their ethos. Recorded at a storefront in nearby Hamtramck, Michigan, <em>The Shaw Tapes</em> captured the Gories at their ascent; they had been playing together for nearly 2 ½ years but had just begun recording <em>Houserockin’</em>.</p>
<p>If you’re looking for a high-quality soundboard recording here…you probably shouldn’t have started reading an essay on the Gories to begin with. It sounds like it was recorded onto a cassette deck with <em>maybe</em> a boom mic attached. This live document features Collins, Kroha, and O’Neill in their natural element: an enthusiastic but sparsely attended room, a blown-out fuse upon launching into their cover of Iggy Pop’s “Again and Again” mid-set (they also played a faithful [read: incendiary] cover of the Stooges “Real Cool Time”), Collins shouting out a couple friends from the stage. The fidelity is low but the vibes are immaculate.</p>
<p>The band plays “Thunderbird ESQ,” which wasn’t released until 1990, but it’s telling as a live favorite because if you listen closely, you can hear one or two people singing along. They play a sublimely sloshed version of “I Think I Had It,” a truly wild rendition of “Give Me Love,” and a thunderous take on “Sovereignty Flight.” I remember playing a friend their cover of Bo Diddley’s “I Just Want to Make Love to You” and the guitars were so noisy and out of tune that my friend thought the Gories were a no wave band.</p>
<p>Maybe the no wave approach is a good way to view the Gories. They were as much rhythm &amp; blues deconstructionists as they were faithful admirers of the genre. Collins told Detroit Punks that the band was so noisy and so primitive, people felt they somehow went all the way around the “shitty garage band” tag and landed in an avant-garde space.</p>
<p>Perhaps unwittingly, the Gories were avant rock trailblazers, but they never played their game that way. They punked up rhythm &amp; blues music like precious few bands before or since, played to practically nobody for years, and then after they broke up, amassed a legion of admirers, fans, musicians, and scholars who felt this is what rock ‘n roll should’ve sounded like all along.</p>
<p>And I proudly place myself in that group of people.</p>
<h3>Only one band could have been enshrined as the first of this newsletter’s canonical artists. Here be the Gories.</h3>
