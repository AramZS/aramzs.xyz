  Palestinian Families Trapped by Settlers as Israeli Army Shows Up, and Then
  Leaves

<p>Two Palestinian families have been trapped since Sunday inside their homes on a rocky West Bank hillside by a group of violent Israeli settlers, who set up a tent in the front yard of one of the houses. Residents say about 10 to 15 settlers have been attacking them around the clock, throwing stones and an ax.</p><p>Early Wednesday morning, after news of the siege began to draw news attention, the Israeli army’s commander for the West Bank, Maj. Gen. Avi Bluth, showed up in the village of Qusra and listened as one of the families described how the settlers had been terrorizing them since January. Outside, his soldiers tried to disperse the settlers with tear gas.</p><p>Yet, by 10:20 a.m., videos from security cameras show, the soldiers had left. Soon after, residents said, the settlers resumed their attacks.</p><p>“We had some hope in the morning when we saw that the army was present,” Aisha Hassan, 35, who was in the meeting with General Bluth, said by telephone, even as she said one of the settlers was throwing stones at her. “But when we saw that the army could not stop the settlers — now, honestly, we are more afraid.”</p><p>On Wednesday evening, the army said in response that it was assigning an extra battalion to the West Bank to try to “strengthen order and operational control in the area.”</p><p>The army’s initial retreat and broader struggle to contend with settler violence cast a spotlight on the increasingly chaotic situation in the West Bank, where hundreds of Israeli settlers <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/05/04/world/middleeast/israel-palestinians-west-bank-settler-violence-iran-war.html">have been terrorizing Palestinians</a> with near-impunity all year.</p><p>As of late July, 18 Palestinians have been killed by settlers, one more than in all of 2025, according to U.N. data. Settlers have been boasting on social media of their attacks, posting videos showing mosques that have been torched in Palestinian towns.</p><figure><img alt="A dozen men are seen on and alongside an unpaved road covered in boulders." src="https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/08/12/multimedia/12int-westbank-2-ptql/12int-westbank-2-ptql-articleLarge.jpg"/><figcaption>CCTV footage showing Israeli settlers in Qusra on Wednesday.</figcaption></figure><p>At times, soldiers have taken part in the violence, Palestinian and Israeli officials say, reflecting disciplinary problems that <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/03/30/world/middleeast/israel-west-bank-battalion.html">the military</a> has acknowledged are an issue.</p><p>“It’s a great fiasco,” said Gadi Shamni, a retired two-star general and former head of Israel’s Central Command, whose area of responsibility included the occupied territory. “There’s a loss of control over the West Bank.”</p><p>Israel’s right-wing, pro-settler government says that concerns about settler violence is overblown, and officials have repeatedly signaled their support for Israelis accused of attacks. The defense minister, Israel Katz, boasts of having stripped the army of the authority to issue detention orders for settlers — which had been a key tool for restraining Israeli extremist violence. In July, he visited in prison a settler arrested as a key suspect in the <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/03/18/world/middleeast/west-bank-sexual-assault-israel-settlers.html">sexual assault of a Palestinian man in March</a> and clashed with General Bluth over the suspect’s detention.</p><p>In Qusra, located near the city of Nablus in the northern West Bank, the violence has afflicted two families in particular: the Hassans and their neighbors the Abu Ridis.</p><p>Louai Abu Ridi, 46, a native of Qusra who immigrated to Ohio in 2003 and is a U.S. citizen in the gas-station business, said he had poured about $1 million into building a house in his birthplace. The settler attacks, beginning in January, started to lay waste to his investment, he said. His brother moved into the house to try to protect it.</p><p>Mr. Abu Ridi said he had appealed to the U.S. Embassy in Jerusalem for help and was told to report the attacks to the Israeli authorities. An embassy spokesman did not immediately respond to a request for comment on Wednesday.</p><figure><img alt="A woman is sitting inside a home. Her young daughter stands beside her." src="https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/08/12/multimedia/12int-westbank-4-lkzj/12int-westbank-4-lkzj-articleLarge.jpg"/><figcaption>Ruqaya Hassan, 28, with her daughter Kinda, 4, at her father-in-law’s house on Wednesday after Israeli settlers besieged her home in Qusra. Ismael Khader/Reuters</figcaption></figure><p>Ms. Hassan, whose brothers Yusuf and Rizq built two other homes on land owned by their father, said that settlers had broken windows and doors, cut utility lines, destroyed solar panels and stolen the birthday gifts a 2-year-old had received days earlier.</p><p>Residents said the settlers appeared to be based in an unfinished home outside Qusra. In March, witnesses said, settlers based in that same home <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/05/04/world/middleeast/israel-palestinians-west-bank-settler-violence-iran-war.html">killed another Qusra man, Amir Odeh</a>, and beat and stabbed his father.</p><p>Sometimes the settlers have tried to intimidate the families, grazing sheep on their land and destroying their plantings. But the violence has escalated, Ms. Hassan said. Settlers once tried to push the family’s cooking-gas tank into the house through an open window, she said. They’ve fired gunshots and thrown Molotov cocktails at the house, she added.</p><p>Efforts by New York Times reporters on Wednesday to reach the scene and speak with the settlers and residents were rendered fruitless by roadblocks guarded by soldiers and settlers.</p><p>On Sunday, after the settlers set up camp just outside the Hassans’ homes and erected a tent, the family called the police. When an Israeli soldier showed up, Ms. Hassan said, he ignored the family and instead greeted the settlers warmly. Video recordings show him joining the settlers in prayers.</p><p>On Monday night, when Ms. Hassan’s 2-year-old niece, Hoor, spiked a fever and the family called an ambulance, she said, settlers blocked the ambulance and, the driver said, tried to ram it with an all-terrain vehicle. Late Tuesday afternoon, other soldiers made it possible for another ambulance to take the girl to a local clinic, Ms. Hassan recounted.</p><figure><video controls="" poster="https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/08/12/12int-westbank-video-28814-cover/12int-westbank-video-28814-cover-articleLarge.jpg"><source src="https://vp.nyt.com/video/2026/08/12/176265_1_12int-westbank-video-28814_wg_1080p.mp4" type="video/mp4"/></video></figure><p>The military issued a statement on Tuesday acknowledging reports of Israelis seizing Palestinian homes and land in Qusra and expressing “condemnation” for what it called “illegal, reprehensible and unacceptable” actions. On Wednesday, it said that disciplinary action would be taken against the “security personnel involved.”</p><p>General Shamni, the retired Central Command chief, said that what had allowed him to keep the Israeli-occupied West Bank relatively calm during his tenure, from 2007 to 2009 — close cooperation with the Israeli police, the Internal Security Agency and the military’s Civil Administration — could no longer be counted on. Pro-settler extremists, like the cabinet ministers Bezalel Smotrich and Itamar Ben-Gvir, wield too much influence over all those agencies, General Shamni said.</p><p>Moreover, he added, the military units serving in the West Bank are largely composed of soldiers who grew up in the settlements. “They are the settlers,” he said. “That’s the problem.”</p><p>General Shamni said he wished that President Trump would force Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, who has minimized settler violence, to crack down on it.</p><p>General Bluth’s visit to Qusra on Wednesday appeared to signal a new resolve to address the situation, but it evidently alarmed the settlers. The young men who had been attacking the Qusra homes were quickly joined by scores more, who could be seen throwing stones onto the road to make it impassable and then scuffling with soldiers, according to witnesses and video viewed by a reporter.</p><p>Soldiers confiscated a tarp and an all-terrain vehicle but ultimately drove off, leaving the settlers behind.</p><p>They were still there on Wednesday night.</p><figure><img alt="Two men in dark clothes look out over a terraced hillside in the distance.
