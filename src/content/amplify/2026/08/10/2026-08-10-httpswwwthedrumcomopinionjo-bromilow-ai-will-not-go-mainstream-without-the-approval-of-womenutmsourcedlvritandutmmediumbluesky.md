<p>Recently, OpenAI invited a group of ‘lifestyle tech’ influencers to <a href="https://www.independent.co.uk/life-style/chatgpt-open-ai-brand-trip-backlash-new-york-b3028101.html">what could best be described as a wilderness retreat</a> – call it DataCenter Parcs – where creators reconnected with the natural world through sound baths, meditation, foraging and candle-making, the sort of event Dove might run in the next mountain lodge to promote a new moisturizer. It’s a standard piece of brand marketing right out of the growth playbook, where influencers can meet, mingle and authentically connect with the product against a beautiful aesthetic background.</p>
<p>The fascinating thing is that it’s being done by this company, at this point, when we’re barely weeks out from Meta launching its latest AI glasses with Kylie Jenner front and center. Yasss kween.</p>
<figure><img src="https://thedrum-media.imgix.net/thedrum-user-assets-prod/s3/images/original/images-1786363143.jpg?width=1020&amp;fit=crop&amp;crop=faces,edges&amp;auto=format&amp;q=100&amp;dpr=2"/></figure>
<p>The same technology being celebrated in some corners of the internet as a way to make women redundant is now being packaged for women through wellness retreats, lifestyle creators and female entrepreneurship. That contradiction is the interesting bit.</p>

<p>The AI marketing machine has kicked into a new gear, making the tech something other than tech. Tech brands, and the posturing around them, sell to men. Lifestyle brands, and the softer, more soulful side of what they can facilitate, sell to women.</p>
<p>And women remain major commercial drivers around the world, controlling and influencing huge amounts of household and consumer spending despite all the best efforts of politicians and tradwife influencers to chip away at that. They are also, in many markets, more skeptical about AI than men. That makes them commercially important and, crucially, harder to convince.</p>
<p>AI, like any product, has exhausted its disruptor, challenger-brand image and energy. It now needs to establish itself as a core part of family, or daily, life. The next challenge is familiarity: getting people to use it routinely, trust it enough to keep using it and see it as something practical rather than a shiny toy for tech bros. And that matters increasingly as <a href="https://www.thedrum.com/opinion/andrew-tindall-the-all-you-can-eat-ai-buffet-has-closed">the economics behind limitless AI use start to collide with a much harder commercial reality</a>.</p>
<p>And its founder, Sam Altman, posting about using AI to create a podcast about his kids’ hobbies so he can understand them, rather than talking to them, isn’t going to cut it. It shows a class of men creating solutions to problems that exist only for them and no one else.</p>
<p>Taking a glance through the creators who went on the brand trip (not all of whom were women) is interesting too. Just as actress-turned-entrepreneur Reese Witherspoon was touting the need for women <a href="https://www.thedrum.com/opinion/reese-witherspoon-is-right-women-need-to-start-leading-on-ai">not to get ‘left behind’ by AI</a>, a lot of the attendees are entrepreneurial LinkedIn types (hey, where was MY invite) whose personal brands speak of savvy girl bosses forging paths in a male-dominated landscape.</p>
<p>They’re founders, they’re entrepreneurs, they’re in tech. They’re beating the boys at their own game, girls! They’re leaning in!</p>
<p>Which makes them extremely useful people to sell AI to women.</p>
<p>OpenAI needs people who can translate AI into a version of everyday life that their audiences already recognize. A sound bath and a nice cabin do something that another keynote about model capabilities cannot: they strip away some of the machinery and make the product look normal.</p>
<p>Or, realistically, the creators are either turning off comments or using the engagement to rage-bait their way to virality. You know, like all the creators who went on the Tarte brand trip that gained notoriety over criticism of its racial diversity. So far, so boilerplate.</p>
<p>The creators who were on this brand trip are largely back to normal posting schedules and their comment sections, where active, are slowly returning to normal. The people furious in those comment sections were never the target customer, and the ride-or-dies who love everything the influencers do will post positive comments that will look good on PCA reports.</p>
<p>The backlash does not necessarily mean the strategy failed. Influencer marketing works over a longer tail than one angry comments section. People absorb associations. They remember what their favorite creators use, recommend and normalize. So when they are next planning a holiday, organizing school admin, searching for dinner ideas or trying to make sense of a work task, ChatGPT may already feel like one of the obvious tools to reach for.</p>
<p>And that is what ChatGPT is investing in: normality.</p>
<p>We are in the same hype cycle we see with any new tech innovation, but developers get a little smarter each time. The cognitive dissonance between individuals crowing about social media bans and feeding photos of their children into AI image generators is real.</p>
<p>It is the job of these influencer brand trips to sell the lifestyle that reliance on ChatGPT and its ilk can facilitate and to normalize them as essential, simple and practical tools in the arsenal of any working woman.</p>
<p>Am I making sweeping, gendered generalizations partly to make my point? Sure, but considering we work in an industry where every creative brief still uses ‘Gen Z’ as a catch-all phrase for cool, I’ll sit back in my glass house and watch it shatter with stones thrown from yours.</p>
<p>In the same way we need ‘Gen Z’ to make a brand cool and relevant right now, AI companies need cultural permission to make their products ordinary. That feels particularly important when the industry itself is openly debating <a href="https://www.thedrum.com/opinion/rory-mcentee-i-was-wrong-to-say-ai-can-replace-some-agencies-i-meant-most-agencies">whether AI can already replace huge chunks of agency work</a>. Women are central to that job, both as consumers and as the creators whose recommendations help products cross into everyday life.</p>
<p>We saw it with Facebook, which built itself deep into the routines, interests and social lives of huge numbers of women. We’ve spent years collectively noting the irony of a field largely driven by women’s activities, hobbies and pastimes that is still dominated by men at founder and executive level.</p>
<p>So, for any future innovation that comes out of the techbrosphere (hey, at least they’ve taken a break from inventing buses) that incels crow will bring about the end of women, remember this moment.</p>
<p>They can fantasize about AI making women irrelevant all they like. The companies commercializing that future seem considerably less convinced they can make it mainstream without us.</p>
