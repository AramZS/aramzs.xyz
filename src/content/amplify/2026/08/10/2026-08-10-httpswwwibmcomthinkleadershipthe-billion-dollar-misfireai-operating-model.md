<ul><li>Companies are investing heavily in AI, but few are seeing measurable value because most efforts aren’t tightly tied to strategy or scaled across the business.</li><li>Organizations that succeed treat AI as a business transformation, aligning executives and assigning clear ownership of end-to-end workflows while holding teams accountable for results.</li><li>High-performing companies are systematically integrating AI into workflows across the enterprise while upskilling employees and measuring impact.</li><li>CEOs are done with AI pilots and now expect measurable value, cost discipline, and risk control. Growth is driven by new products, better service, and faster execution.</li><li>Competitive advantage comes from what organizations build—not what they buy—as leaders combine models, data, and infrastructure into systems that competitors can’t replicate off the shelf.</li></ul>
<p>However, only a small group is seeing meaningful return on investment.</p>
<p>Global corporate AI investment totaled almost USD 582 billion last year, up nearly 130% from 2024, a recent Stanford <a href="https://hai.stanford.edu/assets/files/ai_index_report_2026.pdf">report</a> found. What’s more, in a recent global McKinsey <a href="https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/quantumblack/our-insights/the-state-of-ai#/">survey</a>, almost 90% of respondents at all levels of an enterprise indicated that their organizations use AI regularly. And yet a global IBM <a href="https://newsroom.ibm.com/2025-05-06-ibm-study-ceos-double-down-on-ai-while-navigating-enterprise-hurdles">survey</a> of chief executive officers found that only about 25% of AI initiatives have delivered expected return on investment in recent years. Just 16% have scaled enterprise-wide.</p>
<p>Indeed, independent studies from BCG, McKinsey and MIT <a href="https://www.ibm.com/think/leadership/the-billion-dollar-misfire/c-suite-gap">found</a> that, at most, 10% of organizations are seeing measurable returns from AI at scale.</p>
<p>It’s no surprise, then, that signs of the AI divide on corporate America’s earnings sheets are unmistakable. While S&amp;P 500 operating margins have grown from 14% to 17% in 5 years, Morgan Stanley research found AI adopters are seeing twice the margin expansion of other companies.</p>
<p>The issue isn’t the technology. A recent <a href="https://www.jonathantrevor.com/ai-everywhere">article</a> by Jonathan Trevor, Professor and Associate Dean at the University of Oxford’s Saïd Business School, found many organizations are still determining where AI fits in the logic of their enterprise. Survey results spanned from the mailroom to the corner office. “Having AI is not enough,” Trevor said in an interview. “It’s how you use it, in a very specific way.”</p>
<p>Often, what is missing is a clear link to corporate direction. “Many organizations aren’t explicitly tying their major investments to strategy,” said Jeremy Korst, Founder and Chief Executive of AI consultancy Mindspan Labs. Korst goes on to say, “They’re focused on what they could do, versus what they should do. If not tied explicitly to strategy, it’s likely a failed effort.”</p>
<p>An ample body of research confirms that strategic alignment benefits the bottom line. “There is a strong correlation between the degree of strategic alignment and the degree of firm financial performance,” Trevor said. “It’s not enough to think about AI in isolation. You have to also seek to align it with other resources.”</p>
<p><a href="https://mindspanlabs.ai/whitepaper">Research</a> by Korst’s Mindspan, in conjunction with the Wharton School and GBK Collective, a marketing consulting and analytics firm, found organizations that break through with AI treat it as a people problem first and a tech problem second. “We’ve got to get the organization on the same page, particularly executive management and management teams,” said Korst, who also lectures at the Wharton School and Columbia Business School. Korst added, “This is a people and leadership challenge as much as it is an architecture [problem]. Strategic leaders should be thinking about understanding the capacity of the organization for this change and preparing and equipping them for it.”</p>
<p>Of course, aligning a complex, cross-functional organization on strategy is easier said than done. A recent Harvard Business Review article by Trevor found that while 91% of management-level respondents agree that strategic alignment is important for performance, only 14% strongly agree that their own companies are aligned.</p>
<p>The research by Trevor recommends that business start by considering where the technology sits in the company’s value chain and ensure an organization’s AI investments are aligned with its purpose, strategy and capabilities. At the same time, integrate AI investments with a wider system of people, workflows and technologies.</p>
<p>“We need to have a very clear, intentional and effective interface between human and machine resources in order to function well,” Trevor said. “We need to make sure if we’re using agentic AI that we’re setting it up in such a way that it’s appropriately aligned to the organizational structure, so that you can have productive workflows that don’t cancel each other out.”</p>
<p>Research indicated the high-performing organizations that use AI are succeeding not by adopting more technology, but by reshaping how their business operates. A recent <a href="https://roaiinstitute.com/white-paper/economic-maturity-for-artificial-intelligence/">paper</a> from the Return on AI Institute (RoAI Institute), a research and advisory firm, found that the companies capturing the most economic value from AI tend to systematically measure, manage and report the impact of their AI initiatives.</p>
<p>Moving past experimentation into day-to-day business operations around AI appears to require a more deliberate, orchestrated approach. The paper’s co-author Tom Davenport, a distinguished professor of information technology and management at Babson College, said in an interview companies must embed the technology into their workflows. That method, he said, involves upskilling the employees and integrating AI into areas across the enterprise, from transactional systems to performance metrics.</p>
<p>Thanks to the great potential seen in AI, the opportunity has changed since the 1990s, when <a href="https://sloanreview.mit.edu/article/the-new-industrial-engineering-information-technology-and-business-process-redesign/">Davenport’s research</a> helped launch a movement toward business process reengineering. “Now AI is the driver and the enabler, and it really does make sense to think about how you might do it differently,” said Davenport, who is also co-author of the book <em>All in on AI: How Smart Companies Win Big with Artificial Intelligence</em>. “How you might do it differently ought to be driven in part by what AI is capable of.”</p>
<p>And while the vast majority of companies are organized by function, they should take care not to overlook their workflows. “People have argued for decades that if you’re gonna have these end-to-end processes established within your organization, you really do need process owners for them,” Davenport said.</p>

<p>As IBM’s Chief Commercial Officer, Rob Thomas said in a recent IBM <a href="https://www.ibm.com/think/videos/think-keynotes/powering-agentic-enterprise">Think keynote</a>, “Can you understand what’s happening? Can you automate your processes? Can you scale this across all of your business and can you secure how it’s governed?” The implication is that companies should ask themselves these questions and build a system that delivers the answers.</p>
<p>The IBM AI operating model consists of all four system categories, according to Thomas. “This is the definitive playbook for changing how your business operates with AI,” he said.</p>
<p>This approach is rooted in IBM’s client engagements and its own transformation—referred to internally as client zero—in redesigning business processes. The clients of IBM Consulting® have themselves been successfully undertaking the same lofty redesign challenge, according to Mohamad Ali, Senior Vice President and Head of IBM Consulting. “That is working at companies now and they’re getting productivity,” he said in a recent interview with media platform <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TqtVk7tMJA0">theCUBE</a>.</p>
<p>Create a clear view of what’s happening across the business and what becomes possible as a result so leaders can make informed decisions about what to do next.</p>
<p>Having the right playbook for AI transformation is becoming only more urgent. C-suite leaders face boardroom pressure to turn investments into measurable outcomes that demonstrate value, cost discipline and risk control. Top executives now cite productivity and priority as their #1 priority, according to a recent <a href="https://www.ibm.com/thought-leadership/institute-business-value/report/2026-ceo">IBM survey</a> of chief executives. Customer service was #2, up from #6 the previous year, while technology modernization rounded out the top 3.</p>
<p>“CEOs are done with AI that lives in a pilot,” Neil Dhar, Senior Vice President of the Americas for IBM Consulting, said in a <a href="https://www.ibm.com/think/videos/think-keynotes/architecting-ai-first-enterprise">keynote</a> address. “The mandate now is: Show me the results. But real results don’t come from efficiency gains alone. Growth comes from the new products you launch, the services you deliver with excellence and the moments you seize.”</p>
<p>To capture the value of AI, C-suite leaders are revamping workflows to reflect the research that suggests by 2030, almost half of operational decisions will be made by the technology, according to Dhar. People are shifting into orchestrator roles, where they’ll set the logic, define guardrails and apply judgment. The goal is the right balance of human and artificial intelligence, Dhar said.</p>
<p>Top executives are recognizing, too, that competitive advantage won’t come from picking the best AI model, according to Dhar. IBM’s <a href="https://www.ibm.com/thought-leadership/institute-business-value/c-suite-study/ceo">research</a> found half of chief executives are moving toward hybrid strategies that combine custom models, foundation models and smaller specialized models—“something no competitor can buy off the shelf,” Dhar said. “As AI becomes a commodity, your infrastructure becomes the differentiator.”</p>
<p>“The leaders pulling ahead have their sights set on something bigger, using AI to enter new markets, disrupt existing ones and reinvent what best in class looks like.”</p>
<p><strong>Neil Dhar, </strong>Senior Vice President of the Americas for IBM Consulting</p>
<p>That shift means intentionally creating an AI system where every capability helps the next one pull further ahead of the competition. <a href="https://newsroom.ibm.com/2026-01-19-ibm-study-ai-poised-to-drive-smarter-business-growth-through-2030#:~:text=The%20global%20study*%2C%20based%20on,gap%20between%20expectations%20and%20outcomes">IBM’s research</a> found that 67% of executives expect most AI-generated productivity gains to have been recorded by 2030. So high-performing companies aren’t stopping there. “The leaders pulling ahead have their sights set on something bigger, using AI to enter new markets, disrupt existing ones and reinvent what best in class looks like,” Dhar said.</p>
<p>Working with IBM Consulting, Providence Health used AI to reengineer a 170-year-old hospital system and help caregivers transfer to internal roles faster. AWS has been operating IBM’s AI agents at scale across complex technology architectures. Education company Pearson has worked with IBM to verify AI agents, leading to a credentialed hybrid workforce.</p>
<p>“The future of the AI-first enterprise is no longer theoretical,” Ali said in the keynote. “Three different organizations. Three different challenges. One common truth: AI is now an operating model—not an experiment.”</p>
