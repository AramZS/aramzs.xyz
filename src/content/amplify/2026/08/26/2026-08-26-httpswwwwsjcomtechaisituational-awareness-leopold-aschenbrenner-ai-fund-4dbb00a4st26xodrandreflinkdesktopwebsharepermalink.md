<h1>The Connections That Turned a Precocious Teen Into the Fallen ‘Nostradamus of AI’</h1>
<h2>A 24-year-old investor was hailed as a visionary and attracted billions—before turning into Wall Street’s latest cautionary tale</h2>
<p><img alt="Leopold Aschenbrenner." src="https://images.wsj.net/im-32132428/?width=1280&amp;height=1280"/></p>
<p>For the past few years, at happy hours and dinner parties in <a href="https://www.wsj.com/topics/place/san-francisco">San Francisco</a>, Leopold Aschenbrenner kept confusing people by sharing his favorite outlandish idea.</p>
<p>He wanted to buy galaxies.</p>
<p>Some of his friends weren’t always sure what to make of Aschenbrenner’s intergalactic ambitions. He once left the room and they debated whether he was referring to physical galaxies or a type of private plane.</p>
<p>No, he assured them when he came back. He meant galaxies.</p>
<p>His idea was that advances in artificial intelligence would soon unlock resources on a cosmic scale, enabling humans to colonize faraway planets. He planned to save money now so he could spend on galaxies, he told them, and make his mark across the universe.</p>
<p>To almost everyone in this world, outside the AI bubble of Silicon Valley, the idea of scooping up property rights outside of Earth might sound improbable. But in Aschenbrenner’s world, that sort of fervent belief was among the reasons the 24-year-old investor was hailed as a visionary.</p>
<p>Since graduating from Columbia University in 2021 as the class valedictorian at age 19, he has worked his way into the upper echelons of technology. He spent time working out of now-convicted crypto founder Sam Bankman-Fried’s compound in the Bahamas and then at <a href="https://www.wsj.com/topics/subject/openai">OpenAI</a> in San Francisco. Even as he drew attention as a troublemaker among some of his bosses, according to people who worked with him, he grew close to the industry’s top players.</p>
<p>Two years ago, he made bold predictions about the impending arrival of superintelligence, writing in a 165-page manifesto called “Situational Awareness” that AI would lead to one of the “most intense and volatile moments of human history.” He quickly turned that interest into money for a hedge fund that eventually managed $45 billion in assets, <a href="https://www.wsj.com/finance/investing/the-investing-heavyweights-that-backed-situational-awareness-before-it-blew-up-d73ee3b1?mod=article_inline">backed by investors</a> who believed in his prophecy—or his ability to profit from it.</p>
<p>He was barely of drinking age and had no prior investing experience, but his cozy relationships inside the industry fed into his reputation as an oracle of AI—someone who knew more about where the elusive technology was headed than legacy money managers living in New York. He mixed the personal and professional in a city where the two are rarely kept apart.</p>
<p>“There are perhaps a few hundred people, most of them in San Francisco and the AI labs, that have <em>situational awareness</em>,” he wrote in his viral essay. “Through whatever peculiar forces of fate, I have found myself amongst them.”</p>
<h3>When Aschenbrenner’s hedge fund unraveled</h3>
<p>Only a decade ago, Leopold Aschenbrenner was desperate to come to America.</p>
<p>Growing up in Berlin, he collected academic awards and distinguished himself as one of the country’s brightest young scientific minds. He was also politically engaged, speaking at the national convention of the Green Party as a teenager. But he felt out of place at home, attributing his desire to leave Germany to his country’s “tall-poppy” syndrome—the idea that successful people get criticized and cut down.</p>
<p>“They really, really, really hate anyone that deviates from the norm,” he said in a 2021 podcast.</p>
<p><img alt="Dwarkesh Patel and Leopold Aschenbrenner discussing on a podcast." src="https://images.wsj.net/im-84078171?width=700&amp;height=466"/></p>
<p>He started college at Columbia when he was only 15 years old. Professors remember him as one of the most brilliant undergraduates they ever met. Classmates remember him for getting into heated debates about ancient texts and the scientific method.</p>
<p>While he blogged about students wasting time watching <a href="https://www.wsj.com/market-data/quotes/NFLX">Netflix</a>, he found something else to binge that would end up being far more important to his career: effective altruism.</p>
<p>Aschenbrenner grew interested in the ideas behind the philosophical movement, which aims to apply data and reason to maximize good in the world. One focus is how to reduce longer-term risks that people typically ignore, including AI that could go rogue and declare war on humans.</p>
<p>After his sophomore year, Aschenbrenner went to Oxford for a summer internship at the Future of Humanity Institute, which was affiliated with EA. Aschenbrenner spent his days in a drab office building alongside theorists who worried about extinction issues, like the probability of asteroid strikes, and worked on a paper that examined whether economic growth increased the risk of global catastrophe.</p>
<p>Returning to campus that fall, Aschenbrenner passed out fliers for the Columbia Effective Altruism club at the school activities fair with his friend Collin Burns, whom he met when they were competitive Rubik’s Cubers. (Aschenbrenner says he is not an effective altruist.) They soon organized a club outing to watch Scottish philosopher Will MacAskill, whose ideas drove the EA movement, deliver a Saturday night lecture called “The Ethics of the Next Billion Years,” promising to order takeout with plenty of vegetarian and vegan options.</p>
<p><img alt="Philosopher Will MacAskill speaks at TED2018 in Vancouver, Canada" src="https://images.wsj.net/im-890487?width=1260&amp;height=839"/></p>
<p>That school year, Aschenbrenner watched in “utter despair” as the pandemic shut down campus.</p>
<p>“All I could do was buy masks,” he later wrote, “and short the market.”</p>
<p>When he graduated in 2021, he wasn’t sure what to do next.</p>
<p>After earning a nearly perfect LSAT score that he shared on his LinkedIn page, Aschenbrenner was accepted to Yale Law School and seriously considered the offer, aspiring to become a Supreme Court justice.</p>
<p>Instead of going to law school, he went in a radically different direction—to the Bay Area.</p>
<p>“To find the true American spirit,” he said on a podcast in 2024, “I had to come out here.”</p>
<p>Within a few months of graduating, Aschenbrenner was in a group chat with Sam Bankman-Fried to discuss contacts in the Bahamian government, as the founder of the cryptocurrency exchange FTX was headed to a bitcoin mining farm in Kazakhstan.</p>
<p>Aschenbrenner had joined the FTX Future Fund, a philanthropy backed primarily by Bankman-Fried. The fund had a lofty goal of giving away up to $1 billion in 2022 alone, doing “as much good as we can for the future of the world.” Among its top priorities was AI safety.</p>
<p>Future Fund was run by a small band of effective altruists that included MacAskill, the philosopher, and Avital Balwit, Aschenbrenner’s future wife. She had recently turned down a Rhodes scholarship and managed a failed House campaign for a Democratic candidate funded by Bankman-Fried.</p>
<p>Their work put Aschenbrenner in proximity to the crypto tycoon himself, then one of the world’s youngest billionaires.</p>
<p>Aschenbrenner stayed in the Bahamas with Balwit in the 600-acre luxury resort that FTX executives called home. In a sixth-floor penthouse, the Future Fund team met with Bankman-Fried and the confidantes who would later testify against him. The effective altruists spent hours debating how to be most effectively altruistic.</p>
<p>Aschenbrenner and Bankman-Fried were also in 17 group chats together on the encrypted messaging app Signal, according to documents from Bankman-Fried’s trial, including conversations named “EA Discussion” and “Anthropic Chat.”</p>
<p>In 2021, Bankman-Fried agreed to buy a roughly 8% stake in the AI startup for $500 million.</p>
<p><img alt="Sam Bankman-Fried, FTX founder and CEO." src="https://images.wsj.net/im-36184233?width=700&amp;height=1049"/></p>
<p>In November 2022, <a href="https://www.wsj.com/articles/sam-bankman-fried-ftx-alameda-bankruptcy-collapse-11668824201?mod=article_inline">FTX crumbled</a> into bankruptcy. Its $500 million stake in Anthropic was later sold for $1.3 billion to a group that included Jane Street, the quant trading firm that once employed Bankman-Fried and would later invest in Aschenbrenner’s hedge fund. With Anthropic racing toward the biggest IPO in history, that equity is now worth significantly more.</p>
<p>Aschenbrenner resigned with all of his Future Fund colleagues when FTX imploded. He said in the 2024 podcast that he and Bankman-Fried had already had a falling out by then due to a disagreement over biosecurity grants.</p>
<p>He didn’t have to wait long to find his next opportunity.</p>
<p>Aschenbrenner formulated the worldview behind his paper, called “Situational Awareness: The Decade Ahead,” while he was still at OpenAI. He spent time quizzing colleagues for insights into OpenAI’s future strategy, and some said he struck them as particularly interested in the company’s plans for securing computing power. The notes that he wrote up synthesizing his thoughts would form the basis of his viral manifesto.</p>
<p>He launched his fund with a nearly five-hour appearance on a hit podcast hosted by his friend Dwarkesh Patel. They grew close after Patel benefited from a small but pivotal grant from Future Fund early in his podcasting career. These days, they share office space.</p>
<p>Aschenbrenner brought on a veteran of the EA movement, former hedge-fund investor Carl Shulman, to run the fund with him. They eventually <a href="https://www.wsj.com/finance/stocks/the-24-year-old-ai-wiz-who-counts-jane-street-as-an-investor-1c30d751?mod=article_inline">won over Jane Street</a>, the EA-tinged trading firm that rarely allocates capital to outside managers. They also took investment from Silicon Valley heavyweights like Stripe co-founders Patrick and John Collison.</p>
<p>The fund loaded up on public companies across the AI supply chain—semiconductors, energy, software. It also invested in private companies. The crown jewel of the portfolio was a stake in Anthropic, where Aschenbrenner briefly considered trying to get a job after he left OpenAI.</p>
<p>Many of the companies Situational Awareness backed also did business with Anthropic and OpenAI. They included CoreWeave and Cerebras, as well as smaller data-center companies such as Fluidstack.</p>
<p>Aschenbrenner’s social circle came to include some of the company’s most influential figures, including its chief scientist Jared Kaplan, who mingled in the crowds at a July 4 party at his mansion. Another regular at his events was his friend Sholto Douglas, an AI researcher at Anthropic who recently predicted that Situational Awareness would be bigger than Citadel by the end of the decade.</p>
<p>Even some top employees from OpenAI were invited to his social gatherings.</p>
<p>After years of success that inspired one podcaster to nickname Aschenbrenner the “Nostradamus of AI,” Situational Awareness was undone by forces outside of his tightknit universe. He didn’t see them coming.</p>
<p>In July, the market was spooked by the release of cheap, powerful AI systems developed in China. With a heavily concentrated portfolio that was long on AI and highly leveraged, the brutal tech selloff left Aschenbrenner’s fund exposed.</p>
<p>When the AI stocks in Aschenbrenner’s book rallied, they helped make July <a href="https://www.wsj.com/finance/investing/citadels-purchase-of-situationals-stocks-at-discount-is-already-paying-off-62cef121?mod=article_inline">the best month in years</a> for Citadel’s flagship fund. Situational, by contrast, was down 67% through the 30th of that month. Jane Street lost about $15 billion, the <a href="https://www.wsj.com/finance/investing/jane-street-suffers-loss-of-about-15-billion-due-to-troubles-at-situation-awareness-9be40c51?mod=article_inline">firm’s worst monthly loss</a> ever.</p>
<p>By the time he signed the paperwork early on a Thursday morning, Aschenbrenner was already preparing for his wedding in Carmel that weekend.</p>
<p>As the big day approached, Aschenbrenner’s friends, former colleagues and fellow members of the Silicon Valley intelligentsia filtered into the wedding hotel. There were more than a dozen Anthropic employees on the guest list, as well as MacAskill and others from the FTX Future Fund, plus effective altruists and the billionaires who had put their money into Situational Awareness.</p>
<p>The wedding was officiated by Ross Douthat, the former New York Times columnist who recently joined “60 Minutes.” He had reached out to Aschenbrenner after reading his manifesto, and they met last year when his family was on a <a href="https://www.wsj.com/topics/place/california">California</a> road trip. Aschenbrenner and Balwit hosted them for dinner, Douthat said, soliciting marriage advice from him and his wife and shocking their children with predictions about AI.</p>
<p>When the couple asked him to officiate their wedding, he was surprised and sought the counsel of a priest friend, who told Douthat he should do it if he could be spiritually helpful.</p>
<p>“Hopefully I was in some small way,” he said, “even if the circumstances of the wedding ended up being very unusual.”</p>
<p>When it was time to toast the newlyweds, one speaker thanked Citadel CEO Ken Griffin for making it all possible.</p>
<p>Another guest gushed about something more romantic: Aschenbrenner’s promise to buy his wife a galaxy.</p>
