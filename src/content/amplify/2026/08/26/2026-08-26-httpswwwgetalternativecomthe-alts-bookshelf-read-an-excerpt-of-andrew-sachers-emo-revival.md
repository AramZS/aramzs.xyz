<p>We love emo. We love the writing of Andrew Sacher. We’re proud to bring you an exclusive excerpt, in fact, of Andrew’s upcoming <em>Emo Revival </em>book, which, true to its title, documents the emo revival, often in the voices of the musicians themselves. It’s a great read, one all you Alternative readers are sure to love, and we’re excited to preview a section of it below, in which Andrew recaps some of the ups and downs that led to the release of <a href="https://www.getalternative.com/it-holds-up-the-world-is-whenever-if-ever/"><em>Whenever, If Ever</em></a> (with assists from a few members of TWIABP). Give that a read below, and <a href="https://lnk.bio/emorevivalbook">preorder <em>Emo Revival </em></a>if you like what you see (you’ll like it).</p>
<p>Just days before The World Is a Beautiful Place &amp; I Am No Longer Afraid to Die were set to begin a spring 2013 tour at the Cambridge Elks Lodge in Cambridge, MA, their debut album <i>Whenever, If Ever</i> leaked. Leaks were par for the course in those days, but this one hit a little harder than usual. Topshelf Records had planned to gradually roll out the album with multiple pre-release singles in order to hopefully garner some press, and the leak happened so far ahead of the expected release date that the entire plan was botched. And the band really felt like the album needed all the help it could get; it had taken several more months to complete than they planned, they nearly broke up during the process, and when they previewed the new material on recent tours, the reactions were muted.</p>
<p>“People would just kinda stare at us,” remembers Chris Teti. “If we played something a little older, they would go off. We were like, ‘That’s not a good sign, no one gives a shit.’”</p>
<p>To their surprise, the Cambridge Elks Lodge show was packed. “The room itself was sweating condensation all the way down every wall,” remembers Topshelf’s Kevin Duquette. “There were like a million people in the basement just going nuts, people hanging off pipes off the ceiling, this whole room sort of push-pitting in unison as one large being, and everyone singing every word to this record that technically hadn’t come out yet.”</p>
<p>“I was like, <i>What happened?! </i>No one reacted when we played these songs for the past year,” says Chris. “I couldn’t move while I was playing because the floor was so wet. It was crazy.”</p>
<p>It was a milestone moment not just for the band but also for Topshelf; Kevin knew in that moment that something about this album was going to be different than anything they’d released prior. “I remember being like, this is some shit I’ve never experienced before. We’ve never done anything like this, we’ve never worked with a band like this before,” he says. “I’d seen The Wonder Years pop off, I’d seen Title Fight pop off, I watched the explosive growth of when Run For Cover put out that Citizen/Turnover 7”. We were finally having that as a label; that was our sort of celebratory, ‘we can do that too’ kind of moment.”</p>
<p>After TWIABP put out their 2011 split with Deer Leap and welcomed Chris into their lineup, hardly any time passed when the band wasn’t on the road, writing, or recording. But meanwhile, there were personal and interpersonal issues that were making it increasingly difficult for TWIABP to move forward.</p>
<p>“There were a lot of differences between people in the band at that period, [vocalist/keyboardist] Tom [Diaz] was going through a lot of personal struggles,” remembers Greg Horbal. “It was like, <i>Are we gonna break up?</i> There was just so much tension.”</p>
<p>“I just assumed the band was gonna implode,” says Chris.</p>
<p>Tom and guitarist/vocalist Nicole Shanholtzer had been the band’s core songwriters heading into the album process, but they were butting heads so much that multiple other members started getting more involved, purely out of necessity. Chris, who was sharpening his skills as a recording engineer and doing some work at the Burlington, CT studio Silver Bullet Studios, didn’t feel up for the task of recording the actual album, but he took the reins on all of the demoing for the album, and the process just kept getting longer and longer. Even after the band entered Format Audio in Haverhill, MA for a couple weeks in June of 2012 to properly record the album with engineer Ryan Stack, they headed back to Chris’s studio to keep making tweaks to the recordings and fix mistakes.</p>
<p>During a run of East Coast shows in October, TWIABP came to New York to play Topshelf’s 2012 CMJ party, and that’s when things reached a breaking point with Tom. “We played SUNY Purchase [the next night] and he did not play, he did not sing because he was just so out of it,” Greg says. “He struggled with a lot of mental health issues. It just got to the point where it was coming to the reality of, ‘He can’t keep doing this if we want to keep doing this. How are we gonna move forward?’”</p>
<p>They made it down to Florida for The Fest less than a week later, and friends of the band filled in to sing in Tom’s place. At that point, Chris says, “We were like, ‘Either the band’s gonna break up right now, or we’re gonna finish the record.’”</p>
<p>They chose to keep pushing forward, and they brought in two new members in Tom’s place—new lead vocalist David F. Bello and keyboard player Katie Dvorak—bringing TWIABP’s lineup to a total of seven people. “That’s when the band size started to get kinda out of hand,” Greg says.</p>
<p>They toyed with the idea of releasing what they’d already recorded as a statement of what the band had been, and they toyed with throwing it out entirely; instead, they decided to finish the album with Dave and Katie adding some of their own flavor. “We still left Tom over huge parts of the record because it was his DNA that made that,” Greg says, “but we were kind of thinking that moving forward we want to also show what we’re about to become.”</p>
<p>The long-in-the-works end results were clearly informed by both ‘90s Midwest emo and the late-2000s basement show network that TWIABP came up in, but none of their forebears or peers sounded like <i>Whenever, If Ever</i>. The way this record just barely came together, no other band <i>could</i> have sounded like it. There are post-rock climaxes, string and horn arrangements, bloopy synths, and bursts of screamo all worked into a version of emo that feels more pastoral than twinkly. There are casual tales of pissed-off landlords, and memories of endless tour routings, with Rival Schools and mewithoutYou blasting out of the car speakers. There are moments when the band is firing on all cylinders, and moments when they sound like they’re barely holding it together.</p>
<p>“It felt like you were on a rollercoaster and the thing was about to go off the rails at any given moment,” Chris says of the album process, “and I feel like the record feels like that. To me that record was almost about the band constantly imploding.”</p>
<p>Even after TWIABP finished recording the album, things took another turn for the worse when Topshelf got the first vinyl pressing of the album back and realized it was a mispress. “The Layers Of Skin We Drag Around,” which was supposed to be the last song on Side A, was instead the first song on Side B. “I remember being like, <i>This record is just going to kill us</i>,” says Chris.</p>
<p>They thought about delaying it further so they could fix the vinyl issue, but before they could take the steps to do that, the record leaked. Forced to act quick, they tossed up a full album download on Bandcamp and scrambled to get LP/CD pre-orders live. Two days later, when they were greeted in Cambridge by a crowd who had quickly devoured the new album, they couldn’t believe their eyes.</p>
<p>Those sweaty Elks Lodge walls could barely contain TWIABP that night, and it was indicative of something more; the entire community was on the cusp of boiling over into something bigger than it had ever been. The basements were becoming too small to contain all the energy that had been building up within this community for the past five-plus years. The word-of-mouth was getting too loud for it to remain a well-kept secret for much longer. A little more fuel on the fire was all it needed to really explode.</p>
