<p>OSLO, Aug 24 (Reuters) - Norway's government on Monday asked the ‌country's supreme court to overturn a lower-court ruling that invalidated permits for three oilfields, in a closely watched case that could reshape how the country approves new petroleum projects.</p>
<p>The case pits environmental groups against Norway's oil industry, provider of half the ​country's export revenue, as well as the government whose policy is to <a data-testid="Link" href="https://www.reuters.com/business/energy/norway-will-drill-arctic-regardless-eus-position-says-energy-minister-2026-08-24/">prolong the extraction</a> of ​crude and natural gas for decades to come.</p>
<p>Two lower courts have <a data-testid="Link" href="https://www.reuters.com/sustainability/climate-energy/norway-government-loses-appeal-case-over-oilfield-developments-2025-11-14/">ruled in favour</a> of ⁠Greenpeace and Young Friends of the Earth since the two groups first brought the case to ​trial in 2023, finding that Norway failed to properly assess the environmental impact from the three fields.</p>
<p>"If ​we win this round, it could change Norwegian oil politics forever," Haldis Tjeldflaat Helle, leader of Greenpeace Norway, told Reuters outside the court.</p>
<p>"It means that Norwegian politicians will have to actually assess how many lives will be lost, how ​many forest fires will be created and how much ice will melt due to Norwegian oil."</p>
<p>The case ​concerns Equinor's <a data-testid="Link" href="https://www.reuters.com/markets/companies/EQNR.OL">(EQNR.OL)</a> Breidablikk and Aker BP's <a data-testid="Link" href="https://www.reuters.com/markets/companies/AKRBP.OL">(AKRBP.OL)</a> Tyrving and Yggdrasil fields, of which the first two are already in production ‌while ⁠the third is due to come on stream next year.</p>
<p>The green campaigners argue that the government failed to consider the climate impact of the burning of the fields' oil and gas, known as Scope 3 emissions, and that efforts to rectify this at a later stage were invalid.</p>
<h2 data-testid="Heading">ENERGY SECURITY AT STAKE, GOVERNMENT ​SAYS</h2>
<p>But the lower courts ​have declined to order ⁠a halt to production while the legal process is ongoing, and have also suggested that the government could seek to remedy regulatory shortcomings.</p>
<p>"The government's position ​is of course that the licences are valid," Fredrik Sejersted, Norway's attorney general, told ​Reuters ahead ⁠of the hearing.</p>
<p>"If they should be overturned then it will be quite complicated," he said. "Legally, politically and for energy security in Europe."</p>
<p>Norway produces around 2% of global oil and became Europe's largest supplier of natural gas ⁠after ​Russia's invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, making it a ​crucial provider of energy.</p>
<p>The supreme court hearing is set to run for four days, and a verdict in the case is expected ​sometime later this year.</p>
