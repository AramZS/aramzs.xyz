<p>More than a dozen local law enforcement agencies across the country have used opioid settlement money to procure <a href="https://www.motherjones.com/politics/2026/07/police-losing-jobs-flock-cameras-alprs/">Flock cameras</a> and other AI-powered automated license plate readers (ALPRs), an investigation by <em>Mother Jones</em> has found.</p>
<p>Settlement funds began flowing to states and localities in 2022, after major pharmaceutical companies agreed to resolve thousands of lawsuits over the industry’s role in the opioid crisis. Altogether, state and local governments secured more than $50 billion in funds, to be paid out over 18 years, from the various manufacturers, distributors, and pharmacies accused of fueling the deadly epidemic.</p>
<p>Public health advocates hoped the settlements would provide lifesaving resources for historically underserved communities ravaged by opioid addiction. But as the money has been disbursed, local officials have <a href="https://drugfree.org/drug-and-alcohol-news/opioid-settlement-funds-misused-nationwide/">come under fire </a>for putting crucial dollars toward policing instead of prioritizing addiction prevention, recovery, treatment, and harm reduction services: <a href="https://kffhealthnews.org/public-health/opioid-settlements-law-enforcement-spending-states-towns-guns-narcan/">KFF Health News</a> found that police spent settlement money on Tasers, firearm silencers, drones, and more; in West Virginia, some rural counties <a href="https://westvirginiawatch.com/2025/07/08/investigation-wv-counties-spend-opioid-crisis-money-on-jail-instead-of-recovery/">used</a> the funds to pay off their jail bills.</p>
<figure><blockquote><p>“Whatever funds you’re spending on a camera, you’re not spending on getting someone into treatment.”</p></blockquote></figure>
<p>But little attention has been paid to how police departments have used those opioid funds to build out AI-powered surveillance networks like Flock, whose ALPRs have become increasingly popular with police even as they’ve drawn intense <a href="https://www.morningbrew.com/stories/backlash-to-flock-cameras-is-now-bipartisan-issue">bipartisan pushback </a>in the communities where they’ve been deployed. A review of meeting minutes, <a href="https://whoarethesepeopleorg.substack.com/p/surveillance-over-substance">local</a> <a href="https://thebeaconnews.org/stories/2026/04/15/missouri-opioid-settlement-spending-2025/">reporting</a>, state opioid expenditure disclosures, and invoices obtained via public records requests reveals that at least twelve sheriffs’ offices and police departments have since 2024 spent their opioid funds on Flock cameras. Another seven law enforcement agencies used the money to purchase similar ALPR technology from competitors <a href="https://www.npr.org/2026/08/23/nx-s1-5931446-e1/flock-safety-alternatives-new-names-same-surveillance">Motorola</a>, <a href="https://www.404media.co/cities-are-ditching-flock-immediately-replacing-it-with-axon-license-plate-readers/">Axon</a>, and <a href="https://cybernews.com/privacy/flock-verkada-license-plate-cameras-comparison/">Verkada</a>.</p>
<p>In sum, <em>Mother Jones </em>found that 19 agencies in ten different states spent more than $920,000 in opioid funds on Flock and other ALPRs, a likely undercount given the inconsistency with which opioid settlement expenditures are tracked and disclosed across states and local jurisdictions.</p>

<p>In city council and county commission meetings, police sought to convince local officials that the cameras were a good use of the opioid money because they would help catch drug traffickers and reduce drug-related crimes. But Robyn Oster, director of policy at the nonprofit Partnership to End Addiction, said that this type of spending doesn’t honor the spirit of the settlements.</p>
<p>“It’s just really not the way to make the most impact in addressing addiction,” said Oster. “While $50 billion may seem like a lot of funds, once it’s divided up over 18 years and thousands of localities, a lot of places really aren’t getting that much, particularly given the scope of the crisis…Whatever funds you’re spending on a camera, you’re not spending on getting someone into treatment.”</p>
<p>At a <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=m8hfbz93nFo&amp;t=5940s">June 2025 meeting</a> in Sunnyside, Washington—an agricultural hub where roughly one in five people live in poverty—council members were thrilled by the prospect of spending their opioid settlement money on a Flock camera. Around $200,000 had been sitting, untouched, in the city’s coffers <a href="https://www.yakimaherald.com/news/local/government/yakima-county-communities-around-the-state-to-receive-funding-from-wa-opioid-deal/article_718e6860-4378-11ed-8f59-431fe8885abe.html">since 2022</a>, even as the area faced a dire need for expanded treatment services: Yakima County <a href="https://www.applevalleynewsnow.com/news/yakima-county-faces-critical-need-for-medical-detox-center-amid-overdose-crisis/article_17f8a616-506b-4468-ad4d-7e2c3aaacc92.html">lacks a medical detox center</a> and has <a href="https://adai.washington.edu/wadata/deaths.htm#showdiv1">one of the highest</a> overdose death rates in Washington state.</p>
<p>“We’ve had several people … in that strip mall next to the hotel … that have complained about drug use and things like that behind their business, and they’ve asked us to place a trailer there,” the Sunnyside police chief <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=m8hfbz93nFo&amp;t=5940s">told city councilmembers</a>. He requested the approval of $24,000 in opioid settlement funds to go to a one-year lease for a mobile Flock security trailer, which would integrate with the city’s existing Flock surveillance system.</p>
<p>“I was trying to find creative ways to pay for one because $24,000 wasn’t in our budget this year,” he added, “but we’ve got this opioid settlement money, and if we’re tying it to the drug use problem we’re having at these hotels, I think we can justify the expenditure there.”</p>
<p>The city council unanimously voted to approve the police chief’s request. “Can we afford two of them?” one councilmember asked with enthusiasm.</p>
<p>Thousands of miles away, in Ohio County, West Virginia, county commissioners also voted unanimously in favor of spending opioid money on Flock cameras. In September 2025, the commission approved the sheriff’s office’s request for six Flock ALPRs, totaling $57,900 for the first three years. According to meeting minutes, Chief Deputy Kent Lewis “said a representative from the company is able to come and give a presentation,” and “added that Walmart and Lowe’s use FLOCK, so we may be able to tie into their system as well.” When one commissioner asked whether the cameras were an appropriate use of the funds, the deputy sheriff responded that they would “[help] track drugs coming into our area.”</p>
<p>Three months later, Sheriff H. Nelson Croft Jr. gave an update on Flock to the commissioners. According to meeting minutes, the system logged 28,000 license plate readings on Black Friday alone. Ohio County has a population of around 40,000.</p>
<p>The surveillance industry has taken notice, explicitly encouraging cops to spend opioid money on the technology. A December blog post from the multinational security firm Leonardo <a href="https://www.leonardocompany-us.com/lpr/blog/public-safety-grants-remain-strong-even-as-other-programs-shrink">called opioid settlement funds</a> “a major new opportunity” for law enforcement to invest in license plate readers.</p>
<p>“Eligible spending categories vary by state,” the blog post noted, but approved uses often include “LPR technology due to its role in detecting vehicles tied to drug trafficking, overdose events, and fugitive recovery.” In June, <a href="https://www.404media.co/this-company-will-add-phone-airpod-and-smartwatch-trackers-to-license-plate-readers/">404 Media reported</a> that Leonardo planned to add sensors to its ALPRs that “would also sweep up unique identifiers of mobile phones, wearables, and other Bluetooth-enabled devices” in passing cars, enabling the cameras to track not just cars but “the location of particular people.”</p>
<figure><blockquote><p>“The funds that are supposed to go to the public to help build back and reverse the nightmare that we lived through are being appropriated to use artificial intelligence to surveil us.”</p></blockquote></figure>
<p><em>Mother Jones </em>identified four additional locations where local officials explored using opioid money to pay for Flock cameras but ultimately funded them through other parts of city or county budgets. In Green, Ohio, for instance, the city council <a href="https://www.akron.com/articles/green-city-council-approves-flock-cameras/">approved</a> the purchase of 14 Flock cameras in 2024, and the mayor stated at the time that the city could use $20,000 of opioid money to offset the cost. But Shelley Goodrich, Green’s director of finance, told me the city ultimately paid for the first two years of the contract using a different funding source. “We were planning to use Drug Task Force funds beginning in 2026; however, we have not made any payments in 2026,” she wrote. This month, the Green City Council voted <a href="https://www.akron.com/articles/green-council-votes-down-flock-camera-amendment/">against</a> renewing its contract with Flock, citing concerns about privacy and oversight.</p>
<p>In Etowah County, Alabama, commissioners authorized two disbursements from the opioid settlement fund to pay for ten Flock cameras in August 2024 and March 2025, totaling $42,267. But minutes from October 2025 show that the sheriff department’s budget was increased by $42,267, “to reimburse the Opioid Settlement fund for the FY 2024 and FY 2025 annual contract for the Flock safety cameras.” The Etowah County Sheriff’s Office did not respond to questions about why it reimbursed the funds or whether it plans to use opioid money for future renewals of the Flock contract.</p>
<p>Opioid spending on police surveillance technology isn’t limited to ALPRs. <em>Mother Jones</em> also identified six locations that have used opioid settlement money to fund contracts with Peregrine Technologies, an AI-powered software platform that integrates law enforcement surveillance data from a variety of sources.</p>
<p>In October, the city of Campbell, California, approved a three-year, $71,500 annual contract with Peregrine using opioid settlement funds. A publicly accessible <a href="https://lflink.campbellca.gov/LFLINK/DocView.aspx?id=234929&amp;dbid=0&amp;repo=CityHall&amp;cr=1">copy</a> of the contract states that “the initial Customer Data sources and systems” integrated into Peregrine would include both Flock and Axon ALPRs, as well as Axon body cameras, police records, and the Axon Fusus intelligence platform. Two months later, the city council in Chicopee, Massachusetts, approved a five-year, $837,500 contract with Peregrine, to be paid using opioid funds.</p>
<p>Chicopee was among the western Massachusetts cities hit hardest by the opioid crisis, and in 2024, it still had the second-highest overdose rate in Hampden County. In meeting minutes, the Chicopee mayor noted there was “quite a bit of money in the opioid agreement account” and said that the Hampden County Sheriff’s Office supported increasing the Peregrine contract from three years to five years.</p>
<figure><a href="https://datawrapper.dwcdn.net/klwy8/3/"><img alt="Peregrine Contracts" src="https://datawrapper.dwcdn.net/klwy8/full.png"/></a></figure>
<p>Peregrine itself has been proud to announce “new partnerships” with police departments in Cranston and South Kingstown, Rhode Island. “Both agencies leveraged opioid settlement funds to invest in their communities by strengthening interagency collaboration, operational awareness, and investigative workflows through Peregrine,” <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/posts/peregrine-technologies_we-recently-kicked-off-two-new-partnerships-activity-7478443350606413825-nsjD/">stated</a> a July LinkedIn post from the company.</p>
<p>Revelations that local governments spent residents’ opioid money on Flock cameras has stoked furor in both Pensacola, Florida, and Monongalia County, West Virginia. In Pensacola, the mayor approved a two-year, $108,000 contract for 18 Flock cameras using opioid funds. <a href="https://opgov.news/articles/pensacola-city-council-now-controls-opioid-litigation-settlement-funds">After seven months of public backlash</a>, the Pensacola City Council announced in June that it would no longer allow opioid money to be spent on Flock cameras when the contract next came up for renewal.</p>
<p>In Morgantown, West Virginia, constituents have shown up at the Monongalia County Courthouse every Wednesday morning to speak out against local officials’ support for Flock. In November, the Monongalia County Commission <a href="https://www.newsandsentinel.com/news/local-news/2026/07/flock-safety-camera-usage-stirs-controversy-in-monongalia-county/">approved</a> the sheriff’s request to use up to $180,000 of settlement funds to install 20 Flock cameras across the county. To some people in the area, this use of the opioid money felt like salt in an all-too-fresh wound.</p>
<p>“As somebody who has had family members directly affected by the opioid crisis, I find the use of these funds absolutely disgusting,” said Harrison York, a 25-year-old electrical engineering student at West Virginia University in Morgantown. “The thing that got my family members clean were methadone services, and that’s what this opioid settlement money should be used for.” He pointed to <a href="https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2831697">research</a> <a href="https://www.japha.org/article/S1544-3191(22)00413-7/abstract">showing</a> that criminalization <a href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1360-0443.2010.02990.x">does little</a> to help people struggling with drug addiction, and contended that further policing “would punish the poor and the working class, who are victims” of the opioid epidemic.</p>
<p>Adam Menear, a 45-year-old father born and raised in Morgantown, started a <a href="https://www.change.org/p/stop-the-installation-of-flock-ai-surveillance-cameras-in-monongalia-county">petition</a> in March calling on the Monongalia County Commission to <a href="https://www.change.org/p/stop-the-installation-of-flock-ai-surveillance-cameras-in-monongalia-county">halt</a> the installation of Flock cameras that has received more than 1,500 signatures to date.</p>
<p>“There is not a single human being inside the state of West Virginia who was born and raised here who has not been adversely affected by the opioid crisis,” Menear told me. “The new crisis is the fact that the funds that are supposed to go to the public to help build back and reverse the nightmare that we lived through are being appropriated to use artificial intelligence to surveil us.”</p>
