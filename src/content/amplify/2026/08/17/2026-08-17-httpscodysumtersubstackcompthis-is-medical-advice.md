<p>Over the past year, there has been a dramatic increase in the number of YouTube channels producing medical advice videos targeted at seniors.</p>
<figure><a href="https://codysumter.substack.com/p/this-is-medical-advice"><img alt="Video preview" src="https://substack-video.s3.amazonaws.com/video_upload/post/208607133/c8105a53-3cf9-4b2d-bb6c-f1c34fb09cd6/transcoded-00155.png?refresh=Mon%20Aug%2017%202026%2005:34:39%20GMT-0700%20(Pacific%20Daylight%20Time)"/></a><figcaption>Video — play in the original article</figcaption></figure>
<p>And it’s not just YouTube. You may have seen the NYT’s recent piece on the broader trend of <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/video/technology/100000011001849/ai-influencers-health-supplements-fake-ads.html">fake health influencers hawking supplements</a>. Or, if you’re a nerd, Indicator’s write up on <a href="https://indicator.media/p/ai-generated-doctors-are-dispensing-dubious-health-advice">AI-generated doctors</a> from earlier this year. No social media platform with a video upload button appears to be safe.</p>
<p>For the past few months, I’ve been monitoring a network of 150 such inauthentic medical channels targeted at seniors in an attempt to understand what they are, why they exist, and how they’re made.</p>
<p>Spoilers: the answer is AI. But unlike <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KGfyXzQKvYI">shrimp Jesus</a>, this AI slop realized that the only thing more profitable than memes is convincing your parents to go off their meds.</p>
<p>An example might be useful here. So let’s look at the case of a 72-year-old diabetic from earlier this year. In May he stumbles across a video from a doctor he doesn’t know. The doctor promises a simple, natural remedy for high blood pressure. Notably, one better than his pills. Over the next half hour, this doctor discusses recent studies and references patients she’s personally seen and treated before laying out changes the 72-year-old should make. After watching it, he leaves a comment thanking the doctor and commits to trying the new routine.</p>

<p>The problem, obviously, is that she isn’t real. What she is is a ChatGPT subscription masquerading as a cardiac surgeon.</p>
<p>In other words, she’s AI slop.</p>
<p>Dr. Claire isn’t even particularly high-quality AI slop. If you look closely, there’s some pretty obvious artifacting, especially around the name badge. And her voice has a distinct robotic accent. It’s all squarely in the uncanny valley. But for those older, who may be less aware of what to look for, or who may have some mild hearing or vision loss, it appears to be good enough.</p>

<p>At that scale, it’s not a stretch to say that a meaningful percentage of older adults are watching these videos. Of those, many will not realize they are AI, or if they do, they may believe the information is still trustworthy. And of those, a not-insignificant number have likely already made real health and medical decisions based on that advice.</p>
<p>Maybe you could make the argument that this is fine, if the information in the videos were accurate.</p>
<p>Unfortunately, what I’ve found shows that:</p>

<p>So buckle up, this is going to get dark.</p>
<h2><strong>Part 1 — Full of lies</strong></h2>
<p>Fact-checking AI slop channels is a literal nightmare.</p>
<p>They put out near-daily videos. Those videos are filled with medical claims. And citations for those claims are often lacking. So if we did want to analyze a channel like Dr. Claire’s, we would have to knowingly subject ourselves to 65 hours of AI slop, transcribe every claim and citation made across more than 100 videos, and then spend dozens more hours trying to track down sources for each and every one of those claims.</p>
<p>Like I said, a literal nightmare.</p>
<p>So, anyway, here’s a breakdown of every claim made in a Dr. Claire video over the past 8 months. All 2,276 of them. Thankfully, many of them turned out to be duplicates, so we can cluster them to land at a nice even 456 distinct claims backed by 235 explicit citations.</p>

<h2><strong>Part 2 — Why is she doing this?</strong></h2>
<p>Money. The answer is money. Obviously.</p>
<p>These channels are attention farms. They’re Cocomelon for those with an AARP membership. The longer they keep you or your parents watching, the more ads YouTube will play and the more money they will make. They do not care what they have to say in order to keep you watching. It’s just a grift. It’s just the thing that came after dropshipping and NFTs.</p>
<p>And, to be sure, there is a buck to be made if your channel is the one to break through. Medical videos on YouTube can earn up to <a href="https://vidiq.com/blog/post/most-profitable-youtube-niches/">$10-20 per thousand views</a> (RPM). <p>But because the ad revenue isn’t enough on its own (and isn’t as lucrative on other platforms), they seem to always have a product to sell you too. In the case of Dr. Claire, that product is an <a href="https://seniorhealthlife.gumroad.com/l/bloodpressureprotocol">AI slop e-book</a> that they will happily sell you for $89 on Gumroad. And don’t bother writing a bad review when you discover that the chapter they told you to jump to if you’re experiencing heart failure doesn’t exist. They’ll just delete your bad review to keep the book at 5 stars.</p>

<p>By this point, I hope that we can agree that Dr. Claire is not a cardiac surgeon with 20 years of experience, and that the people behind her channel shouldn’t be giving out medical advice on the internet.</p>
<p>However, at the start of all this I said that not only were these AI slop channels spreading harmful medical misinformation, but that they were doing so <em>intentionally</em>. Let me now demonstrate that by showing you how these channels are made and operated.</p>
<p>For the Dr. Claire channel, our first clue comes from the name. Whether intentional or not, the selection of Dr. Claire Whitmore as a name was quite clever, as there turns out to be an actual doctor by a similar name at a prestigious university.</p>
<p>She is not a cardiac surgeon, nor is she involved in this channel in any way. But by piggybacking off the existence of a real Dr. Whitmore, who works at a real School of Medicine, the channel is able to survive a cursory "is this person real" Google search. And it certainly has been effective in tricking viewers. A quick perusal of the comments on her videos reveals people defending the channel as legitimate on the basis of that search result.</p>

<p>The promise here is simple. They will teach you how to set up an AI automation pipeline that will brainstorm a channel identity for you, come up with video ideas for that channel, write scripts for each idea, “film” and “edit” the videos, and manage the channel with as little human involvement as possible. In the process, they’ll teach you how to create channels focused on everything from financial advice to baby animals to horror stories to bible stories to police body-cam footage to music videos to whatever the next unexplored, but potentially profitable, niche is.</p>
<p>Over the past few months, I’ve been collecting the prompts these communities share back and forth.</p>
<p>Here, for example, are excerpts from the prompts they use to generate video ideas:</p>
<blockquote><p><em>Primary Goal: Create authoritative, data-driven, and fear-inducing video titles that trigger urgency, trust, and curiosity among senior viewers.</em></p></blockquote>
<blockquote><p><em>MANDATORY RULES:</em></p><ul><li><p><em>Use strong punctuation for emotional impact (e.g., !!, ???).</em></p></li><li><p><em>Every title must include at least one specific number (examples: 9X, 70%, 1 FOOD, 2:00 PM, 30 DAYS).</em></p></li><li><p><em>Titles must include senior-relevant health keywords, such as (but not limited to): Sarcopenia, Muscle Loss, Memory Decline, Joint Pain / Joints, Cholesterol, Blood Pressure</em></p></li><li><p><em>Tone should emphasize urgency, prevention, and hidden danger, while still sounding credible and evidence-based.</em></p></li></ul><p><em>OBJECTIVE:</em></p><ul><li><p><em>Maximize click-through rate (CTR) by combining fear-based curiosity with practical solutions seniors feel they must know now.</em></p></li></ul></blockquote>

<p>The prompts here are straightforward. And they produce what you would expect: fear-based titles and thumbnails optimized to get an old person to click them.</p>
<p>As much as I might personally hate clickbait, though, it’s not the problem here. Well, at least it’s not the biggest problem we care about right now. Coming up with a title and thumbnail before figuring out the actual video is common across YouTube. That’s basically MrBeast’s whole shtick. And it works fine for content that can be made up as you go along. When the title is a jumping-off point and not a conclusion. Or even as a means to crystallize and focus an idea you’ve already been thinking through.</p>
<p>But what happens when you take a title that makes a medical claim with no basis in reality and tell another AI to write a script for a video based on that title? Well, for the outlandish video titles these prompts generate, it either has to walk back the promise made in the title, or it has to lie. And I’ll let you guess which one they tell the AI to do.</p>
<blockquote><p><em>Your goal is to write a complete, high-authority, long-form video script based on the provided video title. The script must be structured to build maximum trust and viewer retention in the senior demographic (60+).</em></p><p><em>Start with a rhetorical question directly referencing the Core Problem from the title (e.g., “Are your doctor’s orders actually making your joint pain worse?”).</em></p><p><em>Define the problem with a specific, alarming statistic relevant to the title’s topic. Explicitly promise to reveal the solution.</em></p><p><em>Trigger fear, urgency, and curiosity</em></p><p><em>Clearly show what seniors stand to lose if they ignore this issue</em></p></blockquote>
<blockquote><p><em>Immediately establish credibility. Introduce yourself as the Expert (”As a doctor who has treated thousands...”). Use a specific, high-percentage data point to validate your method (e.g., “92% of my patients who followed this saw results”).</em></p><p><em>Mention years of experience, number of patients treated, and high success or improvement rates</em></p><p><em>Establish trust and medical legitimacy</em></p></blockquote>
<blockquote><p><em>Announce the controversial nature of the advice, often by stating that the commonly accepted “healthy” solution is actually the problem (e.g., “For decades, you’ve been told to eat Fish Oil... they were wrong. I’m going to show you why.”).</em></p><p><em>Invent and quote a statistic from a fictional but credible study to prove the danger (e.g., “A recent study showed seniors who ate X lost 31% more cognitive function...”).</em></p></blockquote>
<blockquote><p><em>Use a <strong>(</strong>fictional but realistic) study with:</em></p><ul><li><p><em>A big number of seniors (e.g. 1,800 seniors).</em></p></li><li><p><em>Time frame (e.g. 12-18 months).</em></p></li><li><p><em>A % difference in muscle loss or weakness.</em></p></li></ul></blockquote>
<p>When I started researching these channels, I had assumed that the fabricated claims and citations were an unintentional byproduct of a hallucinating AI being pushed beyond its training data. But this explicit instruction to lie is found all across the prompts these channels use.</p>
<p>Similar language is used when instructing LLMs to generate fabricated patient stories.</p>
<blockquote><p><em>Tell a detailed, emotional story about a fictional patient who was suffering due to this specific mistake, and how the eventual solution transformed their life. The result must be specific and aspirational.</em></p><ul><li><p><em>Give the person a name, age, and background (e.g. Margaret, 62, office worker; Harold, 71, retired carpenter).</em></p></li><li><p><em>Show their struggle (falls, weak legs, can’t carry groceries, can’t climb stairs).</em></p></li><li><p><em>Reveal their bad habit.</em></p></li><li><p><em>Show the transformation.</em></p></li></ul></blockquote>
<p>In the course of over 100 videos, Dr. Claire brings up 281 fake patients.</p>
<p>Including, notably, 36 Margarets and 11 Harolds.</p>
<p>Plus 22 Roberts. 20 Thomases. 14 Jameses. 12 Susans. 12 Dorothys. 11 Patricias. 9 Helens. 8 Davids. 8 Williams. 7 Richards. 7 Georges. 5 Otises. And 5 Eleanors. Among many. Each with a made-up age, background, and tragic story.</p>
<p>But don’t forget the real reason for these videos:</p>
<blockquote><p><em>Sprinkle engagement:</em></p><ul><li><p><em>“If you’ve been eating this for years, type ‘GUILTY’ in the comments.”</em></p></li><li><p><em>“Does this surprise you? Comment ‘SHOCKED’.”</em></p></li></ul><p><em>Ask for viewer engagement (e.g., “Did you believe the lie about Fish Oil?”). Re-ask for the Subscription/Like in an educational, non-pushy tone.</em></p></blockquote>
<p>The creators of these channels <em>explicitly ask for</em> fake studies, fake statistics, and fake patient stories. The AI does not accidentally hallucinate. They are <em>told to lie</em> to justify the outlandish medical claims made in the video’s title and thumbnail in order to keep people watching and buy whatever it is that they happen to have a referral link for.</p>
<p>Now, I can’t prove that any of these are the exact prompts used by Dr. Claire or any other particular channel. But I also can’t ignore just how close the end results are.</p>
<p>So, to me, the verdict is clear. These channels have a willful disregard for your health. They do not care about you. They do not care about your wellbeing. They only care about the money.</p>
<h2><strong>Part 4 — Can anything be done?</strong></h2>
<p>Health misinformation is nothing new. There have always been quacks and snake-oil salesmen. Over time, I’d like to think that we’ve developed a societal immune response to such quackery. Though some decades it appears to work better than others…</p>
<p>However, a couple of things have structurally changed over the past few years as a result of AI.</p>
<p><strong>1. Production cost.</strong> A laptop and a few subscriptions now produces a video in minutes that looks nearly as good as one that takes real people days to script, film, and edit. This makes it easy to flood the internet with content that looks superficially high-quality while actually being incredibly hollow.</p>
<p><strong>2. Disposable personas.</strong> When prior generations of quacks got caught, they had a real face and a real reputation to go along with it. The snake-oil salesman would have to literally get up and leave town in hopes of finding new marks elsewhere. Today? The cost to create a new channel with a new face and a new daily upload schedule is essentially zero.</p>
<p>That doesn’t mean that this is all inevitable. As we saw, people aren’t making these channels for fun.</p>
<p>When Jonathan Jarry at McGill University <a href="https://www.mcgill.ca/oss/article/medical-health-and-nutrition-pseudoscience-technology/deceitful-ai-videos-mislead-seniors-important-health-issues">looked into a similar channel</a> late last year, he found that the operators were likely part of a content farm based in Vietnam, running a whole network of such health scam channels. It’s hard to know whether any particular channel is being run out of such a content farm or from a random griftrepreneur’s bedroom. Either way, they’re doing it because there’s money to be made.</p>
<p>And if that money dries up or becomes hard enough to extract, they will stop. Or, more likely, move on to whatever hyped get-rich-quick scheme comes next.</p>
<p>There have been some hopeful signs on this front in recent months. YouTube appears to have <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=14Vm0CiyUVE">expanded enforcement</a> of their Inauthentic Content policy, developed <a href="https://research.google/pubs/scalable-detection-of-adversarial-synthetic-slop-and-coordinated-media-abuse-a-lora-enabled-multimodal-defense-system/?utm_campaign=the-slop-purge-has-arrived&amp;utm_medium=referral&amp;utm_source=insidethecreator.beehiiv.com">new methods</a> for detecting Adversarial Synthetic Slop, and announced they will be adding a <a href="https://blog.youtube/news-and-events/improving-ai-labels-viewers-creators/">label to videos they believe are AI</a>. This increased transparency is a meaningful step. I should note though: when I looked at the 68 channels in my dataset with over a million views, 41% were not labeled or had managed to disable the AI label since it went live and it appears that very legitimate channels (not AI slop) are being <a href="https://x.com/Kurz_Gesagt/status/2083191397981561232">mistakenly</a> <a href="https://youtu.be/OX1yHmeLPy8?si=tZ1K9M4grdRiUruw">punished</a> under these expanded enforcement efforts.</p>
<p>Trust &amp; Safety is an unglamorous job. If done well, it’s an invisible fight to preserve the potential of the internet while defending against the worst of its abuses. Do your job well and folks start to wonder if the work actually matters. Make a mistake? Accidentally over-enforce a policy? Fail to predict and prepare for the next novel abuse type? Well, that’s when overlong articles end up getting written, politicians start eyeing you, and conspiracy theorists start circling.</p>
<p>And, unfortunately, just as AI is dramatically changing the scope and scale of abuse types that must now be considered, platforms appear to have a rapidly shrinking appetite for this exact type of work. But it is work that must be undertaken if we are to ensure that AI is a net-benefit to humanity. Both by platforms and by policy makers.</p>
<p>Should a channel dispensing medical advice be monetizable without a person being accountable for it? Should a synthetic persona be allowed to claim a credential it doesn't have? Should an AI label be something the uploader can switch off? Should it even be the only signal of provenance we have? Thankfully, none of these require “solving” AI. But they do require deciding that a persona is not a person, and that the difference should cost something.</p>
<p>Though… if we did want to start somewhere, might I suggest California’s <a href="https://www.mbc.ca.gov/Consumers/unlicensed-practice.aspx">Business and Professions Code Section 2052 and 2054</a> (or your local equivalent). Aka why it’s a crime for humans to hold themselves as a physician, surgeon, or medical practitioner without actually being licensed. These Unlicensed Practice codes are fairly universal. They just weren’t written with fake AI doctors in mind.</p>
<p>Thankfully, that’s starting to change.</p>
<p>Last year, <a href="https://calmatters.digitaldemocracy.org/bills/ca_202520260ab489">AB 489</a> was passed in California. Authored by <a href="https://bonta.asmdc.org/">Assemblymember Mia Bonta</a>, it extended the Business and Professions Code to cover AI chatbots. A number of other states have passed similar laws over the past 18 months. Unfortunately, for us, they are largely focused on developers building AI chatbot products that claim to be doctors or actual medical professionals using AI in questionable ways. Both are important, but it’s unclear if they cover the types of abuse we’ve been looking at.</p>
<p>So, either 1/ these existing laws should be explicitly and publicly interpreted as covering fake AI doctors on social media platforms OR 2/ new laws, like AB 489, should be drafted and championed to extend the Codes to explicitly cover these synthetic entities.</p>
<p>If it’s illegal for a human, then it should probably be a crime when an AI does it too.</p>
<p>This will not magically solve the problem of medical mis- and disinformation online (AI-enabled or not). It won’t even, necessarily, address all the channels we’ve been discussing. The operators behind these channels are not always discoverable and even if we could unmask them, many are likely to be in jurisdictions that would make any sort of legal remedy challenging. But defining the boundaries of what we, as a society, consider unacceptable allows us to then ask the obvious next question: how much of this content is actually out there and why is it being allowed, promoted, and monetized?</p>
<p>Given that 74% of the channels in my dataset currently impersonate some form of medical professional, I’m going to go out on a limb and say that doing so is the most effective strategy for fooling older adults. If the next batch of such scams are forced to stop impersonating doctors, then they will be less effective, they will make less money, and there will be fewer of them.</p>
<p>So let’s do something about this.</p>
<p>Because if we don’t, then we, as a society, are going to have to start asking who is responsible when an AI-generated doctor tells your mother to skip her medication.</p>
