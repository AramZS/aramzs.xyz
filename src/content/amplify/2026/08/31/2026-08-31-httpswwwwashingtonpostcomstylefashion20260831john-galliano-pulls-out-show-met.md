<html><body><figure><img alt="" src="https://cloudfront-us-east-1.images.arcpublishing.com/wapo/U6IQNTW6VRSAHVJVOEWFL7ZKOI.jpg"/><figcaption>John Galliano at London's Langham Hotel in March. (Dave Benett/Getty Images)</figcaption></figure><p>John Galliano said Monday that he was withdrawing from an exhibition at the Metropolitan Museum of Art <a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/style/2026/08/03/fashion-genius-who-hurled-anti-semitic-slurs-strangers-2011-will-be-honored-next-met-gala-what-now/" rel="noreferrer" target="_blank" title="https://www.washingtonpost.com/style/2026/08/03/fashion-genius-who-hurled-anti-semitic-slurs-strangers-2011-will-be-honored-next-met-gala-what-now/">focused on his work</a> as a fashion designer. His statement comes after donors and Jewish leaders criticized the Met for featuring Galliano, who has made antisemitic and anti-Asian comments on multiple occasions.</p><p>“After much reflection and discussion with all those involved, I have decided, with great sadness, that it is best for the exhibition not to take place at this time,” Galliano <a href="http://instagram.com/p/DctHgVHtnCb/?hl=en" rel="noreferrer" target="_blank" title="http://instagram.com/p/DctHgVHtnCb/?hl=en">said in a statement</a> on his Instagram page.</p><p>He added that he didn’t “want the debate surrounding me to place the Met in a difficult position or distract from the remarkable work of the Costume Institute.”</p><p>Galliano was fired from his job as the creative director of Dior after the Sun, a British tabloid, shared a video in 2011 of Galliano saying “I love Hitler.” Months later, a French tribunal found Galliano guilty of hate crimes after he berated patrons at a Paris cafe on different occasions.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cloudfront-us-east-1.images.arcpublishing.com/wapo/FVUQSDFFHYI7DHRY64C5ASF5LI.jpg"/><figcaption>Galliano in 2010. (Jacques Brinon/AP)</figcaption></figure><p>But Galliano’s outbursts only temporarily derailed his career. In 2014, he was hired as creative director at Maison Margiela and was welcomed back to the fashion arena after a period in an Arizona rehab facility. He spent the prior two years meeting with Jewish leaders to ask for forgiveness, including Abraham Foxman, a former national director of the Anti-Defamation League, who said in 2013 that he believed Galliano’s apologies to be sincere. </p><p>The Met has faced considerable backlash from Jewish figures — including Elisha Wiesel, the son of Holocaust survivor Elie Wiesel, and New York City Council Speaker Julie Menin — after the museum announced in July the nine-month show focused on Galliano’s work. “At a time of rising antisemitism, honoring Mr. Galliano sends the wrong message,” Eric S. Goldstein, CEO of the UJA-Federation of New York, a Jewish philanthropy, said in an emailed statement. </p><p>Anna Wintour, the global editorial director of Vogue who has organized the yearly Met Gala for decades, remained a supporter of Galliano’s despite the controversy. She arranged a temporary stint for him at Oscar de la Renta in 2013 and helped the designer land at Maison Margiela. Ahead of the show’s July announcement, Wintour — along with Andrew Bolton, curator of the Met’s Costume Institute, and Galliano — met with Jewish leaders and rabbis in New York. </p><p>“John’s decision that the exhibition devoted to his work should not take place at this time is very courageous and a measure of the man he is,” Wintour said in a statement Monday. “I believe this is the right decision, and both he and the Museum have my full support.”</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cloudfront-us-east-1.images.arcpublishing.com/wapo/WUPCSEK46XK5TCWNU3ILP2JIRI.jpg"/><figcaption>Anna Wintour and Galliano in 2017 in New York. (Dimitrios Kambouris/Getty Images)</figcaption></figure><p>Galliano maintains that he didn’t remember his hateful statements as a result of his substance abuse. “It’s the worst thing I have said in my life, but I didn’t mean it,” he said in <a href="https://archive.vanityfair.com/article/2013/7/1/galliano-in-the-wilderness" rel="">his first interview</a> after he was fired. His return to the spotlight hadn’t been entirely uncomplicated: In the 2024 documentary “<a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/style/fashion/2024/03/17/galliano-documentary/" rel="">High &amp; Low: John Galliano</a>,” one of Galliano’s victims, Philippe Virgitti, said that the designer never apologized to him. </p><p>“It’s clear they can’t do it in his lifetime; they can’t do it anytime soon,” Dana Thomas, who wrote the 2015 biography “Gods and Kings: The Rise and Fall of Alexander McQueen and John Galliano” and criticized the Met’s decision to feature him in a <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/08/06/opinion/met-gala-galliano-antisemitism.html" rel="">recent New York Times op-ed</a>, told The Washington Post, referring to the Met’s scuttled exhibit. “They need to put it in a box on the shelf and let it sit there for a while.”</p><p>In <a href="https://www.metmuseum.org/press-releases/spring-ci-2027-announcement" rel="">a statement</a>, the Met added that information around “a new spring Costume Institute exhibition and the 2027 Met Gala will be announced at a later date.”</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cloudfront-us-east-1.images.arcpublishing.com/wapo/EPLBB3VTGMTZTIAFYDU2SJE2WU.jpg"/><figcaption>Galliano, right, arrives at a police station in Paris in 2011 to face accusations that he made illegal antisemitic slurs. (Michel Euler/AP)</figcaption></figure><p>Galliano’s highly public remarks were roundly criticized. But before that — and since — his work in fashion received near-universal acclaim. Upon graduating from the prestigious British art and design school Central Saint Martins in 1984, his student collection was purchased by the London designer clothing boutique Browns; in 1995, he was tapped to lead design at Givenchy and became the first British designer to helm a French couture house. </p><p>His work at Givenchy was romantic and theatrical, and Madonna showed up to the 1996 premiere of “Evita,” in which she played Argentine first lady Eva “Evita” Perón, wearing a full look from Galliano’s Fall 1996 runway show: a leopard-print double-breasted coat, a black chiffon dress and a pink fascinator.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cloudfront-us-east-1.images.arcpublishing.com/wapo/LVIHFFHTQJLYX3GAYQFFTTVA3Q.jpg"/><figcaption>Madonna in Galliano at the "Evita" premiere in 1996. (Roberto Ramirez/Gamma-Rapho/Getty Images)</figcaption></figure><figure><img alt="" src="https://cloudfront-us-east-1.images.arcpublishing.com/wapo/QCCOQX3DT5BIXHROKXKA3TBVTA.jpg"/><figcaption>Nicole Kidman and Tom Cruise at the 1997 Oscars. Kidman wears an embellished chartreuse Dior dress by Galliano. (Chris Pizzello/AP)</figcaption></figure><p>Galliano’s most celebrated designs, though, largely come from his time in the 1990s and 2000s as the creative head of Dior. Galliano’s inspirations were wide-ranging and eclectic, incorporating gestures toward history and legend, and mashing them up with modern elements of punk rebellion and sex appeal. </p><p>The backward tuxedo Celine Dion wore to the Oscars in 1999 came from a surrealism-themed collection with references to Salvador Dalí and Jean Cocteau, for example. Other memorable looks from the era include the absinthe-colored, Chinoiserie-inspired gown Nicole Kidman plucked from the runway to wear to the 1997 Oscars, and the lingerie-adjacent navy lace slip dress Princess Diana wore to the 1996 Met Gala, right as her fashion choices were beginning to reflect a rebellion from palace protocols after her divorce from then-Prince Charles. </p><p>In recent years, Zendaya has more than once reached into the Galliano Dior archive for red-carpet appearances, including the “Spider-Man: Brand New Day” premiere this summer, where she wore a shimmering deep-blue “spiderweb” dress from a 1997 Dior collection. And Galliano’s gowns aren’t his only works that have become the stuff of legend: His 2011 newsprint-themed collection, beloved by fashion fans, made a cameo this year in “The Devil Wears Prada 2” when Emily Blunt wore a newsprint Dior sweatshirt on-screen. </p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cloudfront-us-east-1.images.arcpublishing.com/wapo/XYNBQ7WJYD34PSETSS2ILP3ZCU.jpg"/><figcaption>Zendaya wears a shimmering deep-blue “spiderweb” dress from a 1997 Dior collection in London this summer. (Jeff Spicer/Getty Images)</figcaption></figure><p>When Galliano returned to fashion after his ouster at Dior, the acclaim picked up right where it left off. His fall 2015 Maison Margiela ready-to-wear show, a haunting spectacle in which several models slunk down the runway hunched over and wearing garish clown makeup, was immediately met with resounding applause and cheers from the audience in Paris. His <a href="https://fashionista.com/2024/01/maison-margiela-artisanal-couture-2024-review" rel="">2024 couture show</a>, inspired by the Parisian underworld of the 1930s, is still talked about with near-reverence not just for its visual beauty but for its innovation (the collection debuted several construction techniques pioneered by Galliano), and its elegant inclusion of models with diverse backgrounds and body types. </p><p>As Shane O’Neill <a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/style/2026/08/03/fashion-genius-who-hurled-anti-semitic-slurs-strangers-2011-will-be-honored-next-met-gala-what-now/" rel="noreferrer" target="_blank" title="https://www.washingtonpost.com/style/2026/08/03/fashion-genius-who-hurled-anti-semitic-slurs-strangers-2011-will-be-honored-next-met-gala-what-now/">pointed out</a> in The Washington Post this year, Wintour’s employer, Condé Nast, has played a significant role in boosting Galliano’s profile over the years, both before and after the controversy in 2011 that cost him his job at Dior. “Early in his career, Wintour and her then-Vogue colleague André Leon Talley were huge boosters of Galliano’s work, connecting him with patrons and resources and cheering him on in person, in interviews and in the pages of Vogue,” O’Neill wrote. “After Galliano’s fall from grace, Wintour, who now serves as Condé Nast’s global chief content officer, stood by him. So did Jonathan Newhouse, Condé Nast’s chairman of the board, who made it a personal project to get Galliano’s career back on track.” </p><p>Condé Nast films co-produced “High &amp; Low: John Galliano,” which, as O’Neill noted, spent considerable time on Galliano’s antisemitic and racist remarks, even including footage of the designer being confronted with comments he did not remember making. Still, Wintour says in the documentary that she knew it was Galliano’s addiction that was causing his behavior: “I knew that’s not who he is, and I just wanted to help him.”</p></body></html>
