<p>There’s something really joyful about watching humanoid robots eat shit.</p>
<p>Last week, I turned in the first edit of the full manuscript of my book <em>Dad: A Pop History</em> (about which, too much later). It is the longest single piece of writing I’ve ever done, and every sentence of it — good bad or bland — is a sentence I came up with myself or in collaboration with my patient editor, my beloved agent, or my trusted family and friends. It is, for whatever else it is, a certified human-made object.</p>
<p>Last week was also the start of the fall college semester, and so, three times in a row on Monday, I delivered an impassioned plea to a roomful of undergraduates on the virtue and necessity of creating and submitting human-made work themselves. This course is about writing, I tell them. We will read and discuss the work of both creative writers and critics, analyze the meaning of their words, and speculate about how the writer produced that meaning. You will also be asked to produce writing yourself, and to account for your experience, understanding, and critical perspective on the course topics and readings. When I ask you to do research, you will be searching for sources, assessing their value and merit, synthesizing their arguments, and responsibly citing their authors. These processes — reading, writing, research — are not secondary to the work of this course; they ARE the work of this course, I say. I have been heartened, this week, to see some higher ed institutions — in <a href="https://www.forbes.com/sites/michaeltnietzel/2026/08/24/the-university-of-chicago-bans-ai-other-technology-in-more-courses/">Chicago</a> and in <a href="https://aiandeducation.mit.edu/report/">Boston</a> — release thoughtful statements and policies affirming how necessary this work is.</p>
<p>Both of these tasks were stressful ones. One, because I know that my own human words are fallible, that they might not live up to my dreams or the dreams of others for them; the other because it sometimes feels like that little speech is left doing the work of an entire higher ed infrastructure that has been utterly dismantled and ruined on purpose over the past couple of years. To convince students of the worth and possibility of their own human minds is supposed to be the work of a university, all of us together, is it not?</p>
<p>So: the aforementioned robots eating shit. Last week, I posted countless <em>America’s Funniest Home Videos</em>-level clips of the World Humanoid Robot Games in Beijing on my Instagram stories. Rarely have I posted anything on social media that’s been met with such an overwhelming wave of approval. My messages are filled with near-identical expressions of hilarity and happiness at the sight of these images. The inadvertent slapstick of these humanoid robots is virtuosic. I gasp and marvel at the hairpin turns they take <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yzXI7MYoNRM">from fluid mastery to absolute catastrophe</a>. No Warner Bros. animator could capture <a href="https://futurism.com/robots-and-machines/robot-michael-jackson-wiping-out-illustrates-industry-problem">the chaotic flailing of the “Billie Jean” robot</a> struggling to climb a stair in rhythm. I find it hard, with my paltry words, to describe what’s so enlivening about these terminators biting it, but it far outstrips any feeling of awe I might have at their feats of strength and dexterity. And I don’t think it’s a coincidence that so many of the crying-laughing emoji responses I’ve been collecting are coming from fellow teachers and writers.</p>
<p>I do realize, however, as a person with feelings and a strong sense of guilt, that it was, after all, human beings who made those robots who’ve been competing and crumpling in Beijing. I don’t like laughing at the failures of other people. Human hands crafted the robot feet that are occasionally performing great flying leaps but also collapsing into inert piles of garbage. Teams of human collaborators had the ingenuity to fashion the robots that can’t hammer a nail without assistance. Human minds spent years creating the exceptional robot who broke Usain Bolt’s 100-meter sprint record before booking it so fast into a wall that its ass exploded.</p>
<p>But I also realize that the goal of these competitions is to sharpen the theory and practical use of embodied intelligence, to outsource — in the way I tell my students to avoid — the very work of being a human in the world.</p>
<p>Earlier this month, Nicholas Johnston, the publisher of <em>Axios</em>, posted a short article — I mean, his name was on the byline, I don’t know who wrote it — called <a href="https://www.axios.com/2026/08/11/robot-book-club">“Robot Book Club.”</a> In it, Johnston describes how he was having difficulty working his way through his summertime pleasure reading. “AI has become my reading buddy,” he confesses/brags, “as I’ve plowed through my summer book list, helping me keep track of plots and characters, unpack themes, and make sense of wordy, old prose.” He asked his AI agent to help him understand James Fenimore Cooper’s <em>The Last of the Mohicans</em>, a novel he found perilously difficult to read and comprehend. The AI responded sympathetically. “Cooper’s sentences are long and his diction is archaic,” it commiserated. So, the AI wrote plain-language recaps of <em>Mohicans </em>for Johnston to turn to, ostensibly, after struggling his way through those long, archaic sentences. The AI read the novel for him, in other words, and he published an article to tell us how neat that is.</p>
<p>That the “diction is archaic” is one of the main complaints you hear from new undergrads in lit courses. Literature from olden times, literature from foreign places, literature from unknown, unusual perspectives. We call writing “archaic” to suggest that it’s old, irrelevant, obsolete, but, ironically, what we mean is that it feels <em>new</em> to us. The job, as I see it, is to help students learn how to read language that is different from their own, to detect style and meaning in it, to unpack why a sentence speaks to us in ways we haven’t heard before. To learn that the language is not a formal obstacle but the actual content itself. When AI boosters or even adopters talk about reading as a problem to solve, it gives away a signature flaw of their philosophy. These are not programs designed to help you more easily access knowledge; they’re programs designed to help you avoid accessing knowledge at all.</p>
<p>From the standpoint of a writer and a reader and a teacher of writing and a teacher of reading, these are not tools made for us. They were not designed by people who struggled through the work of reading and writing and want to help others. They are tools clearly designed by people who think that that work — all of it — is unnecessary, too hard, meaningless. They are tools <em>for</em> cheating, whether that’s how you use them or not, whether that’s what you understand “cheating” to mean or not. Imagine asking the guy who got an F in your general education intro to English literature class to design a program to help you pass it. Imagine asking the guy extinguishing the blazing butt of a robot to help you train to run the 100-meter dash.</p>
<p>It’s been a long week. Over the next couple months, I’m going to be editing my book and agonizing over sentences and paragraphs and transitions and kickers and small jokes and passing citations and turns of phrase that I will be the first and last person to lavish with that sort of attention. And over the next handful of weeks, I will be asking a roomful of first-year college students to take notes on paper and write essays in blue books and read <em>The Picture of Dorian Gray </em>and <em>The Custom of the Country </em>and all of their long, archaic sentences front-to-back and then show up in the seminar room and tell me what it was like for them. And it won’t feel intuitive for everybody, and it won’t always work, and maybe all of it is unnecessary, too hard, meaningless.</p>
<p>Perhaps I’ll one day look foolish for resisting in this way. But will I look as foolish as the men gathering up the lifeless skeleton of the robot who died trying to do the choreography from “Billie Jean”? I don’t know. The human thing is to keep living, keep dancing, keep running and find out.</p>

<p>This is from <a href="https://newrepublic.com/article/214282/furious-serial-killer-not-real-villain">my most recent </a><em><a href="https://newrepublic.com/article/214282/furious-serial-killer-not-real-villain">New Republic</a></em><a href="https://newrepublic.com/article/214282/furious-serial-killer-not-real-villain"> piece on what I think is one of the best new shows of the year: </a><em><a href="https://newrepublic.com/article/214282/furious-serial-killer-not-real-villain">Furious.</a></em></p>
<blockquote><p><em>Furious</em>, created by Elizabeth Meriwether, is about the systems through which men abuse women and girls, as well as the systems through which they get away with it. Sometimes those systems are occult bureaucracies and complicated conspiracies, but often, we see, there’s really no system at all but the patriarchy. The villainous men of this show—domestic abusers, biased bosses, sex traffickers, dirty lawyers—get away with their crimes by being bold enough to commit them in the first place, and they rely on the reflexive fear or complacency or even sympathy of their witnesses to protect them. It’s a world in which it’s perfectly natural for a man’s desires and impulses to put him afoul of the law, but unnatural for a woman to try to hold him accountable.</p><p>…</p><p>None of that would matter, though, if Meriwether didn’t have the genius for casting that she has. Amanda Seyfried, Michelle Williams, and Jenny Slate have all delivered career-best performances under her care. In <em>Furious</em>, there is, likewise, an embarrassment of riches: prestige TV veteran Emmy Rossum, stage actor and award-winning audiobook narrator Quincy Tyler Bernstine, and ferocious up-and-coming Irish actor Lola Petticrew. Each performance is so tenderly crafted and full of life—not one of these actors takes the available route of stoic gloom, the path of any number of raincoat-bedecked traumatized detectives and murderers before them. Abuse, violence, inequity have made these women strange, unpredictable, unable to play along and conform to the world around them. And that makes them spellbinding to watch onscreen—three weird sisters.</p></blockquote>
