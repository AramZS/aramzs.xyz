<p>The first time Davis Bates visited Hampshire College, he was technically trespassing.</p>
<p>With only a backpack and a sleeping bag, the high school senior had hitchhiked from his hometown of Sudbury, Massachusetts, to Amherst to see if the private liberal arts school might, as his guidance counselor suggested, be a good fit. Bates arrived well after dark to the rural campus. With no place to stay, he found his way into an unlocked lecture hall and decided to sleep there until he could explore on his own the next day. In the middle of the night, he awoke to the bright light of a security guard’s flashlight shining over him. The guard laughed and left Bates to go back to sleep. “That’s when I decided to come to Hampshire College,” he told me, a fellow alum, when I met him in May.</p>
<p>We were back on campus for the college’s annual commencement ceremony—its last. Earlier this year Hampshire succumbed to declining enrollment and other financial pressures and announced it would be closing its doors after fifty-six years. Though some students close to finishing their degrees would be able to do so during a limited “<a href="https://www.highereddive.com/news/hampshire-college-secures-loan-agreement-hold-final-fall-semester/822436/">teach-out</a>” in the fall, the grand pedagogical experiment that began in western Massachusetts more than half a century ago was coming to an end. “We live in a system that is not as kind to an idea of Hampshire as the system was when Hampshire started,” Bates told me. With its passing, he added, “It’s just really, really sad to not have this incubation space for people who create and for people who don’t have anywhere else to go.”</p>
<p>Hampshire was always unique in the landscape of higher education. Students were the architects of their own education. With faculty approval, you could do art, write a book, spend several months brokering corporate tax credits, or study spermatogenesis in cockroaches. There were no grades, only narrative evaluations. In the words of its founders, the college saw itself as “a vehicle for the realization of self <em>in </em>society.” As Archibald MacLeish, a poet and playwright, said at an event marking the school’s opening in 1970, these men saw higher education as a means of healing “troubles in the culture itself, in the civilization, in the state of civilization.” To them, these were not problems that could be healed through government intervention or, for that matter, even revolution.</p>
<p>There’s no doubt that the American university is in crisis. Donald Trump has accused higher educational institutions of being “infested with radicalism” and has leveraged the full force of the federal government to rid schools of what his administration perceives as “antisemitism” and “wokeness.” Yet as damaging as Trump’s second term has been to colleges and universities, the turmoil has been going on for some time. Over the past decade, public institutions, many of them facing declining state support, have slashed funding to the liberal arts. Smaller private institutions have implemented austerity measures due to declining enrollment and other market pressures. Education has refigured itself—and not particularly well—to serve the demands of policymakers and students demanding job-focused majors. “Campus and program closures, mergers, and consolidations have damaged an essential element of academic freedom . . . the right to listen and to hear as part of the right to examine issues and seek truth,” <a href="https://www.aaup.org/academe/issues/spring-2025/organizing-just-and-democratic-future">wrote</a> American Association of University Professors president Todd Wolfson and executive director Mia McIver in spring 2025.</p>
<p>In this context, worrying about the closure of a single small private school in one of the wealthiest states in the country may smack of irrelevance, if not elitism. Yet Hampshire’s unconventional pedagogy and holistic consideration of the “self” and “society” were a necessary check on the consensus belief that the main purpose of college is to build human capital. It is naive, perhaps, to think that the liberal arts as practiced on an elite college campus might fix today’s world. But a future that abandons it is a dire one.</p>
<p>In 1967, John Boettiger did the unimaginable: He gave up a tenure-track job at an esteemed liberal arts school to join a college that didn’t even have students. Boettiger, a professor of psychology and human development, had secured a position at Amherst College, his alma mater, only a year earlier. Partway through that first year, a fellow Amherst graduate, Ken Rosenthal, approached him with a proposition: “We were having lunch,” Boettiger recalled of his meeting with Rosenthal. “And he said ‘John . . . you’re really wasting your time teaching at Amherst College when you should be going down the road and joining those who are forming a new college.”</p>
<p>The origins of Hampshire College go back further. In 1958, presidents from four schools in the Pioneer Valley—Amherst, Mount Holyoke College, Smith College, and University of Massachusetts Amherst—floated the idea of creating a fifth institution in a document called “The New College Plan.” The treatise outlined a need for new, leaner institutions that would grant students independence in designing their own education. It didn’t see professors as shaping students so much as it saw students shaping themselves. “The most important contribution a college can make to its students is to develop in them a capacity to continue their education throughout their lives,” its authors wrote.</p>

<p>In 1965, an Amherst graduate and retired lawyer named Harold F. Johnson pledged $6 million to the creation of a new institution that adhered to many of the same principles of “The New College Plan.” Later pledges came from the Ford Foundation and donors to the Hampshire College Founders Fund. The federal government, which was injecting millions into college campuses to support campuses dealing with a rapidly growing student body, chipped in as well. “I had been floundering around wanting to do something with money which I had gotten rather quickly and not much through my own efforts, in other words through the stock market,” Johnson said in an interview nearly two decades later. Johnson and the fledgling school’s board of trustees, which included the presidents of all four neighboring institutions, filed articles of organization in September 1965, and the school welcomed its first class of 250 in 1970. At the time, tuition was $3,800 (just over $32,000 in today’s dollars). The amount of financial aid the school provided at its onset was limited, with approximately 20 percent of the student body receiving at least some in 1978. The <em>New York Times </em>compared its acceptance rate to that of “the most prestigious colleges.”</p>
<p>From the jump, Hampshire branded itself as an “independent, experimenting” institution, and its rigorous academic program was not for the faint of heart. An early catalog dedicated over a dozen pages to the school’s philosophy of serving as “a laboratory for experimenting with ways the private liberal arts college can be a more effective intellectual and moral force in a changing culture,” in addition to two dozen pages on the structure of its academic program. Students’ studies were divided into three separate “divisions” instead of the traditional four-year framework. Division I, or “basic studies,” served as a sort of broad survey of liberal arts as Hampshire understood it and aligned roughly with a student’s first year or so. In Division II, students chose an area in which to “concentrate” in—there were no majors or minors—and establish a program of study around that concentration in consultation with faculty. That “concentration” led to Division III, when a student was expected to spend “half or more of his time” on a large project, whether it be a play, academic thesis, book of poems, or some other product. In principle, as the college’s first president and vice president, Franklin Patterson and Charles Longsworth, explained in the 1966 book <em>The Making of a College</em>, “A student could receive his degree at Hampshire . . . and might, in the process, take no courses at all.”</p>
<p>Boettiger—who adopted the title of professor of human development—encouraged students to see their studies as not just academic but personal. “I [told] the students who were interested in my courses that if our focus is human development, that includes you,” Boettiger said. “You know, that’s not just human development as an academic enterprise. It’s human development in experiential terms.” Robert Emmet Meagher, who started teaching humanities at Hampshire in 1972, told me that at first, most classrooms didn’t have clocks. Courses did have start times and end times, but students could come and go as they needed. He compared it taking a book from the library shelves: Some you read cover to cover, maybe even multiple times. In other cases, you read a few chapters, learn what you need, and put the book down. “Course completion was not a crucial measure any more than reading a book,” he said.</p>
<p>Entrusting eighteen- to twenty-two-year-olds to be stewards of their own education wasn’t always easy. In 1971, Van R. Halsey Jr., Hampshire’s admissions director, griped to the<em> New York Times </em>that while most of the applications the school received were “high,” some were less serious. “Many thought this would be some kind of a lotus-land where they could sit around smelling the dandelions for four years,” he complained. Some students thrived, and local news captured the accomplishments of some students putting on their own plays, conducting environmental research, or creating their own courses. Others fared less well. One of the school’s first students transferred to a neighboring institution and accused Hampshire of “suffering from ‘isolationism, academic anxiety, and hyperseriousness’.”</p>
<p>When I started at Hampshire College in 2007, the school’s reputation as either a rigorous institution or as a “lotus-land” depended on who you asked. Things had changed from its early days: For one, students were expected to attend class—and do so on time. The idea that you could get through your entire college career by doing independent studies and projects without registering for courses was long gone. The shift, Jim Wald—a historian who started teaching at the college in 1986—told me, arose from the fact that students weren’t entirely prepared for Hampshire’s project-based approach in their first year. Over time, the entire curriculum, Meagher told me, became more course based.</p>
<p>I graduated in 2011, having written my Division III—the year-long project that functions as Hampshire’s rough equivalent to a senior thesis—on a nineteenth century nationalist heresy in the Eastern Orthodox Church. I spent the rest of the year living with my parents, applying to entry-level jobs and internships as the economy struggled in the wake of the financial crisis. After a few years at a nonprofit—and then this magazine—I started covering the radical right as a journalist, latching on to the resurgence of white nationalist activity during Trump’s angry, retaliatory first presidential campaign.</p>
<p>It didn’t surprise me that, on November 29, 2016, Trump tweeted that flag burners need to face “consequences” that could include “loss of citizenship or [a] year in jail.” What <em>did </em>surprise me was the fact that the post, as <a href="https://www.npr.org/2016/11/29/503719125/trumps-flag-burning-tweet-brings-back-1980s-era-controversy">major news outlets reported</a>, appeared to be a reference to Hampshire. Two days prior to Trump’s post, some four hundred people protested the college’s decision to temporarily remove the Stars and Stripes from the middle of campus following an incident in which someone had stolen the flag and burned it. No one knew who did it. It wasn’t clear that it was even a student.</p>
<p>Nora Claire Miller, who attended Hampshire from 2013 to 2017, told me that the atmosphere on campus after Trump’s election was intense. She recalled right-wing media outlets posting blogs harassing students on campus and debates among students as to how to respond to the demonstrators near campus. The whole saga culminated with Jesse Watters, a Fox News host, haranguing then-Hampshire president Jonathan Lash at his home. “Don’t you realize the whole country’s laughing at you right now?” Watters asked as Lash walked from his car to his home. When Lash tried to close his door, the Fox News pundit didn’t relent. “You made a lot of people very upset. I think you owe the country an explanation,” Watters demanded.</p>
<p>Hampshire College was never wealthy, especially compared to its next-door neighbor, Amherst College. In summer of 2007, Hampshire’s endowment was valued at a mere $45.4 million, and Hampshire’s then-president Ralph Hexter said in 2006 that 84 percent of the school’s revenue depended on tuition. The student body reached a high of 1,529 students in 2010—approximately half of which came from households that accounted for the top 20 percent of earners, according to a later <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/projects/college-mobility/hampshire-college"><em>New York Times</em></a> study. But the school had a persistent problem: retention. Under Lash, Hampshire sought to identify and recruit students it called “thrivers” to improve its retention rates, which had been as low as 78 percent for first-year students in the 2010s. Amherst College, by contrast, boasted an average retention rate of 95 percent. “Retention was a major, major issue. And obviously finances were by far worse,” Ali Mirsepassi, who sat on Hampshire’s board of trustees from 2016 to 2019, told me. Hampshire’s tuition-heavy budget needed students to stay. In 2012, the school took on a $14 million bond to fill financial gaps.</p>
<p>By 2016, the college’s fortunes, like that of many other small private liberal arts schools, had darkened. There were financial concerns: To complete infrastructure improvements and construction, as well as refinance some earlier debt, the institution took on another $15 million bond, bringing its debt to over $28 million. The culture on campus had shifted too. “There was a feeling that the place was under threat, which it was,” David Matheson, a former board member who led its finance committee, told me. “The level of trust between the various constituencies started eroding. That, unfortunately, continued over the subsequent few years and was a big part of the crisis in 2019,” he added.</p>
<p>The “crisis” that Matheson referred to is Hampshire’s first brush with death, which came under the leadership of the school’s seventh president, Miriam Nelson. (Nelson didn’t respond to a request for comment.) A former nutrition professor, administrator at Tufts University, and author of a series of bestselling self-help books on women’s health, Nelson ascended to her position in 2018. By then, enrollment had dropped to 1,191. The “thrivers” that Hampshire identified were paying less than earlier classes, with the average student paying 40 percent of the school’s listed tuition of around $50,000 in 2018, compared to 56 percent in 2013.</p>
<p>In mid-January, mere months into Nelson’s tenure, she announced the school was seeking a “strategic partner” to ensure the health of the college. The search appeared to have not lasted long. On February 1, Nelson told the Hampshire community that the school would not be accepting a new class and intended to close. Ali Mirsepassi, a board member at the time, recalled that he and other members of the board were called to a Zoom meeting, where they were informed less than an hour before Nelson’s public announcement. “It was a shock to virtually the whole board,” Matheson said. Later reporting from <a href="https://www.nepm.org/regional-news/2019-02-22/emails-show-hampshire-considered-umass-a-potential-partner-in-november">New England Public Media</a> and <a href="https://gazettenet.com/2019/09/02/new-documents-shed-further-light-on-umass-amherst-hampshire-college-partnership-discussions-28087521/">the </a><a href="https://gazettenet.com/2019/09/02/new-documents-shed-further-light-on-umass-amherst-hampshire-college-partnership-discussions-28087521/"><em>Daily Hampshire Gazette</em></a> offered more detail. In late 2018, before Nelson publicly announced the school’s search for a “partner,” she had entered negotiations with UMass Amherst to let them assume control of Hampshire’s facilities. UMass officials had requested that Nelson forego admitting a full class in 2019 in preparation to close the school permanently.</p>
<p>The backlash was swift: Students occupied Nelson’s office for seventy-five days, demanding transparency from the administration. A coalition of alumni and supporters, including prominent documentarian Ken Burns, banded together to raise emergency funds to keep the college afloat. By April, Nelson and key members of the board leadership stepped down. Ken Rosenthal became the interim president. Within a few hours of accepting the post, Rosenthal found himself at the Red Barn—an iconic building on Hampshire’s campus—telling the community that they would do what they could to reverse the damage. The college was going to remain open, but there would have to be cuts. Hampshire let approximately twenty-four staff members go in late April. The new administration worked with faculty to find creative ways to reduce the need for major cuts to the school’s faculty, representatives from Hampshire’s American Association of University Professors chapter told <em>Academe </em>in 2019. One of these involved agreements with Amherst, Smith, and Mount Holyoke to provide jobs to staff who weren’t ready to retire while paying them to continue to advise those Hampshire students they had previously been working with, Rosenthal recalled.</p>
<p>As miraculous as Rosenthal’s and others’ efforts to revive the college were, the school was never the same. “What they did was a stake to the heart of Hampshire College. And somehow miraculously the patient survived for seven years,” Alan Goodman, who taught biological anthropology at Hampshire for forty years and served as its dean of faculty, told me of Nelson. Rosenthal, Burns, and other alumni raised $55 million to support the college’s operations. By the time Ed Wingenbach, who took over as college president after Rosenthal’s tenure, left in 2025, the school’s total enrollment had grown by 60 percent since 2020. But as Goodman told me, the school was “incredibly weakened.” Hampshire’s financial pressures were never far away and operating deficits remained. In 2024, Hampshire cut some employee benefits and laid off 9 percent of its employees as part of a “restructure” that aimed to save the school approximately $2.7 million, according to <a href="https://www.highereddive.com/news/hampshire-college-staff-layoffs-9-percent-restructuring-edward-wingenbach/720351/"><em>Higher Ed Dive</em></a>.</p>
<p>Another issue loomed on the horizon: Hampshire’s millions in bond debt. A 2024 audit said the college had failed two covenants—or rules in a bond agreement that dictate how a company manages its finances with the bond—and declared it “could trigger a default.” The school tried, and failed, to refinance its debts and sell some of its land to raise funds.</p>
<p>Then, in April of this year, students, faculty, staff, and alumni received a notice from Jennifer Chrisler, the school’s current president, saying the board of trustees had voted to close the college. After 2019, the statement said, the school had decided to “maintain independence and remain true to Hampshire’s deepest-held values.” It nodded to the “herculean effort” of those who had tried to save the college in 2019 but said the school found itself “faced with the clear, heartbreaking reality” that its progress had “fallen far short of what we had hoped.” Unlike the miscommunications that had marred Hampshire’s prior near-death experience, financial records told a clear story. The school had been drawing on its endowment for years. The pool had shrunk close to 20 percent since I had graduated in 2011—and even more compared to its endowment’s high point a few years later. It was operating at a deficit, and its bondholders wanted their $21 million back.</p>
<p>When I arrived on campus for the school’s final commencement in May, what I found was sadness and frustration. Outside the library—named after one of Hampshire’s original financial backers, Harold Johnson—students had set up several poster boards with messages thanking “life changing professors.” A large, bright orange poster called the “rage board” that students had scribbled on outside one of the lecture halls took a slightly different stance. “I hate how much I love this college,” wrote one anonymous contributor in purple marker. “We could have [been] saved,” mourned another.</p>
<p>To honor Hampshire’s last official graduating class, graduates, faculty and former faculty, staff, parents, and alumni gathered on a warm, sunny morning on May 16 at the center of campus. In her address, Chrisler didn’t dwell on the school’s imminent closure. “Hampshire doesn’t end today. It disperses. It goes with you into every room you enter. Every problem you take on. Every question you refuse to stop asking,” she said. To her point, I had met alumni undertaking an anthology series to collect memories of the college and others leading an effort to protect the land from outside developers. Even if Hampshire wasn’t ending in a philosophical sense, it was in a literal one. Staff and professors would receive no severance pay. When I got back to my car, I found a flyer for <a href="https://www.helphampshireworkers.com/">an emergency relief fund</a> to support them.</p>
<p>The night before commencement, I stood among a crowd of four or five dozen students, staff, alumni, and community members gathered around the Hampshire library. After Davis Bates, the storyteller and Hampshire alum, said a few words, students who were preparing to graduate the next day came up to ring a bell hanging on the side of the library. It was a long-standing tradition of the college that students would do so after completing and passing their Division III.</p>

<p>As I glanced around to see if I recognized any faculty, one of the graduating students came up to me and gave me a hug. It was Shawna Itakura, a former New College of Florida student, with whom I had met a week or two earlier on Zoom. Itakura, who uses they/them pronouns, transferred to Hampshire from New College of Florida in 2023. As a state honors college, New College was a more affordable option for Florida residents interested in experimental education, but that made it susceptible to meddling from the state’s reactionary government. Governor Ron DeSantis overhauled the school’s leadership and board of trustees as <a href="https://inthesetimes.com/article/new-college-florida-republicans-higher-ed">part of a conservative takeover in 2023</a>. With DeSantis’ support, Christopher Rufo and other far-right activists took on key leadership positions at New College. They went to war with the school’s faculty, staff, and students whom they perceived as too ideological, too “woke”—and above all, too weird. Itakura described seeing zines, flags, and crafting equipment from a student-run center in a dumpster after the new administration came in. “I just started crying. I just couldn’t handle it,” they said.</p>
<p>Hampshire gave New College students an out, offering to match their tuition costs. That fall, Itakura and thirty-four other former New College students relocated to western Massachusetts. “It was a really beautiful show of solidarity,” Libby Harrity, another New College student and activist who transferred to Hampshire in 2023, told me. Harrity, who uses any pronouns, finished Hampshire in 2025, prior to news that the school intended to close. But they saw parallels in the two school’s experiences. “I think that what happened to Hampshire and what happened at New College are kind of the two fates of an institution that dates to be you openly and outright anti-authoritarian,” Harrity said. “It will be killed fast, intentionally, or starved slowly, and then, you know, have its grave pissed on.”</p>
<p>It’s difficult to really explain what the death of an institution like Hampshire means. There are, of course, the immediate and obvious effects: Staff and faculty lose their jobs, and current students are forced to go elsewhere. The Five College consortium, of which Hampshire was part, is now reduced to four. On a broader scale, as one <em>Inside Higher Ed </em>contributor noted, <a href="https://www.insidehighered.com/opinion/views/2026/05/13/what-hampshire-closing-means-progressive-ed-opinion">it marks the end of an era</a> where major donors no longer serve as a bulwark against persistent threats like declining enrollment, slim endowments, funding cuts, and economic turmoil. The road for these essential but niche institutions is narrow—and growing narrower.</p>
<p>Nearly three hundred colleges and universities have closed between 2008 and 2023, <a href="https://hechingerreport.org/college-closures/">according to the Hechinger Report</a>. Their loss is not the apocalypse. Higher education is faced by numerous, interlocking challenges. A lack of job security for professors, disputes over academic freedom, and debates over high education’s responsibility over students securing employment after graduation have reshaped the academy—and not in a good way. Inequality runs rampant. At Harvard University, where I attended graduate school, the endowment is $56.9 billion; Hampshire’s, when it closed, was $26.5 million.</p>
<p>The death of the “boomer utopia” mentality that <a href="https://nymag.com/intelligencer/article/hampshire-college-closing-boomer-utopia.html">some have attributed to Hampshire</a> was, perhaps, inevitable. The notion that one can do what one loves, and the rest will follow, is a nice fantasy but an unsustainable one in an era of growing inequality and crass technological “advancement.” As impossible as it may be, though, optimism is necessary. “I mean, what is a utopia?” Margaret Cerullo, a sociology professor from Hampshire, said to me. “It’s a place where you can imagine that the world could be different.”</p>
