<p>Today, as we continue digesting the story of those four unprepared hikers who were recently rescued from New Hampshire’s Franconia Ridge—and billed for the cost of their rescue—I’d like to introduce you to a special guest; a former co-worker of mine whose recent work has involved being on the frontlines of the search-and-rescue that we’ve been seeing in backcountry hiking destinations. <strong>Patrick Scanlan</strong>, who lives in the White Mountain village of Intervale, New Hampshire, is an avalanche forecaster, a search-and-rescue leader, a certified ski guide, and owns <a href="https://gravitystrategy.co/">Gravity Strategy</a>, a design consultancy working with tech companies creating products meant to get more people outside.</p>
<p>Back in June, after another headline-making rescue in the White Mountains, Pat wrote a tremendous essay for his newsletter—<a href="https://designonaridgeline.substack.com/p/the-illusion-of-being-looked-after">Design on a Ridgeline</a>—which challenged the conventional narrative of backcountry rescues involving underprepared hikers. It was the type of thoughtful analysis that’s so often missing from discourse on search-and-rescues, from someone with deep experience in the field, and my immediate impulse was to share it with my network. Then, it occurred to me that a followup chat with Pat would be natural territory for Mind The Moss and potentially enlightening for a lot of people who are coming into this from a more observational place. SO, I’ll let Pat take it from here, in this reproduction of a conversation that the two of us had last Friday.</p>
<p><em>Pat, we just saw another high-profile rescue go down in the White Mountains; a rescue involving hikers who were really unprepared, and are now going to be billed by the state for the cost of their rescue. This has sparked a lot of debate online. Thinking about the surge of hiker emergencies and rescues, to which this most recent incident belongs, I’m curious how you—a rescuer—feel about the way we often talk about these incidents?<br/>
<br/>
</em>Whenever I click on the comments section for a story or a post about an emergency and a rescue in the backcountry, I tend to get pretty frustrated by what I see: A lot of highly opinionated, armchair quarterbacks who think that they have the full story of the incident; a more developed knowledge set than everyone else in the comments.</p>
<p>There’s usually a lot of victim blaming happening, with the person at the center of the incident. It can get pretty derogatory. But I think the reality is that anybody who has been an outdoor enthusiast has—at some point in their lives—made a decision that was not good. It either created an emergency, or it was a close call that they got away with. I can remember plenty of times, especially when I was younger, where I either got injured or I had a really close brush. And while those incidents became less frequent with age and experience, they’ve never gone away entirely. The outdoors is an uncontrolled environment, there’s always going to be risk, and the incidents that lead to emergencies and rescues are often more complex than people might assume.</p>
<p>And, the billing conversation sits on top of this. I understand why the state does it, and I won’t pretend the debate isn’t real. But look at the imbalance of the problem. New Hampshire can bill a hiker for being ‘unprepared’ but nobody can bill an app that provided the information or shaped the decision making. We’ve built a chain where the only person carrying any liability is the one who probably had the least amount of information going into that activity.</p>

<p><em>Indeed, and one of the things that jumped out at me when I read your essay is the way that you identified the information stream coming from trail apps as a key force behind this escalation of backcountry accidents and rescues. A lot of online commentators point to this trend and talk about personal responsibility, but you described this ongoing wave of hiker emergencies and search-and-rescues as a design problem. Please explain that.<br/>
<br/>
</em>Let’s say you’re going to a new place and you’re looking to do some hiking. You’re not sure what types of trails are available, how hard they are, or what kind of gear you’re going to need. 10 years ago, I think the most common way of finding answers was a combination of print or digitized guidebooks with lots of in-depth information, and maybe some online blogs or forums with great trip reports from other hikers. I think the key here was it took some effort to go and do this research. The information was not right at your fingertips. I think this drew a type of person who was maybe a little bit more prepared; because they had to put in the extra work with the hiking research.</p>
<p>Now, fast forward to today or the last few years, and we have a plethora of these apps for hike planning; apps that distilled a lot of that information that used to come from guidebooks or blogs. I use some of these apps myself, and a lot of them are fantastic. If you allow for location sharing on your phone, you can search within a certain radius of where you are and an app will automatically populate the map with trails that are right there. So instead of going down this rabbit hole of information, in figuring out where you want to hike, you’ve now got a condensed version placed in front of you.</p>
<p>In a lot of ways, this is really awesome! It’s contributing to greater accessibility in the outdoors, and it’s making it easier for people to find outdoor activities that are great for them. I think the flipside is that we don't always know where the information being put in front of us is coming from, or what kind of information is being uplifted. These apps are built for engagement. Millions of dollars have been poured into a design and an interface to encourage user interaction. These apps are optimized for you to find something quickly, and then to go out and do that thing. What they're <em>not </em>optimized for quite yet is information quality, and also transparency with information sources.</p>
<p>For example, one of the design choices in several of these outdoor apps is that they crowdsource GPS data in order to populate their maps with routes that are popular. Then they put that crowdsourced information in front of people and essentially say, “Hey, this is a really popular trail!” These are often real trails that are perfectly fine for hiking. But in other situations, that crowdsourced data might produce trail lines on a map that run across private land. Or it might misadvertise trails that are actually closed—or trails that are more like bushwhacking tracks—as viable routes for a hike.</p>
<p><em>There’s also a particularly dangerous problem that you raised in your recent post about rescues in New Hampshire. A lot of these apps are built to encourage finding cool stuff and doing hard things in the outdoors. But they don’t really encourage turning around.<br/>
<br/>
</em>Discovery is something that these apps do exceptionally well, and whenever someone chooses an option for a nearby hike, they’re basically making a decision based on an estimation of whether they’re able to complete this trail. This is often based on a trail difficulty scale of Easy, Moderate, or Hard. The definition of what each of those things looks and feels like is highly subjective. If I’ve spent a ton of time hiking in the White Mountains, your definition of “Hard” is going to be shaped by that. In describing trails, the apps are using definitions of Easy, Moderate, and Hard that a lot of users won’t be able to relate to experientially, and some folks will find themselves in over their heads.</p>
<p>What makes this even more of an issue is that it’s not necessarily in the best business interest of the apps to display more nuanced, in-depth safety information that hikers who are struggling might use—for gauging whether it makes sense to keep going or to turn around. Because there’s often a negative connotation with turning around. In outdoor sports culture, setting out and completing a big task is often presented in a much more positive light than bailing; even in the face of terrible weather, equipment failure, or health concerns. And I wonder if the apps are reinforcing this cultural norm.</p>
<p>If you start climbing a mountain and things aren’t going well, the right decision is to turn around and come back some other time. I think we’ve yet to see a feature in an app that rewards that type of decision-making. Instead, the question always seems to be, “Did you complete the hike?” This can be a really dangerous oversimplification of mountain environments, if these apps are presenting more users with objectives that increase in difficulty level and exposure. The mindset that if you weren’t successful if you didn’t complete something needs to go away, and that’s also a design problem.</p>

<p><em>One of the things that makes this problem tougher to figure out right now is that we’re still trying to decide who should own the problem. The hikers who are using the apps? The tech companies that are designing them? The authorities who are responding to a changing outdoor rec landscape? A combination of all the parties? What do you think?<br/>
<br/>
</em>I think there's some rescuers who’ve been doing this a long time, before the big wave of consumer mobile technology, would say that the ownership is on the person who's doing the activity. And a part of me wants to agree with that. I do feel that people need to understand the risks of what they're doing, and to take some responsibility for their own safety. But this also feels like ignoring the current situation in the backcountry, and online. We shouldn’t underestimate how powerful it can be when a person sees friends or influencers who they might follow on social media engaging in these big outdoor activities. A video shot on a mountain on a beautiful day might get someone downloading a trail app, forming an objective, and setting off expecting the same thing; whereas the next day, the conditions on the mountain could be very different.</p>
<p>As bad as this sounds, I’m not a tech doomerist. I'm actually very much an optimist when it comes to the technology that we’ve been building around this. One of the most interesting programs that I've seen from a few different trail app companies is a public lands liaison program. This is basically a connection between a land manager, like the National Park Service, with an app that's surfacing trails to users in and around that National Park. What this allows for is a feedback loop of trail information and data quality, looping back to someone local who’s a subject expert. This would allow the land manager to go to the app company and say, “No, that isn't right. This trail description language needs to be changed to this. That trail line should be removed because it goes through an area that’s super dangerous and shouldn’t be listed at all.”</p>
<p>I think this approach has a lot of potential. The only issue is that it's hard to scale. It requires engagement from more people. And, you know, when it comes to staffing and resources, U.S. public land managers are not having the best time these days. A lot of them may not have the bandwidth to actively track all of the information that's being populated on these apps. That said, the early signal is real. Olympic National Park reported a 66% decrease in search and rescue incidents after partnering with one of these apps to get accurate conditions and closures in front of their users. This is one data point, but it is early evidence that better information design changes outcomes.</p>

<p><em>When you look to the horizon, contemplating this issue, its complicated layers, and the lack of obvious, actionable solutions, is there anything that you feel empowered to do?<br/>
<br/>
</em>Usually, over time, data quality gets better. I think the problems with trail information and data being surfaced in apps will get better in the long term. But in the meantime, we’ve still got a really serious issue that’s contributing to more accidents and rescues in the backcountry. I think we have to ask whether waiting for data quality to improve is enough—whether we need to consider more interventions that can happen now.</p>
<p>I recently published a guideline for apps that guide people into remote terrain; I’m calling it the <a href="https://outdoorriskcalibration.org/1.0/">Outdoor Risk Calibration Guidelines</a>. With it, stakeholders can assess their product against several types of criteria, and also have some guidance for addressing the problems we’re seeing. The broader idea here is to create a decentralized, open set of best practices like the web content accessibility guidelines that were introduced in late 90s and early 2000s, when more people were starting to realize how important web accessibility was going to be, as the Internet became a bigger part of everyday life.</p>
<p>I explored similar concepts in the spec guidelines that I published; not just for trail app companies, but also land managers and policymakers. It’s an open source project, so anyone can go in, access the source code, make edits, and add recommendations. My hope is that if enough people start hearing about this, looking at it, and working with it, then maybe we can collectively develop firmer best practices for trail apps or information systems that are putting people into situations that could be dangerous.</p>
