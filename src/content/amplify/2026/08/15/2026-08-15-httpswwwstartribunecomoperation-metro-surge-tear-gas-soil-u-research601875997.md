<p>The study aims to determine whether chemical munitions used by federal agents left contaminants in neighborhood gardens and soil.</p>

<p>Lars Lidahl drove a metal soil sampling probe into the ground of a south Minneapolis garden on a hot August morning, just a couple of blocks from where <a href="https://www.startribune.com/renee-good-found-refuge-in-minneapolis-then-ice-arrived/601560595">Renee Good</a> was shot and killed by a federal agent last winter.</p>
<p>Lidahl and a group of fellow University of Minnesota students were wrapping up their field work for a unique and timely project: studying the environmental impacts of federal agents’ tear gas use during <a href="https://www.startribune.com/reckoning-retribution-and-resistance-the-behind-the-scenes-story-of-operation-metro-surge/601583420">Operation Metro Surge</a> by sampling dirt and snow from numerous locations across the city.</p>
<p>“As we’re getting the data back, it’s kind of wild to see,” said Lidahl, a geography major. “Like seriously? 1,300 parts per million of lead?”</p>
<p>The average Minnesota natural soil lead amount is between 16 and 17 parts per million, according to a United States Geological Survey <a href="https://www.epa.gov/superfund/usgs-background-soil-lead-survey-state-data?utm_source=chatgpt.com">study</a>.</p>
<p>After thousands of federal immigration agents flooded into Minnesota last winter, many of the faceoffs between protesters and agents resulted in the officers <a href="https://www.startribune.com/federal-agents-are-deploying-clouds-streams-and-spatters-of-chemical-irritants-what-are-their-health-consequences/601568140">firing chemical weapons such as tear gas or smoke grenades.</a> Some turned the snow bright orange or green in parks and along sidewalks.</p>
<p>Rebecca Walker, a professor in the geography, environment and society department who is co-leading the project, felt compelled as a south Minneapolis resident to help the surrounding neighborhoods understand what level of harm these chemical weapons may have had on homegrown produce and lawns.</p>
<p>To do this, researchers created a form in which residents could offer their garden to be tested if they worried it was contaminated.</p>
<p>The students took samples from 72 yards and from nine snow sites near where agents used tear gas such as near the fatal shootings of Good on Portland Avenue S. and <a href="https://www.startribune.com/alex-pretti-identified-as-man-fatally-shot-by-federal-officers-in-minneapolis/601570109">Alex Pretti</a> on Nicollet Avenue S., not far away.</p>

<p>They’ve already confirmed there were elevated levels of lead and chromium in the snow where tear gas was used, but are still working on analyzing the dirt. Work on the project has been done by a large group of students, professors, researchers and neighborhood nonprofits.</p>

<h2>Assisting neighbors</h2>
<p>After finishing soil sampling next week, the team’s next step is to inform participating households of how much and what kind of heavy metals were found in their yards.</p>
<p>Some metals such as lead can already be found in urban gardens without the presence of tear gas. But the findings will be useful to check for a possible correlation between tear gas sites and the yards nearby having elevated levels of chemicals, Walker said.</p>
<p>The idea for the project started when Lea Pollack, an environmental contaminants researcher, reached out to Walker in January as agents descended upon the Twin Cities. From Walker’s house a few blocks from the Good shooting site, she could smell the off-putting odor of tear gas drifting by.</p>
<p>Walker hopes that the study can alleviate some of the fears by helping residents know what risks there could be and how to ensure their gardens are safe to use.</p>
<p>“I think that people just have a lot of questions, and I think [the operation] inspired a lot of fear,” she said. “For a lot of people who experienced like the brunt of the tear gas this winter, I mean, it felt like the whole point was fear.”</p>

<p>Asked about the study and the chemicals used, an Immigration and Customs Enforcement spokesperson did not specify what was in the irritants. The agency questioned the legitimacy of environmental concerns.</p>
<p>“Let’s be honest about what is happening,” the spokesperson said. “This isn’t about the environment. It’s about trying to stop President Trump from making America safe.”</p>
<p>The spokesperson added that agents are “trained to use the minimum amount of force necessary to resolve dangerous situations, prioritizing the safety of both the public and our officers.”</p>
<p>Part of the need for the study is there’s “almost no research” about the question of how harmful tear gas is on soil, Walker said. Even knowing the full list of chemicals that are in the various chemical weapons is difficult to know as the government has not been transparent about it, and the ingredients can be proprietary information for the manufacturers.</p>
<p>As Lidahl helped collect samples in a garden last week, he pushed back against the government’s argument that the winter’s operation was solely about law enforcement.</p>
<p>“Somebody’s got to be out here documenting the impact to community … you brought a pile of nasty stuff into gardens that people are trying to use to raise their families,” he said.</p>

<h2>Early findings</h2>
<p>The snow samples from the winter showed elevated levels of lead and chromium in particular around major tear gas use sites, Walker said. That includes near the site where Pretti was shot and killed, and outside the Whipple federal building where protests lasted for weeks.</p>
<p>Because the metallic particles are heavy, “there’s good reason” to think if the chemicals got onto the snow, it could now be in the soil underneath where it was deployed, she added.</p>
<p>Lidahl said on Tuesday they’re not ready yet to make any sweeping conclusions about the soil and that there has been a wide range , with some garden beds having very low metal levels and others being high.</p>
<p>The Minnesota Pollution Control Agency said in a statement that so far the university study’s results are “very concerning” and that the agency “hopes to learn more” as the researchers complete the project.</p>
<p>The researchers have also compiled a list of best gardening practices they suggest for after tear gas is used nearby. Those include:</p>
<ul data-testid="bulleted-list-unordered"><li>As with all urban gardening, wash vegetables thoroughly to remove harmful chemicals.</li><li>Garden in raised beds by adding one to two feet of new soil to dilute contaminants.</li><li>Add thick layers of mulch around the garden.</li><li>Add garden compost to reduce the chance contaminants spread.</li></ul>
<p>One challenge for researchers, Walker said, is that some tear gas chemicals aside from heavy metals are more expensive to test for, such as hexachloroethane in dangerous HC smoke, which requires more advanced technology. It’s unclear whether HC smoke was used in the Twin Cities.</p>

<p>Juniper Simonis, an environmental biologist in Oregon who studied tear gas and smoke used during Portland’s Black Lives Matter protests in 2020 and 2021, stressed that some munitions used in Minneapolis like pepper balls and canisters stay harmful long after they are used.</p>
<p>She also referenced a previous study by Forensic Architecture, a research agency in London that created a simulation of how far CS tear gas would drift through the air after Portland police used it during protests on June 2, 2020. The simulation showed the particles would have been carried more than half a mile across the city on that day.</p>
<p>Simonis added that she thinks the Minneapolis study is an important step towards addressing fears that residents may have.</p>
<p>“There’s so much that, unfortunately, there hasn’t been a lot of rigorous work on in terms of what happens to these chemical mixtures afterwards,” she said.</p>
