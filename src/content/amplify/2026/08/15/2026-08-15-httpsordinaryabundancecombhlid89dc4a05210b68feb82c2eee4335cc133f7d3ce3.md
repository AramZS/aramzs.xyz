<p>It’s eight o’clock. The apartment is quiet, aside from the music playing softly over the speaker. It’s a playlist your friend made for you years ago. You send them a text to check in, then pick up a book, flick on the lamp, and settle into the chair to read.</p>
<blockquote>If we could have devised an arrangement for providing everybody with music in their homes, perfect in quality, unlimited in quantity, suited to every mood, and beginning and ceasing at will, we should have considered the limit of human felicity already attained.</blockquote>
<p><a href="https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/624">Edward Bellamy, 1888</a> — From Looking Backward, his utopian novel set in the year 2000. Before recordings, listening to music meant having instruments or performers nearby, and favorite songs could go unheard for years at a time.</p>
<blockquote>The people, almost with bated breath, stood overwhelmed with awe, as if in the presence of the supernatural. The strange, weird light, exceeded in power only by the sun, yet mild as moonlight, rendered the Court House square as light as midday.</blockquote>
<p><a href="https://archive.org/details/historyofwabashc01wees">A witness at Wabash, Indiana, 1880</a> — The night Wabash, Indiana became one of the first cities lit entirely by electric light. Candles, oil lamps, and gas jets gave relatively feeble light, and were all smoky open fires inside the home.</p>
<blockquote>A museum without walls has been opened to us, and it will carry infinitely farther that limited revelation of the world of art which the real museums offer us within their walls.</blockquote>
<p><a href="https://monoskop.org/images/0/0a/Malraux_Andre_1947_1974_Museum_Without_Walls.pdf">André Malraux, 1947</a> — Le Musée imaginaire, later translated as Museum Without Walls. Before reliable reproduction, most people saw major artworks only by visiting churches, collections, or museums.</p>
<blockquote>The very shadow of the person lying there fixed for ever! ... I would rather have such a memorial of one I dearly loved, than the noblest artist's work ever produced.</blockquote>
<p><a href="https://archive.org/details/lettersofelizabe0001brow">Elizabeth Barrett Browning, 1843</a> — On first seeing a daguerreotype, in a letter to Mary Russell Mitford. Preserving a likeness used to require a painted portrait; many families had no accurate image of relatives who died.</p>
<blockquote>It is not twenty years since there was discovered the art of making spectacles ... one of the best and most necessary in the world. I myself saw the man who discovered it, and I talked with him.</blockquote>
<p><a href="https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_peIL7hVQUmwC">Friar Giordano da Pisa, 1306</a> — A sermon at Santa Maria Novella, Florence. Age-related vision loss was common, and before spectacles it could end reading and other close work.</p>
<blockquote>Of all the marvellous achievements of modern science, the Electric Telegraph is transcendently the greatest and most serviceable to mankind. It is a perpetual miracle, which no familiarity can render commonplace.</blockquote>
<p><a href="https://archive.org/details/storyoftelegraph01brig">Briggs &amp; Maverick, 1858</a> — The Story of the Telegraph. For most of history, messages traveled only as fast as the person who carried them. Receiving a reply from far away could take weeks or months.</p>
<p><a href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/51034/51034-h/51034-h.htm">Jakob Wimpfeling, 1505</a> — The German humanist, on the new art of printing. A hand-copied book was valuable enough that libraries chained theirs to the reading desks.</p>
<p>It’s nine. A chapter ends, and you stretch and wander into the kitchen. You’re not exactly sure what you want, so you aimlessly rifle through your fruit bowl, open and close the door to the fridge, and settle on a cup of tea. You lean against the counter and let your mind wander as the water comes to a boil.</p>
<blockquote>Water! Water! is the universal note which is sounded through every part of the city, and infuses joy and exultation into the masses.</blockquote>
<p><a href="https://archive.org/details/diaryofphiliphon02honeuoft">Philip Hone, 1842</a> — On the arrival of Croton water in New York City, in his diary. Manhattan’s wells shared soil with its privies and burial grounds; the cholera epidemic ten years earlier had killed 3,515 New Yorkers.</p>
<blockquote>Like lovers' kisses, she biteth — she is a pleasure bordering on pain from the fierceness and insanity of her relish.</blockquote>
<p><a href="https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/10343">Charles Lamb, 1822</a> — On the rapture of the pineapple. In eighteenth-century Britain, a pineapple was costly enough that hostesses sometimes rented one for the evening rather than eat it.</p>
<blockquote>The first transport of ice from the shores of the United States to the banks of the Ganges is an event of no mean importance ... the names of those who planned and have successfully carried through their adventure at their own cost, deserve to be handed down to posterity with the name of other benefactors of mankind.</blockquote>
<p><a href="https://books.google.com/books/about/Refrigeration_Nation.html?id=_V2-AAAAQBAJ">The Calcutta Courier, 1837</a> — On the arrival of New England pond-ice in tropical India. Without cold storage, perishable food like dairy and meat spoiled within a day, and most foods could not be kept or eaten out of season.</p>
<blockquote>It is said that these things come from the earthly paradise; for the wind blows down the trees in paradise, just as the wind blows down the dry wood in the forests of our own land.</blockquote>
<p><a href="https://archive.org/details/memoirsofcrusade0000vill_j9w1">Jean de Joinville, c. 1309</a> — On merchants who netted ginger, cinnamon and rhubarb from the Nile. In medieval price lists, a pound of ginger sold for the price of a sheep; a pound of saffron, the price of a horse.</p>
<p>At ten, the night starts to wind down. You wash your face, then wash down a pill with a glass of water. As you brush your teeth, you make a mental note of the last few chores you need to cross off the list tomorrow morning before heading to the airport.</p>
<blockquote>You have erased from the calendar of human afflictions one of its greatest. ... Future nations will know by history only that the loathsome smallpox has existed, and by you has been extirpated.</blockquote>
<p><a href="https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Jefferson/99-01-02-3718">Thomas Jefferson, 1806</a> — In a letter celebrating Edward Jenner’s vaccine, sent to Jenner’s nephew. Smallpox killed about three of every ten people it infected, and in Jenner’s century it killed an estimated four hundred thousand Europeans a year.</p>
<blockquote>Before whom, in all time, surgery was agony; by whom pain in surgery was averted and annulled; since whom, science has controlled pain.</blockquote>
<p><a href="https://archive.org/details/historicalmemora00bige">Epitaph of W. T. G. Morton, 1871</a> — Morton first publicly demonstrated ether anaesthesia. Before anesthesia, operations were performed with the patient conscious, and the primary means of managing pain were speed, alcohol, and opium.</p>
<blockquote>It just felt like I was the wealthiest person in the world. It felt great not to have to go outside to go to the restroom.</blockquote>
<p><a href="https://www.iowapbs.org/iowapathways/artifact/1535/electricity-and-plumbing-change-rural-farm-life-early-20th-century">Patty Doak, 2003</a> — Recalling her family's first indoor bathroom, rural Iowa. Well into the twentieth century, most rural American homes had no indoor bathroom, and every gallon of water for cooking, washing, and bathing was carried in by hand.</p>
<blockquote>I think it has done more to emancipate women than anything else in the world. I stand and rejoice every time I see a woman ride by on a wheel.</blockquote>
<p><a href="https://ignoranceisblixt.com/2020/08/champion-of-her-sex-nellie-bly-interviews-susan-b-anthony/">Susan B. Anthony, 1896</a> — Interviewed by Nellie Bly for the New York World. The safety bicycle opened up new possibilities for independent travel, especially for women under restrictive social rules.</p>
<blockquote>Come, Muse, and sing the dreaded Washing-Day. Ye who beneath the yoke of wedlock bend, With bowed soul, full well ye ken the day</blockquote>
<p><a href="https://archive.org/details/sim_monthly-magazine_1797-12_4_25">Anna Laetitia Barbauld, 1797</a> — From her poem 'Washing-Day.' A household wash meant hauling and heating water by the barrel, then scrubbing, rinsing, and wringing every piece by hand; it filled an entire day each week.</p>
<blockquote>What philanthropy failed to accomplish, what religion, poetry, eloquence, and reason had sought in vain, has been produced by — the Sewing Machine.</blockquote>
<p><a href="https://archive.org/details/godeys-1860-v-61/page/77/mode/2up">Godey's Lady's Book, 1860</a> — From 'The Queen of Inventions,' in the era's leading women's magazine. Before the machine, most people owned only two or three outfits, and a single new shirt meant some fourteen hours of hand stitching.</p>
<blockquote>It is so cold that the freezing of the ink on the point of my pen renders it difficult to write. We have had the thermometer at 12°.</blockquote>
<p><a href="https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Jefferson/01-29-02-0169">Thomas Jefferson, 1796</a> — In a letter to his son-in-law, the ink freezing as he wrote. Bedrooms in winter often fell close to the temperature outside, and it was not unusual to wake up to a frozen washbasin.</p>
<blockquote>I sometimes think that the desire to fly after the fashion of birds is an ideal handed down to us by our ancestors who, in their grueling travels across trackless lands in prehistoric times, looked enviously on the birds soaring freely through space, at full speed, above all obstacles, on the infinite highway of the air.</blockquote>
<p><a href="https://catalog.hathitrust.org/Record/001114607">Wilbur Wright, 1908</a> — Remarks at a banquet of the Aéro-Club de France, Paris. Before flight, crossing an ocean took a week or more at sea, and emigrating across one often meant never seeing your family again.</p>
<p>At eleven, you switch off the lights, silence your phone, and lie down to sleep. As your head hits the pillow, you idly wonder what people slept on before mattresses.</p>
<p>All the items in this room were once out of reach; some not yet invented, others too rare or costly for the vast majority of people. Today, most of us lucky enough to live with them walk past without a second thought.</p>
<p>It is to humanity’s credit that we remain restless in the midst of all of this progress. We continue to look forward, pushing the frontier further with new treatments, new tools, and new institutions that will help future generations in ways we can’t even picture yet.</p>
<p>But our lives today are a gallery of past generations’ heroic efforts to do the same. It serves us, and honors them, to recapture whenever possible the old sense of awe at these wonders that have long since become commonplace.</p>
