<p>On the first floor of a nondescript building on an industrial estate near Rainham in Essex, there is a unique collection of curios. A sperm whale’s tooth from Fiji; a Williamson P14 camera from the 1933 Everest mapping expedition; William Howard Russell’s diary from the Crimean War, its handwriting so neat it looks like newsprint.</p>

<p>This is the Times and Sunday Times archive, home to the physical records of both papers (the sperm whale tooth was a gift). On stacks that stretch into every corner are eight million newspaper cuttings filed in 6,700 boxes (snipped from both papers, and News UK’s other current and historic titles).</p>

<p>There are also a mind-boggling number of photographs — 50 million negatives and 25 million prints. These pictures tell the stories of our times: of wars and prime ministers, royal babies, FA Cups and Olympic records. They also immortalise quieter, quirkier moments: morris dancers outside a Kent country pub in 1953; a 1958 strike picket at a bus depot in Catford, south London; the residents of Southfield Square in Bradford in 1976, standing united in the face of proposed demolition plans.</p>

<p>For Chris Youngs, 32, the opportunity was obvious. Youngs joined the Times and Sunday Times puzzles team last year after six years on the picture desk, so he “knew the kind of rich imagery we have”, he says — and as geoguessing games, in which players try to pinpoint the exact location of a scene, had become “super popular” he sought to “combine the two”.</p>
<p>In April, he did just that with <a href="https://www.thetimes.com/puzzles/quizzes-and-teasers/picle">Picle</a>.</p>
<p>There are now 50,000 Picle players on our website every day, with about 2,000 joining weekly. The rules are simple: players examine an archival picture and guess what year and where it was taken, zooming in on a map to identify the location as accurately as possible.</p>
<p>You are scored out of 100 — 50 points maximum for the location, 50 for the year — and given a ranking.</p>
<p>I scored 98 on a 1977 picture outside Woolworths on Oxford Street (I was one year off and a mere 347 yards out).</p>

<p>The game’s alchemy is its combination of knowledge and wits. Newshounds and amateur historians delight in mining a picture for clues — for the Woolworths photo, a Transport for London logo on a bus stop gave away the city (I originally missed the black cab in the foreground).</p>
<p>When developing the game, Youngs asked Michael-John Jennings, the meticulous News UK archive specialist, to select photos with distinguishing features such as “cars, architecture, fashion, road signs, anything someone could use to figure out where and when the image was taken.”</p>

<p>Newer pictures work — I got 96 for identifying England fans in Berlin at Euro 2024 — but Youngs thinks older ones are more magical. “It’s looking back in time,” he says.</p>
<p>Not every great picture makes a great Picle. Something too obvious is no fun and some are “too obscure”, Youngs admits. Mostly, it’s UK images for now. Sometimes a Picle stumps players; Youngs recalls one featuring an elderly woman near a tree stump. “Loads of people had no idea,” he says. But taking a stab in the dark is “part of the game”.</p>

<p>I try to pinpoint the location first, then make an educated guess at the date — also Youngs’s approach. Other tips are to really comb a scene, from the font on a shop sign to the look of a street lamp, and to pay attention to hemlines, shoes and trouser legs (the wider 1970s cut is unmistakeable). If you’re still stuck, have a punt. “If it is a really hard one, it is just guessing anyway,” Youngs says.</p>
<p>The Picle community is a lively one. A dedicated comments section is coming — at the moment people chat in the <a href="https://www.thetimes.com/topic/times-daily-quiz">Times Daily quiz</a>’s comment section, often exchanging location scores. “I see loads of comments saying ‘only three yards out today’ or ‘56 miles today’,” Youngs says.</p>
<p>On a recent toughie, some readers revealed they’d googled a car to try to date the photo. I am aghast — surely this is cheating? “I think if you enjoy the game and enjoy playing it that way, then it’s fine,” Youngs responds.</p>

<p>Sometimes, a picture is personal. Helen Francis contacted Times Radio to say she had spotted her late mother in a Picle picture. Francis’s mother had dementia and died in 2019, but in the image she was “52, walking through Chester, smiling”, her daughter said. “I was looking at my mum as she once was, healthy and full of life.”</p>

<p>Of course, readers keep the puzzles team on their toes. On May 13, the solution placed Dublin 502 miles away, in Frisia on the Dutch-German border. Such errors set up a meta-game, Youngs says. “People write, ‘I think it was actually on this street corner. And these are the reasons why.’ They enjoy the fact that they can correct us.”</p>
<p>Devotees will be reassured that the vast collection of archive pictures means the game can run and run. “It’s a game only we can do,” Youngs says. “That archive — that’s unique to us, that history and that heritage.”</p>
