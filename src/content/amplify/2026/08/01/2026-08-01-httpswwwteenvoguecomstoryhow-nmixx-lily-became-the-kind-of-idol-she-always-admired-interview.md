<p>In the days following the release of <a href="https://www.teenvogue.com/story/nmixx-snapshot-style-interview-bst-hyde-park-2024">NMIXX</a>'s fifth EP, <em>Heavy Serenade</em>, the group's 23-year-old pop polymath Lily Morrow found herself scrolling through edits set to "LOUD," a self-penned track that quickly became a <a data-event-boundary="click" data-event-click='{"pattern":"ExternalLink"}' data-in-view='{"pattern":"ExternalLink"}' data-include-experiments="true" data-offer-url="https://www.tiktok.com/@dreaminggrl/video/7643303147070344468" href="https://www.tiktok.com/@dreaminggrl/video/7643303147070344468">favorite</a> among fans.</p>
<p>The song is a soaring, synthy declaration of love, and, as Lily describes it, the lyrics flowed out of her and onto paper with surprising ease. Its message is romantic but expansive, capturing the exhilaration of wanting to love someone openly while leaving enough room for listeners to <a data-event-boundary="click" data-event-click='{"pattern":"ExternalLink"}' data-in-view='{"pattern":"ExternalLink"}' data-include-experiments="true" data-offer-url="https://www.tiktok.com/@waltzerrrr/video/7641452231060917517" href="https://www.tiktok.com/@waltzerrrr/video/7641452231060917517">project their own experiences</a> onto it. Before long, the song broke containment, taking on a life of its own. Fans carried it into other corners of the internet, pairing it with <a data-event-boundary="click" data-event-click='{"pattern":"ExternalLink"}' data-in-view='{"pattern":"ExternalLink"}' data-include-experiments="true" data-offer-url="https://www.tiktok.com/@iiartmisii/video/7639122347001154837" href="https://www.tiktok.com/@iiartmisii/video/7639122347001154837">fictional couples</a>, <a data-event-boundary="click" data-event-click='{"pattern":"ExternalLink"}' data-in-view='{"pattern":"ExternalLink"}' data-include-experiments="true" data-offer-url="https://www.tiktok.com/@kyosite/video/7648318344772209938" href="https://www.tiktok.com/@kyosite/video/7648318344772209938">beloved characters</a>, <a data-event-boundary="click" data-event-click='{"pattern":"ExternalLink"}' data-in-view='{"pattern":"ExternalLink"}' data-include-experiments="true" data-offer-url="https://www.tiktok.com/@nsixxsevenn/video/7640008979707350273" href="https://www.tiktok.com/@nsixxsevenn/video/7640008979707350273">Tumblr-era moodboards</a>, and love stories far removed from NMIXX itself. For a fangirl like Lily, the experience was unexpectedly emotional. "Seeing your own lyrics being interpreted in a fandom space," she says over a video call from Seoul, "that really meant a lot to me."</p>
<p>Many of the most popular edits centered on queer love, suggesting that listeners had found something familiar in the song's yearning insistence on loving without apology.</p>
<p>Lily isn't surprised by the variety of interpretations. "In a perfect world, everyone can be free to love however they want, whoever they want, loudly," she says. "But unfortunately, that's not always possible. I wanted to help people going through that."</p>
<p>It's exactly the kind of connection she believes music can create. "It's always been my biggest dream to make music that people can really resonate with in their souls," she says. "Because that's why I love music so much. I love listening to a song, really connecting with it, and feeling it lift me up. And I've always wanted to be an artist who can do that for other people."</p>
<p>Lily takes her job as an idol seriously. Not just the performing part, though she does that too, with the kind of vocal control and stage presence expected from a virtuosic group like NMIXX, now well into their fourth year together. She's a self-diagnosed perfectionist, after all. But Lily cares just as much about the quieter parts. You can see it in the hours she spends discussing books with fans online, in the emotional honesty she brings to songwriting, and in the way she speaks about music like it’s something capable of genuinely changing people. She understands that being an idol now requires more than just performing well. Fans want sincerity. They want someone who feels human on the other side of the screen.</p>
<p>And that's where Lily shines. Talking to <em>Teen Vogue</em>, Lily returns to the same idea in different forms. She talks about songwriting as journaling. She describes fandom as a relationship built on mutual respect. She lights up when discussing books, music, movies, and the artists who inspire her. At one point, searching for an explanation for why she started a book club with fans in the first place, she lands on perhaps the simplest answer of all: "I really just love talking about things that I love." It's a surprisingly effective mission statement.</p>
<p>There's very little distance between Lily the idol and Lily the fan. Even now, in the middle of album promotions in South Korea, Lily is still making time for the things she loves. Books travel with her between schedules. She reads backstage at music shows instead of scrolling on her phone, noting that she's already finished 17 books this year. "Last year was more about the quantity," she says with a laugh. "This year is about the quality." Her favorite author is Taylor Jenkins Reid. Her other favorite Taylor is Swift. On the topic of the "The Fate of Ophelia" singer, she struggles to name her favorite album because it tends to change depending on the day or the weather. "I recently did one of those Taylor Swift album ranking websites," she shares, her voice colored by an unmistakable Australian lilt. "And as I expected, <em>1989</em> and <em>Speak Now</em> were top marks. But I can't..." She pauses. "I don't know. It's just too hard."</p>
<p>After a few more moments of deliberation, she lands on a tentative answer.</p>
<p>"I'd probably have to say <em>Speak Now</em>."</p>
<p>The decision lasts all of a few seconds.</p>
<p>“But I really, really love <em>Reputation</em> too.”</p>
<p>When the conversation turns to what she's listening to right now, the response arrives as another stream of enthusiasm. Lily has been spending a lot of time with Noah Kahan's latest album, revisiting Phoebe Bridgers, and "keeping an eye" on Slayyyter. "It's really a happy time to be alive as a music fan," she says, breaking into a grin. "I'm just living my best life."</p>

<p>The joy is infectious. More importantly, it's something Lily seems genuinely eager to share. Which is the basis for "Lily's Lost the Plot," her ongoing book-and-film club.</p>
<p>Twenty-six episodes into the series, Lily has created something that feels increasingly rare online: a space for thoughtful conversation. She chooses a book or movie (most recently: <a href="http://youtube.com/live/4j_J3i22fTc?pp=SAFgAXgB">Jane Austen's <em>Emma</em></a>), encourages fans to experience it for themselves, then hosts a livestream to unpack it together. They've touched on <a data-event-boundary="click" data-event-click='{"pattern":"ExternalLink"}' data-in-view='{"pattern":"ExternalLink"}' data-include-experiments="true" data-offer-url="https://x.com/nmixxpics_/status/2054511763580952949" href="https://x.com/nmixxpics_/status/2054511763580952949">privilege</a>, <a data-event-boundary="click" data-event-click='{"pattern":"ExternalLink"}' data-in-view='{"pattern":"ExternalLink"}' data-include-experiments="true" data-offer-url="https://x.com/nmixxpics_/status/1674476029463900161" href="https://x.com/nmixxpics_/status/1674476029463900161">toxic masculinity</a>, grief, loneliness, and even the <a data-event-boundary="click" data-event-click='{"pattern":"ExternalLink"}' data-in-view='{"pattern":"ExternalLink"}' data-include-experiments="true" data-offer-url="https://x.com/nmixxpics_/status/1728273053816360992" href="https://x.com/nmixxpics_/status/1728273053816360992">self-absorption</a> that can sometimes accompany fandom itself.</p>
<p>The idea began with a question. Reading has been one of Lily's defining passions for as long as she can remember, second only to music. She grew up watching BookTubers and falling headfirst into fictional worlds. But K-pop idols don't typically host literary discussion groups. "I didn't think it'd be possible, because, you know, I'm an idol," she says with a laugh. "It's not like I can just make a BookTube account." Then she stopped herself. "I was thinking, wait, why don't I, though?" she recalls. The answer, as it turns out, is that Lily is exactly the kind of person who would start a book club.</p>
<p>"I'm such a nerd," she says. "Honestly, even if there was no one reading along with me, I'd probably still do the live and just talk by myself the whole time."</p>
<p>That fervor is part of what makes the club feel bigger than content. Lily is just as likely to disappear down the rabbit hole of her favorite ships or passionately defend a divisive character as she is to unpack a novel's themes. The selections range from classics like <em>Pride and Prejudice</em> and <em>Wuthering Heights</em> to contemporary favorites like <em>The Thursday Murder Club</em>, as well as films such as <em>La La Land</em>, <em>About Time</em>, and <em>Parasite</em>. On paper, the list can seem eclectic. In practice, many of the stories wrestle with the same questions Lily returns to again and again—how people understand themselves, how they connect with others, and how they navigate the complicated process of growing up.</p>
<p>It's an unusual side quest for an idol. Then again, Lily has never seemed particularly interested in staying on the surface. The impulse to dig deeper and look for meaning in stories and emotions rather than skate past them can be traced back to childhood.</p>
<p>As a young girl growing up in Marysville, a town of just a few hundred people in Victoria, Australia, Lily remembers a life filled with contradictions. There were difficult experiences she still carries. There was loneliness. There were ordinary coming-of-age struggles. But there was also freedom. "I had a lot of sad things happen to me in my childhood," she says. “At the same time, I was living in a small town where I was really free to express myself and play.”</p>
<p>She remembers growing up surrounded by adults who celebrated curiosity over conformity, creating an environment where she never felt the need to hide parts of herself. "All of the adults around me were just so unjudgmental and just loved me unconditionally," she says. It's a generosity she now tries to extend to others.</p>
<p>That sense of security was shaken in February 2009, when <a data-event-boundary="click" data-event-click='{"pattern":"ExternalLink"}' data-in-view='{"pattern":"ExternalLink"}' data-include-experiments="true" data-offer-url="https://www.nma.gov.au/defining-moments/resources/black-saturday-bushfires" href="https://www.nma.gov.au/defining-moments/resources/black-saturday-bushfires">devastating bushfires swept through the region</a>. Lily and her family evacuated as the fires tore through Marysville, destroying much of the town. Friends were lost. Familiar places disappeared. In the years that followed, she lived with depression and anxiety, and her mother encouraged her to try music as an emotional outlet. What began as a way to process difficult emotions gradually became something more fulfilling. Music became the center of Lily's world. She sang constantly. She performed whenever she had the chance. For a few years, her family relocated to South Korea, where she was introduced more deeply to her mother's culture and began pursuing child acting and modeling, familiarizing herself with the Korean entertainment industry. By the time they returned to Australia, performing felt more like a calling than a hobby.</p>
<p>"When I was a young kid, I didn't really have an exact plan," Lily says. "I just knew that I wanted to be singing on stage."</p>
<p>She uploaded original songs online and spent years studying the artists she admired and playing small gigs in Australia. Then, when she was 12 years old, an opportunity arrived unexpectedly. Her mother spotted Australian auditions for the popular South Korean reality competition <em>K-pop Star 4</em> and encouraged her to try out. A short time later, Lily and her father were on a plane to Seoul.</p>
<p>What followed was months of competition, televised performances, and the surreal experience of chasing a dream in the public eye. Lily emerged as one of the show's breakout contestants, eventually finishing in fourth place and signing with JYP Entertainment. More than a decade later, those performances remain online for anyone curious enough to search for them. Lily is well aware. "It's all there," she says, laughing. "Every awkward phase, every performance."</p>
<p>Rather than cringe at them, she views those videos as a reminder of how long she's been working toward this version of herself. The girl singing Alicia Keys on a reality show and the woman writing lyrics for NMIXX's latest EP aren't entirely different people. If anything, Lily sees a surprising amount of continuity between them.</p>
<p>Even then, she was trying to connect with people through music. "I've always wanted to make people feel something," she says. That desire has only grown stronger with time.</p>
<p>When NMIXX debuted in 2022, expectations were already sky-high. Lily—the final member to be revealed after years of public anticipation—had become one of JYP Entertainment's most closely watched trainees, and fans had spent years wondering when she would finally debut. But once NMIXX arrived, the conversation quickly shifted. The group's <a href="https://www.teenvogue.com/story/nmixx-holiday-single-funky-glitter-christmas-interview">ambitious "mixx-pop" sound divided listeners</a> from the start. Few rookie groups have entered the industry under such intense scrutiny. Songs that abruptly switched genres became NMIXX's signature, but they also made the group one of K-pop's most hotly debated acts.</p>
<p>Lily remembers the period well. "There are some songs that I'm like, 'Oh my God, this is such a banger,' and then it doesn't get the reaction you were hoping for," she says. Still, she never doubted the group's direction. "We kept doing whatever we really wanted to do," she says. "If you're authentic to the music that you make, it's bound to resonate with people eventually."</p>
<p>Four years later, the conversation looks very different. The skepticism has largely given way to admiration. The group's recent releases have <a data-event-boundary="click" data-event-click='{"pattern":"ExternalLink"}' data-in-view='{"pattern":"ExternalLink"}' data-include-experiments="true" data-offer-url="https://www.uscannenbergmedia.com/2026/05/02/a-slow-burn-rise-how-nmixx-is-entering-the-global-stage-on-its-own-timeline/" href="https://www.uscannenbergmedia.com/2026/05/02/a-slow-burn-rise-how-nmixx-is-entering-the-global-stage-on-its-own-timeline/">connected with listeners</a> at home and abroad, while songs that once sounded jarringly unconventional have become unmistakably NMIXX. Rather than growing out of its sound, the group grew into it.</p>
<p>The members did, too. As NMIXX has evolved, the group has been given more opportunities to shape its own music. For Lily, that has meant stepping further into songwriting, a role that feels like a natural extension of the way she's always engaged with music.</p>
<p>Lily is fascinated by the architecture of a song. She loves references. She loves motifs. Most of all, she loves a theme. "I am a sucker for a theme," she laughs.</p>
<p>That instinct is all over <em>Heavy Serenade</em>. In "Crescendo," Lily filled the lyrics with musical terminology to reinforce the song's sweeping romanticism. "I wanted to convey a special, once-in-a-lifetime type of love," she says. "I really do love music. I am a music stan. I'm a music nerd. Music is one of the loves of my life. I feel like there's no higher praise, at least for me, than comparing someone to music."</p>
<p>In "LOUD," she threaded in a reference to Tantalus from Greek mythology, one of many literary breadcrumbs she likes to leave behind for attentive listeners.</p>
<p>“One of my favorite things about songwriting is that there are always little details that only you know the full meaning behind," she added. "They're like little nuggets hidden in the lyrics… Sometimes they're references that fans might recognize, or maybe they'll look them up and discover something new. Either way, it's another way we can connect."</p>
<p>There are songs Lily writes for NMIXX, and then there are the ones she writes for no one else. They begin in empty practice rooms, where she'll sit alone at a piano until something surfaces. The solitude is intentional. "I'm quite honest," she says. "I get a bit embarrassed [writing songs] in front of other people."</p>
<p>She doesn't write on a schedule. She writes when inspiration strikes and when she has enough space to listen to it. The songs often reveal things she didn't know she was carrying.</p>
<p>"I go back to the same themes a lot," she says. "And then I'm like, 'Oh wait, maybe I did care about this more than I thought.'"</p>
<p>One in particular keeps showing up. "Well, I am a romantic at heart," she smiles. "I really believe in love. I don't know... I'm just a very heartfelt person. There's something so special about being connected to someone. It's such a unique feeling, and it's different for everyone. I think music is one of the only ways you can really get into the nitty-gritty of that emotion. That's probably why there are so many love songs in the world. It's something everyone experiences differently, but everyone understands."</p>
<p>Lily's belief in love extends far beyond romance. It shapes the way she thinks about friendship, creativity, and perhaps most visibly, fandom. Before she was an idol, she was a fan. Stepping onto the other side of the artist-fan relationship gave her a new appreciation for the people she'd admired for years. It also changed the way she thinks about being a fan herself.</p>
<p>"I want to be a respectful fan to my idols,” she says, “just as I want to be respected by my fans."</p>
<p>Becoming an idol hasn't changed the way Lily experiences fandom so much as it has made her more thoughtful about it. She talks openly about the books she's reading, the music she's listening to, and the ideas she's turning over because, as a fan herself, those are the glimpses she always loved getting from the artists she admired.</p>
<p>"I really like it when I get the behind-the-scenes, the juicy dirt on my favorite people," she laughs. "It's not just an idol and a fan. I'm a person as well. I have my own personality and interests, and I love sharing them with fans, especially when they can relate to me, and we can have cool conversations about them. It's one of my favorite things about being an idol."</p>
<p>That openness is intentional. "It's very important to me that the way I'm perceived really reflects who I am as a person," she says.</p>
<p>Toward the end of our conversation, I ask Lily if there's anything else she'd like to add. It's the kind of question that usually produces a familiar answer. A reminder to stream the album. A thank you for the support. A teaser for what's coming next. Instead, Lily takes a moment to think. When she finally answers, she's not talking about NMIXX at all.</p>
<p>"I care about the mental health of teenagers," she says. "So I want you guys all to remember that you're beautiful inside and out, and the way that you look is the least interesting part of you. Your inside is the most important part, and please remember to nourish yourself. Your health is so important. Literally, you are beautiful just the way you are, and don't believe everything you see on the internet."</p>
<p>Earlier, Lily described herself as a "deeply unserious person." Maybe it's more accurate to say that Lily takes certain things very seriously, like music, storytelling, the people who support her, and the responsibility that comes with being someone others look up to.</p>
<p>It's why she hosts a book club. It's why she writes songs that read like journal entries. It's why she still talks about her favorite artists with the enthusiasm of a fan.</p>
<p>And it's why, when given one final opportunity to talk about herself, she'd rather spend it encouraging someone else.</p>
