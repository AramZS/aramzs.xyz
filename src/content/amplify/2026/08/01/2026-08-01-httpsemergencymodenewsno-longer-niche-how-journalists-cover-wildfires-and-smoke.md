<p>Wildfires and smoke continue to be major concerns as <a href="https://www.klcc.org/disasters-accidents/2026-07-27/central-oregon-wildfires-bench-fire-warm-springs-evacuations">more than a million acres have burned</a> in Oregon, and the <a href="https://www.cyclingweekly.com/racing/tour-de-france/final-stage-of-2026-tour-de-france-shortened-due-to-french-wildfires-montmartre-circuit-still-included">final stage of the Tour de France was shortened</a> due to wildfires in France. And <a href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/jul/24/thousands-of-lightning-strikes-trigger-fresh-wildfires-in-western-canada">thousands of lightning strikes have triggered new wildfires</a> in western Canada, with 90 active ones just in British Columbia.</p>
<p>As wildfires and smoke become more frequent, intense, and widespread due to climate change, several journalists told <em>Emergency Mode for News</em> that news coverage needs to be more creative, comprehensive, and anticipatory. It should incorporate science, public health, and practical guidance for the public, just like extreme heat coverage.</p>
<p>“We don’t for some reason kind of have that same approach [with wildfires and smoke,” Independent climate journalist <a href="https://www.kendrawrites.com/">Kendra Pierre-Louis</a> told <em>Emergency Mode for News.</em></p>
<p>“Probably because it’s so new.”</p>
<p>“I think that journalists can be a great resource for their readers who are trying to stay safe in a situation like this,” said Dustin Blitchok, managing editor at <a href="https://planetdetroit.org/"><em>Planet Detroit</em></a>.</p>
<h2><strong>Use clear and precise language</strong></h2>
<p>“Covering fires is really tricky I think because everything that we think we know about fires is wrong,” said Pierre-Louis.</p>
<p>Since fires can occur in many different places, not just forests, it’s important to refer to them as ‘wildfires’, not ‘forest fires’. Pierre-Louis noted that several of the major fires in California took place in <a href="https://wildfiretaskforce.org/southern-california-regional-profile/healthy-resilient-shrublands/">chaparral</a>, a kind of shrubland of native plants. Wildfires can also move into cities, like the <a href="https://www.artnews.com/art-news/news/greater-los-angeles-wildfires-artists-museums-galleries-affected-1234729479/">Palisades, Eaton, and Hurst fires</a> which spread to Los Angeles last January.</p>
<p>Descriptions and recommendations for masks and other personal protective equipment (PPE) should also go into detail about what is necessary to reduce specific risks.</p>
<p>“To just say ‘Wear a mask’, it’s not enough,” Blair said. “Medical/surgical masks, also known affectionately as baggy blues, which many people relied on during COVID — they are so much less effective than a well-fitted, I’ve said this a hundred times in my reporting, a well-fitted N95 or KN95, like well-sealed, well-fitted mask or respirator is the technical term.”</p>
<p>Journalists should also be clear about the link between the growing frequency and severity of this type of emergency with climate change.</p>
<p>“I’ve read so many articles that don’t explicitly link the fact that this is happening because of climate change,” Pierre-Louis said. “I don’t think you can be talking about wildfires and smoke in 2026 and not be talking about climate change.”</p>
<h2><strong>Get specific details about public health</strong></h2>
<p>After Detroit registered <a href="https://planetdetroit.org/2026/07/detroit-air-quality-worst-in-city-history-wildfires/">record-setting air quality readings</a>, Blitchok spoke to a physician at every major hospital system in Metro Detroit, and incorporated their observations and advice into a story. “We wanted to know if emergency room visits were up and what that looked like and kind of the nature of seeking help for respiratory distress and they were up. They were all busier.”</p>
<p>During big smoke and heat events, local reporters can also check health dashboards for emergency room visits in <a href="https://a816-health.nyc.gov/hdi/epiquery/visualizations/?PageType=ps&amp;PopulationSource=Syndromic">New York City</a>, <a href="https://www.er-watch.ca/">Toronto</a> and <a href="https://ph-lacounty.hub.arcgis.com/pages/heat-illness-mortality?utm_content=&amp;utm_medium=email&amp;utm_name=&amp;utm_source=govdelivery&amp;utm_term=">Los Angeles</a>.</p>
<p>“You want to look for spikes because the way smoke pollution in particular presents itself like some people might turn up with an asthma attack but you’re going to get strokes, you’re going to get heart attacks, you’re going to get a whole bunch of cardiovascular issues so you want to look up just like overall increase rates,” Pierre-Louis said.</p>
<p>Multiple journalists told <em>Emergency Mode for News</em> it was important to detail the health risks of smoke, and the groups who are most vulnerable: people with asthma, <a href="https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/copd/symptoms-causes/syc-20353679">COPD</a>, and cardiovascular conditions; people frequently working outdoors, “essential workers”, and the homeless or unhoused population.</p>
<p>“It’s the people who have to go to work no matter what that are at most at risk,” said <em>Toronto Star</em> climate change reporter Marco Chown Oved. “And those are the folks who have no choice.”</p>
<h2><strong>Sources should include a variety of experts</strong></h2>
<p>Blitchok noted how <em>Planet Detroit </em>senior reporter Brian Allnutt spoke to a <a href="https://planetdetroit.org/2026/07/wildfire-smoke-forecast-detroit/">Canadian wildfire expert </a>about weather conditions, fire fighting and forecasts to add context to the outlet’s coverage of the smoke covering the city. “Rather than just saying ‘The smoke is coming from Canada’, let’s talk to someone who’s there,” Blitchok said.</p>
<p>Other sources recommended by journalists for coverage of wildfires and smoke included public health directors, the local office of the National Weather Service, meteorologists from AccuWeather and staff ones from state regulators like the Michigan Department of Environment, Great Lakes, and Energy (EGLE).</p>
<p>Blitchok also recommended reaching out to local college or government researchers doing studies on air pollution or wildfire smoke patterns, as well as Canadian sources of data. “What kind of <a href="https://cwfis.cfs.nrcan.gc.ca/interactive-map">fire maps</a> does Canada have? What kind of <a href="https://cwfis.cfs.nrcan.gc.ca/en/fire-history">statistics</a> do they keep that I might want to quote in my reporting?”</p>
<p>Blair recommended journalists talk to <a href="https://natural-resources.canada.ca/forests-forestry/forest-programs/indigenous-forestry-initiative">indigenous people about additional forest management practices</a>, in contrast to the US-led forest service’s focus on <a href="https://smokeybear.com/">preventing, containing, and extinguishing wildfires</a>. “Give indigenous people a voice [to explain] how maybe we could do better going forward or how it’s been handled for thousands of years, prior to settlement,” Blair said.</p>
<h2><strong>Understand the fire calendar and historical context</strong></h2>
<p>Big, massive wildfires used to be rare, but Pierre-Louis said it is also helpful for reporters to know if an ecosystem is expected to burn, and <a href="https://www.nifc.gov/nicc/predictive-services/outlooks">when fire seasons usually take place</a>, to better identify weird or unusual conditions. For example, the wildfires in California that took place last January were “especially egregious” due to normally wet winters in Los Angeles.</p>
<p>While orange skies and ash are <em>visible</em> signs of poor air quality, it’s also important for journalists to inform and warn the public about less obvious dangers like toxic levels of ozone, methane, and carbon monoxide. “The issue is that as smoke travels, it ages and it gets smaller,” Pierre-Louis said.</p>
<p>While a lot of reporting focuses on fire fighting and suppression efforts, Pierre-Louis noted that policies focused on fire suppression means that places that should have burned have not burned in a really long time even though fires are natural and <a href="https://parks.canada.ca/nature/science/conservation/feu-fire/role">a critical part of the ecosystem</a>.</p>
<p>Kendra Pierre-Louis recommended journalists read <a href="https://www.timothyeganbooks.com/the-big-burn">Timothy Egan’s <em>The Big Burn</em></a> about the largest wildfire in the US for context on why wildfire policies became focused on suppression. “That’s a problem because many ecosystems need to burn. Smaller, more moderate fires removes all of the underbrush, so you don’t get these massive, intense wild fires.”</p>
<p>Blair also strongly recommended journalists read the book <a href="https://www.penguinrandomhouse.ca/books/739360/fire-weather-by-john-vaillant/9780735273160"><em>Fire Weather</em> by journalist John Valliant</a>, about the massive wildfire in Fort McMurray, Alberta in May 2016. “He talks about one of the enormous wildfires that happened in Northern Alberta and the idea that wildfires are getting so big, they’re starting to create their own weather systems, which is kind of unprecedented and a new thing in the world.”</p>
<h2><strong>Be proactive and plan ahead</strong></h2>
<p>If possible, Blitchok recommended journalists reach out to sources ahead of new instances of wildfire and smoke, including the local, city or state health agency that would be responsible. “What do they say about it? Do they have a plan in place?” he said, noting his news organization already had <a href="https://planetdetroit.org/2024/07/planet-detroits-guide-to-using-the-air-quality-index/">guides to air quality</a> available before the most recent hazardous levels. “I don’t think that you have to wait until the emergency is here.”</p>
<p>“Is my city prepared for wildfire smoke? Or what are public health officials in my city doing to prepare? You don’t have to wait until the summer to write that.”</p>
