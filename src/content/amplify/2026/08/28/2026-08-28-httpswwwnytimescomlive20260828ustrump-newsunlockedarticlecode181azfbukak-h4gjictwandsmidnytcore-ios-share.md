<h3><a href="https://www.nytimes.com/live/2026/08/28/us/trump-news?smid=nytcore-android-share/#trump-administration-yosemite-private-developer">The Trump administration looks to give a swath of Yosemite Park to a developer.</a></h3>

<p>The Trump administration has proposed giving a small parcel of land in Yosemite National Park in California to a private developer, according to federal officials and documents reviewed by The New York Times.</p>
<p>The potential deal, which has not yet been finalized, has alarmed conservationists and former federal officials, who say that Yosemite, the crown jewel of the nation’s park system, should be off limits to development.</p>
<p>The proposal, details of which were first reported <a href="https://www.notus.org/agencies/trump-administration-yosemite-private-developer-deal">by the news outlet NOTUS</a>, calls for transferring a small parcel just inside the western boundaries of Yosemite to a Las Vegas-based company. That firm wants to build a road and develop two 40-acre parcels near the park, according to documents submitted to Congress this year and reviewed by The Times.</p>
<p>In exchange, the company would purchase land of equivalent value, either near Yosemite or elsewhere in California, and hand it over to the Park Service, the documents show.</p>
<p>The company is controlled by Kingsbarn Realty Capital, a real-estate private equity firm. The firm bought the parcels in 2024 for $4 million through a limited liability corporation called Sanctuary at Yosemite, property records show.</p>
<p>“This is so ridiculous, it makes you sick,” said Don Neubacher, a former supervisor at Yosemite who is now on the executive council of the Coalition to Protect America’s National Parks, a nonprofit group. Mr. Neubacher said a similar proposal had been raised in the early 2000s by previous owners of the parcels and was rejected, first by the Park Service and later in court.</p>
<p>The proposed swap would effectively provide Kingsbarn with a shortcut into the park.</p>
<p>Aubrie Spady, a spokeswoman for the Interior Department, the parent agency of the Park Service, said in a statement that no final decision had been made.</p>
<p>“Any land exchange or access proposal involving National Park Service lands would be subject to all applicable federal laws, regulations and Departmental policies, including required environmental review and public notification processes,” Ms. Spady said.</p>
<p>The Interior Department notified Congress this year that it was considering paying for the exchange via the Land and Water Conservation Fund. The notification drew criticism from Democrats who said the fund was intended to pay for conservation programs on public lands.</p>
<p>Senator Adam Schiff, Democrat of California, in a statement Friday called Yosemite “one of California’s natural wonders.”</p>
<p>“This administration appears hellbent on moving forward in the face of opposition from the public, Congress and the courts,” he said. “But the only thing the administration cares about is whether there is money involved.”</p>

<p>Declaring that space exploration is “the hot thing,” President Trump on Friday signed an executive order creating a U.S. space academy and presented Medals of Honor to the Artemis II crew, which completed a historic 10-day flight around the moon.</p>
<p>At an event at the NASA Lyndon B. Johnson Space Center in Houston, Mr. Trump presented the Congressional Space Medal of Honor to the crew’s commander, Reid Wiseman; its pilot, Victor Glover; and the mission specialists Christina Koch and Jeremy Hansen.</p>
<p>In doing so, Mr. Trump carried out the traditional presidential duties of honoring American heroes who united the country with their achievements. He referred to the astronauts as “fearless explorers.”</p>
<p>Mr. Trump also announced that he was signing an executive order to create a U.S. Space Academy to feed the U.S. Space Force and NASA. Few details were released on Mr. Trump’s plans for the academy, but the president indicated they were preliminary and its location had not yet been determined.</p>
<p>The executive order created a commission chaired by the head of NASA tasked with “proposing key details for the establishment of the Space Academy” within 120 days.</p>
<p>“Space is the hot thing,” Mr. Trump told the crowd.</p>
<p>The event also carried a political dimension ahead of the midterm elections. The president’s approval rating is underwater, dragged down by high prices and an unpopular war against Iran, and his party is in danger of losing its control of both chambers of Congress.</p>
<p>Speakers at the event in Houston heaped praise not only on the astronauts, but also on Mr. Trump.</p>
<p>Col. Matt Anderson, the deputy administrator of NASA, told the crowd that “none of this would be possible without strong leadership from the top.”</p>
<p>“President Trump ignited the golden age of exploration and discovery. He gave us the vision,” Mr. Anderson said. “His Working Families Tax Cut Act gave us the resources, and the Artemis II crew took the first steps.”</p>
<p>In April, the Artemis II mission galvanized the country and <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/04/11/us/artemis-landing-splashdown-moon.html">transfixed the public</a>.</p>
<p>Over 10 days, the astronauts looped around the moon’s far side before returning to Earth, splashing down in the Pacific Ocean. The astronauts saw <a href="http://nytimes.com/interactive/2026/04/07/science/space/moon-photos-artemis-2-nasa.html">parts of the moon no human had ever laid eyes on</a>. They broke the record for the farthest distance humans have ever traveled from Earth.</p>
<p>The Congressional Space Medal of Honor has been awarded to fewer than three dozen astronauts beginning in the 1970s, <a href="https://www.nasa.gov/history/congressional-space-medal-of-honor/">according to NASA</a>.</p>

<p>The Trump administration has asked the Supreme Court to allow the U.S. military to fully enforce its ban on transgender service members, setting up the latest test of the administration’s policy priorities in front of the court.</p>
<p>The justices are expected to consider whether to review the legality of the administration’s ban this fall and, if they accept the case, could schedule oral arguments during the Supreme Court’s term that begins in October.</p>
<p>A few days after returning to office, Mr. Trump issued an executive order banning all trans troops and characterizing transgender identity as a “falsehood” that “conflicts with a soldier’s commitment to an honorable, truthful and disciplined lifestyle.”</p>
<p>The ban was one of a series of actions by the administration targeting transgender rights. The president also directed government agencies to withdraw federal funding from schools that allow transgender athletes in girls’ and women’s sports.</p>
<p>The Defense Department implemented Mr. Trump’s military order in February 2025, effectively forcing trans troops out of the military.</p>
<p>“No more dudes in dresses,” Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth announced then.</p>
<p>Transgender service members quickly sued. About 4,200 openly transgender people were in the military last year, according to government court filings — about 0.1 percent of the armed forces. They worked as infantry platoon leaders, pilots, nuclear engineers, doctors, intelligence analysts, police and other key professionals.</p>
<p>A series of <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/06/01/us/transgender-troops-appeals-court-ruling.html">lower-court ruling</a>s have protected a group of transgender troops who challenged the ban from being discharged while litigation continued. But last May, the Supreme Court’s conservative majority agreed in a separate case to generally allow the administration to dismiss troops while litigation continued.</p>
<p>D. John Sauer, the solicitor general, urged the justices in <a href="https://glad-org-wpom.nyc3.cdn.digitaloceanspaces.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/20260828_Talbott-USA_Writ-of-Certiorari.pdf">a filing</a> on Thursday to reverse the lower courts on a permanent basis and defer to the nation’s military leaders by upholding the Defense Department’s “considered military judgment” that accommodating trans troops would undermine “military effectiveness and lethality.”</p>
<p>“The authority of the U.S. military to determine who may serve in the nation’s armed forces is a matter of exceptional importance,” he wrote, pushing back on lower-court rulings that said the administration’s policy was discriminatory.</p>
<p>In the past year, many transgender service members have left the military voluntarily rather than face uncertainty and possible harm by contesting the new policy. Hundreds of others have stayed, insisting that their cases be considered individually by <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/08/03/us/politics/transgender-troops-ban.html">military retention boards</a> consisting of fellow service members. Discharge has generally been a foregone conclusion, given the president’s order.</p>

<p>The 28 challengers in the case before the justices are led by <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2021/01/25/us/biden-transgender-ban-military.html">Nicolas Talbott</a>, a second lieutenant in the U.S. Army Reserve. The group claims the policy unfairly and illegally directs hostility and prejudice against a group of people.</p>
<p>In a <a href="https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.cadc.41889/gov.uscourts.cadc.41889.1208855359.0.pdf">2-to-1 decision</a> in June, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit sided with the group, but their lawyers have asked a lower court to extend protections to all transgender troops.</p>
<p>The policy seemed to be “driven by the bare desire to harm a politically unpopular group,” wrote Judge Robert L. Wilkins, who was joined by Judge Judith W. Rogers.</p>
<p>Judge Justin R. Walker dissented, saying courts do not have the power to make such decisions about the military: “The Constitution assigns that authority to Congress and the commander in chief,” he wrote.</p>

<p>She wrote that a group of Democratic-led states that had sued “face irreparable harm where they are required to comply with a likely unconstitutional regulation for which compliance is practically impossible as to the 2026 midterm elections, now little more than two months away.”</p>
<p>“Most plaintiff states have already ordered their mail ballots, and some are required by state law to send them to eligible voters as early as next week,” she added.</p>
<p>She wrote that while the Supreme Court had “admonished” her on Monday that the Postal Service had not finalized any changes when the groups sued initially, making court action premature, the stakes had changed since then because of the step the agency took Friday to formalize its plan.</p>
<p>Her ruling marked the third court decision on the issue in less than a week, a whipsaw of action that <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/08/25/us/mail-in-voting-states.html">threatened to confuse voters</a>. <p>“Where a state or local election official does not obtain envelope and return envelope design approval, or does not upload voter information, or does not include unique intelligent mail bar codes on ballot envelopes, the U.S.P.S. will not mail ballots to voters,” she concluded.</p>
<p>The nonprofit groups behind one of the lawsuits said in a joint statement that they welcomed the court’s order. “This is an important step towards affirming the public’s confidence in the system of mail ballots, which thousands of eligible voters nationwide rely on as a core method of democratic participation,” the groups said in the statement.</p>

<p>“The empty invocation of national security is not a blank check to punish and retaliate against government critics,” she wrote.</p>
<p>In a statement, Anthropic said: “We welcome the court’s ruling that this supply chain risk designation was unlawful. We remain focused on working productively with the government to harness A.I. for our national security so all Americans benefit from this technology.”</p>

<p>The ruling caps the first of two lawsuits that Anthropic filed on March 9 in response to the Trump administration’s action. <p>The California ruling is the latest chapter in the fight between the Trump administration and Anthropic, which is heading toward what may be the biggest-ever initial public offering.</p>
<p>The two sides came to blows at the start of the year over a $200 million contract to provide the Pentagon with A.I. technology on classified systems. <p>Anthropic’s lawsuits argued that the legal statutes for labeling it a supply chain risk were narrow and did not apply to American firms. The company also said that the label was ideologically motivated to penalize Anthropic, and that the company’s First Amendment rights were being violated.</p>

<p>In her final ruling on Thursday, Judge Lin said the Trump administration had backed away from many of its central claims that Anthropic could sabotage its own technology.
