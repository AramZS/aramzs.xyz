<p>Owner Jeff Lawson, CEO Ben Collins, and comedian Tim Heidecker tell Inc. how they found themselves owning goblinlove.net and selling gay frog plushies, and what it means for the future of ‘The Onion.’</p>
<p>When billionaire Jeff Lawson bought <em>The Onion</em> at auction in 2024, he imagined it would offer an exciting next chapter in his career. Lawson co-founded the <a data-internallinksmanager029f6b8e52c="18" href="https://www.inc.com/cloud-computing">cloud computing</a> giant Twilio in 2008 and exited the company in 2024, primed and ready for his next move. He became <em>The Onion</em>’s majority shareholder after purchasing it at auction for an undisclosed amount from G/O Media, after the blog network began to sell off assets to cut losses for its parent company, the private equity firm Great Hill Partners.</p>
<p>At the time, the Chicago-based <em>Onion</em>, known for chronicling American life with an absurdist lens since its founding in 1988, was on life support. “My thought process was that buying <em>The Onion</em> would be fun,” Lawson tells Inc. “Instead, I looked at the financial story, and I was like, ‘Holy shit, this is not buying <em>The Onion</em>—this is saving <em>The Onion</em>,’ because it was in a death spiral.”</p>
<p>The auction was a delicate situation. <em>The Onion</em> had grown from a culty weekly newspaper born in Madison, Wisconsin, to a household name synonymous with viral headlines that captured the zeitgeist of the first two decades of the new century. Depending upon the winning bidder, the outlet risked disappearing forever or perhaps puttering along listlessly as a content mill for <a data-internallinksmanager029f6b8e52c="12" href="https://www.inc.com/artificial-intelligence">AI</a>-generated jokes.</p>
<p>When <em>The Onion</em> went up for sale, the site was a husk of its former glory: The user experience was cluttered with programmatic ads, and full-time staff had been whittled down to a little over a dozen writers and editors. A Gen-Xer who cultivated deep affection for <em>The Onion </em>while he was an undergrad at the University of Michigan in the 1990s, Lawson intervened out of a sense of moral obligation. Except there was a slight hiccup: He had no idea how to run a media company.</p>
<p>Ben Collins, a journalist who spent years covering extremism and misinformation for NBC News, was also leading a group trying to buy <em>The Onion</em>. Collins and Lawson didn’t know each other, but their respective expertise made them an ideal pairing. They quickly teamed up at the suggestion of a lawyer working to broker <em>The Onion</em>’s sale on G/O’s behalf. Collins, who publicly tussled online with internet trolls and provocateurs as a reporter, is now the CEO of <em>The Onion</em>’s parent company, Global Tetrahedron. Under his leadership, the publication has roared back to life, tackling <a href="https://theonion.com/politics/">beltway politics</a>, <a href="http://theonion.com/tim-cook-unveils-rows-of-artificial-wombs-after-announc-1846231807/">tech industry dystopia,</a> and <a href="https://theonion.com/happy-birthday-text-to-mom-spirals-into-whole-conversat-1846250378/">meddlesome text</a><a href="https://theonion.com/happy-birthday-text-to-mom-spirals-into-whole-conversat-1846250378/">s</a><a href="https://theonion.com/happy-birthday-text-to-mom-spirals-into-whole-conversat-1846250378/"> from your mom. </a></p>
<p>When Lawson acquired the outlet, revenue was barreling toward six figures annually, 70 percent of which was drawn from programmatic ads, Collins says. This year, <em>The Onion</em> is set to become an eight-figure business, largely driven by the resurrection of its print newspaper, which was scuttled by its previous owners in 2013.</p>
<p>“Our goal for the year is to get to 100,000 subscribers, because that would beat <em>The Washington Post</em> in terms of circulation,” Collins says. “And we’re going to get there.” Currently, <em>The Onion</em> has around 85,000 subscribers, making it one of the five largest newspapers in the country by circulation. Under Collins’s direction, Global Tetrahedron—named after a deep cut from the publication’s 1999 book <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Our_Dumb_Century"><em>Our Dumb Century</em></a>—continues to reinvent distressed media properties in ways that are audacious and seemingly engineered to go viral.</p>
<p>In November 2024, <em>The Onion</em> won a bankruptcy auction to acquire Infowars<strong>,</strong> the defunct conspiracy-and-fake-news empire founded by Alex Jones, and has been turning it into a sketch comedy platform, with comedian Tim Heidecker, part of the duo behind the surrealist sketch comedy show <em>Tim and Eric Awesome Show,</em> serving as creative director.</p>
<p>Infowars debuted in July with a <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tnz6PTSXiI0&amp;t=45s">YouTube livestream</a> that featured Heidecker replacing Jones in the anchor’s chair, taking live callers and ranting about demons in a gravely Texas drawl. Heidecker’s character is a direct riff on Jones, but he says the new Infowars will host a broad cast of characters, mostly inspired by the red-pilled internet. Programming, Heidecker says, will touch on “the world of tech worship, billionaire rise-and-grind culture, and AI slop.”</p>
<p>Heidecker is the satirical heart of the new Infowars, but Collins is tasked with injecting it with a winning business formula. Gaining control of arguably the most toxic brand in U.S. media is a victory in itself, Collins says. But reinventing Infowars into something with staying power that keeps audiences engaged remains the real challenge. That will require sticking to their philosophical guns in a climate hostile to biting satire about the powerful, while growing a community willing to pay for a product in a historically crowded streaming landscape.</p>
<h2>An old business model is new again</h2>
<p>In <em>The Onion</em>‘s new era, editorial leadership started with a blank slate, focusing on what had made the outlet resonate with so many in previous years. This meant introducing subscriptions, which include a monthly print edition.</p>
<p>Starting at $9 a month or $99 a year, subscriptions are by far the biggest revenue driver for the company, Lawson says, who adds that the new team has increased annual revenue by 4x compared with G/O Media. <em>The Onion’s</em> website barely serves any ads—a marked divergence from the media playbook that drove so many outlets toward extinction in the 2010s. The company also branched out into advertising by launching America’s Finest Agency, which allows <em>Onion</em> writers to work on branded campaigns for paying clients.</p>
<p>Unlike many CEOs, Collins is a chronically online Millennial who bristles at the prospect of getting an MBA. <em>The Onion</em>’s auction prompted a bigger question for him: In a media industry under siege by economic pressure, with the looming threat of AI stealing people’s jobs, what if saving the storied outlet could be a means of fighting the power and also hilarious?</p>
<p>There is ample proof these days that journalists like him can lead their own budding ventures: Defector Media grew out of a staff exodus from Deadspin, the sports website once owned by G/O. There are also successful journalist-owned websites like 404 Media, which was founded by former staff members of <em>Vice</em>‘s tech vertical Motherboard.</p>
<p>So far, the new <em>Onion</em> has the blessing of some of its most devoted acolytes, including one man who helped build the publication from its nascent days as a hardscrabble paper in Madison, Wisconsin.</p>
<p>“Everything that people hear about when it comes to <em>The Onion</em> should be funny, should be satirical. It should further the brand,” says Scott Dikkers, the paper’s original editor-in-chief. “I’m excited about these new owners because they seem to get that and they’re doing a good job with those optics.”</p>
<h2>Transforming Infowars’ absurd spectacle</h2>
<p><em>The Onion</em>’s Infowars coup is an unlikely final chapter for Alex Jones’s once-ascendant empire. Jones started broadcasting Infowars from a studio in Austin in 1999. The program became a leading disseminator of fake news, helping to popularize conspiracies about September 11, vaccines, chemtrails, and a nonexistent cabal of political elites who, as Jones falsely claimed, schemed up a “new world order” that controlled the economy. Clips of his broadcasts regularly circulate on social media, such as <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LGBTQ_chemicals_conspiracy_theory">trademark rants</a> about chemical runoff “turning the frickin’ frogs gay.” Before it was banned by YouTube and Facebook in 2018, the website was drawing 1.4 million daily visits, a <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2018/09/04/technology/alex-jones-infowars-bans-traffic.html"><em>New York Times</em> analysis</a> found that year, audiences that rivaled mainstream news sites.</p>
<p>In 2021, Jones was found liable for defamation and causing emotional distress after he falsely claimed the 2012 Sandy Hook Elementary School shooting was a hoax. A Connecticut jury ordered Jones to pay roughly $1.4 billion in damages to families of Sandy Hook victims in October 2022—at the time, the biggest defamation settlement in U.S. history—and a U.S. bankruptcy judge mandated the liquidation of Jones’s personal assets, including Infowars. <em>The Onion</em> pounced, winning a $1.4 million cash bid at auction to acquire the assets of Free Speech Systems, the Texas-based holding company for Alex Jones’s Infowars.</p>
<p>Sandy Hook was “something that Jones never let go of,” says Chris Mattei, an attorney for families of the children and school staff who sued Jones for defamation in 2018. It was “such a provocative and outrageous claim that whenever he talked about it, it drove significant traffic to his website and his store.”</p>
<p>The judgment is what put Infowars up for sale—and into <em>The Onion</em>‘s control with the support of Sandy Hook families. While Infowars’ IP remains in the possession of a court-appointed receiver in Texas, an agreement was reached in April allowing Global Tetrahedron to lease Infowars’ IP for $81,000 a month, pending approval from the <a href="https://www.npr.org/2026/04/30/nx-s1-5806038/the-onion-infowars-alex-jones-texas-supreme-court">Texas appellate court</a>. (In August, Jones received a <a href="https://www.cnn.com/2026/08/21/us/alex-jones-infowars-newtown-sandy-hook-hnk">legal boost</a> when a different Texas court dismissed a separate $50 million defamation verdict.)</p>
<p><em>The Onion</em> hasn’t waited to launch the new Infowars, however, because Free Speech Systems is no longer owned by Alex Jones, and Collins feels there’s nobody to sue them—at least for now. So far, the new Infowars has given $100,000 from merch sales to the Sandy Hook families, in lieu of Jones’s failed payments.</p>
<p>“As soon as the Texas appellate courts give us the green light,” Mattei says, “our hope is that <em>The Onion</em> will take full ownership of Infowars … and be able to go full flight in whatever direction it chooses.”</p>
<p>Tim Heidecker is leading Infowars’ creative charge. So far, the program has been a delightfully absurd spectacle—like a version of Adult Swim born in a 4chan forum. The goal of Infowars, the comedian says, is to reflect the absurdity of the modern internet—and to provide an alternative to a kind of monoculture taking over comedy in the streaming age.</p>
<p>In a previous era, creative direction may have called for a reboot of The Onion News Network, a video channel that spoofed 24-hour cable news networks. But the new Infowars is trying to reflect the modern world, where millions are more plugged into TikTok reels and podcasts like the <em>Joe Rogan Experience</em> than traditional broadcast news. Naturally, the program so far has been full of characters from that red-pilled huckster milieu, such as <a href="https://www.youtube.com/shorts/g2WaAqC4Kkw">longevity influencers,</a> <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8xqjND34XeI">Second Amendment zealots</a>, and <a href="https://www.youtube.com/shorts/8MNd9rvioIU">supplement-pushing grifters</a>.</p>
<p>“There isn’t a place right now for a late-night, experimental, outsider, kind of punk, low-budget comedy that has a point of view that is trying to reflect the world we live in,” Heidecker says.</p>
<p>Infowars can’t compete with Netflix, Heidecker says, but it can carve out a healthy niche of patrons. “People are starting to find the things that they like and support them directly. It’s the same as going to your local coffee shop instead of Starbucks,” Heidecker says of a hypothetical Infowars subscription.</p>
<h2>The future of Infowars is still in the balance</h2>
<p>Infowars isn’t making much money yet—at least not in the sense that one can quantify on a balance sheet. Collins says overall subscriptions have jumped in tandem with major Infowars news, so the excitement and demand are clearly evident.</p>
<p>By assuming control of Infowars’ IP, Global Tetrahedron gets its second portfolio company. This includes a production studio, 300 website domains, subscriber lists, and a few other assets. Collins is clear, however, that <em>The Onion</em> didn’t bid to capitalize on an obvious commercial gold mine.</p>
<p>“<a data-internallinksmanager029f6b8e52c="15" href="https://www.inc.com/business-plans">Business plans</a>? Not really,” Collins says of the Infowars portfolio. “Comedy and fuck-you plans? Definitely, yes.”</p>
<p>Still, the<em> Onion</em> team finds a kind of sophomoric delight in some of the assets they’ve acquired. “Did you know that part of the domain list is goblinlove.net? Maybe the most prized nonphysical asset we’ve got among people in our office,” Collins says. They’ve immortalized certain tropes from Infowars in the form of merch, such as <a href="https://store.theonion.com/products/gay-frog-plush-replica">“Gay Frog” plushies</a>, to bring Jones’s tirades to life.</p>
<p>More important than squeezing revenue from the assets themselves is cultivating an audience willing to pay for at least some gated content eventually, Collins says. “We will likely eventually go the patronage route once we have the critical mass of content that would make people actually want to pay for it,” says Collins. He references comedy site Dropout as a model, “where it’s not just a bunch of shows, but access to a world of live shows, merch, online and offline community.”</p>
<p>While the legal battle over who owns the official Infowars website continues, <em>The Onion</em> is publishing under the brand name on <a href="https://theonion.info/">its website</a>, YouTube, and social media channels. The new Infowars YouTube channel already has 147,000 subscribers, and the brand has grown to 250,000 followers on Instagram.</p>
<p>Ultimately, <em>The Onion</em>’s vision for Infowars is to serve as a launch pad for experimental comedy and performers who probably wouldn’t have a home on a major streaming platform.</p>
<p>“We all recognize that the marketplace [for comedy] has become pretty narrow, and, if I may, incredibly boring and sanitized,” Mia DiPasquale, Infowars’ head of programming, explains. “And there’s really nowhere for these people to go, to cut their teeth, to get opportunities, or just to make the freaky thing that they want to make.”</p>
<p>In the near term, a few things are clear. The Infowars brand is a vehicle for more than just lampooning Jones’s legacy. It’s part of a real, growing business and serves as proof that something as societally damaging as Infowars can be subverted for satire.</p>
<p>“We finally won something,” Collins says. “People get to dance on the grave of something evil. And it’s insanely rare to be able to do that.”</p>
<p>When talking about <em>The Onion</em> and its future, Collins sounds convincing and determined, motivated to take on any number of societal forces it views as pernicious, from Alex Jones to AI-armageddon to pseudoscience.</p>
<p>“What kind of world do you want to live in, really?” he asks. “Do you want to live in an incredibly extractive world where you’re just one of 55 companies that still exist? Or do you want to create your own path and build an army? We’ve just built the army.”</p>
