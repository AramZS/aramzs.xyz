<p>We spent 10 years pushing people to work out loud. Spirit's data sale is teaching everyone a good reason to stop.</p>
<p>Somewhere in the 500 million Teams messages Spirit Airlines left behind, someone is complaining about a roster change. Someone else is asking a colleague, in confidence, whether it's worth raising something with their manager. Someone has made a joke that will not survive being read by a stranger in six years' time.</p>
<p>They typed all of it on the reasonable assumption that it would sit on a server doing nothing, the way workplace data generally does: retained, technically discoverable, occasionally searched, mostly forgotten.</p>
<p>That assumption expired when the airline did.</p>
<p>Google agreed to pay $10 million for the archive. Then micro1, a startup that builds AI agents designed to work as employees inside simulated companies, came in late at $12.5 million. Its founder has been <a href="http://micro1/?utm_source=reworked.co">admirably direct about why</a>: an agent needs a world to refer to — files, chats, documents — so that what it does looks like something a real person would do.</p>
<p>So: Spirit's people lost their jobs. Their conversations are now being bid over as raw material for software designed to perform work like theirs convincingly. Next month a bankruptcy judge decides if the sale goes ahead, and to which bidder, and if union objections stand. Until then, Google has won the auction, but not yet the data.</p>
<p>There are easier weeks to make the case for working out loud.</p>
<p>I want to be careful here, because there's a lazy version of this argument doing the rounds: that we’re all training our own replacements, one Teams message at a time. I don't think that's right. An archive holds the residue of work, not the work. It has what you sent, not the read of the room; the decision, not the hesitation before it; the thing you said, not the three things you decided it would be wiser not to. A model trained on it might learn the register of corporate communication extremely well while entirely missing the judgement and expertise beneath it all.</p>
<p>But you don't have to <a href="https://www.reworked.co/digital-workplace/transforming-collaboration-and-workforce-planning-with-digital-twins/">believe in the digital twin</a> for this to change behavior.</p>
<p>You only have to believe that other people might.</p>
<h2>We Spent a Decade Arguing the Opposite</h2>
<p>Everyone in this profession has given some version of the same advice: Get it out of the inbox! <a href="https://www.reworked.co/collaboration-productivity/rehumanize-your-workplace-by-working-out-loud/">Work out loud</a>! Put the decision in the open channel where it can be found, searched, inherited and reused. Stop emailing four people a thing that 50 people need.</p>
<p>It was good advice. It was also, always, a request for a little act of faith: write it down somewhere persistent, and trust that persistence works in your favor.</p>
<p>For years we treated that persistence as a feature. Searchable history. Organizational memory. <a href="https://www.reworked.co/knowledge-findability/dont-let-critical-knowledge-walk-out-the-door/">Knowledge that doesn't walk out the door</a> when somebody leaves.</p>
<p>Now we are discovering the awkward corollary: it doesn't walk out the door when <em>everybody</em> leaves, either.</p>
<p>The Spirit sale brings into sharp view something that was always true but ordinary employees rarely had to think about: the archive is an asset. It outlives the conversation, the project, the team, your employment and, in this case, the company itself. It can be sold to someone you have never heard of for purposes nobody specified when you were encouraged to move your work into the open channel.</p>
<p>There's a document, somewhere, called something like Records and Data Retention Policy. It says how long things are kept and why, and it is the basis on which employees are entitled to feel that someone has thought about this. What it does not say is that it is a promise made by an entity capable of ceasing to exist. Spirit's messages are almost certainly younger than Spirit's retention framework. The framework is gone; the messages are in a bidding war.</p>
<p>Retention policy has always been built on the premise that data is a liability — keep less, delete on schedule, reduce what can be discovered. An asset register runs the other way.</p>
<p>This is not shadow IT as we've understood it. The classic version is a capability gap: the sanctioned tool can't do the job, so people route around it with Dropbox and WhatsApp and a personal Gmail account. You can, theoretically, fix that by providing a better tool.</p>
<p>What's coming is different.</p>
<p>The tool does the job. The problem is that it <em>remembers</em>.</p>
<p>You can’t procure your way out of memory. Ask anyone in financial services who watched the WhatsApp record-keeping fines land: when people have a reason to move conversation off-platform, they move it. Discovery was already enough of a reason; AI gives them another.</p>
<h2>Avoidance Without Judgement</h2>
<p>HR worked this out years ago. Anyone who’s hired or fired knows there are conversations experienced HR people will <em>only</em> have on the phone. It's a trained practice with a defined scope — grievances, performance, exits — and someone in Legal who knows when a hold applies and when it doesn't.</p>
<p>That same practice is about to spread through the organization, without any of the training.</p>

<p>That's worse than either extreme, for three reasons.</p>
<p>The first is that reasoning goes before anything else. People aren't particularly worried about recording what was decided. They're worried about recording <em>why</em>: the trade-off, the reservation, the awkward context, the person whose objection was overruled. So you end up with immaculate archives of outcomes and none of the thinking that produced them.</p>
<p>And reasoning is precisely what makes a decision inheritable. New joiners can't learn much from “we decided X.” Auditors can't either. Six months later, even the people who made the decision will confidently invent a rationale that bears only a passing resemblance to the one they had at the time.</p>
<p>The second is that off-record work runs on proximity. A partner can suggest a coffee. A contact-center agent on a rota cannot. Neither can the remote hire three time zones away, or the person whose second language is perfectly good in writing and much harder work on a call.</p>
<p>Push the real decisions back into corridors, coffees and quiet phone calls and you rebuild, remarkably efficiently, the access hierarchy that a decade of digital and hybrid working was supposed to dismantle. The official record becomes cleaner. The actual organization becomes less legible.</p>
<p>The third is that almost everything we claim to know about organizations depends on people writing things down semi-honestly. Pulse surveys, AMAs, comments, communities, sentiment analysis on collaboration platforms — every <a href="https://www.reworked.co/employee-experience/employee-listening-is-a-strategy-not-a-survey/">listening mechanism</a> in the internal communications toolkit assumes some degree of candor.</p>
<p>If people come to believe that their words are a future asset, you’re no longer measuring the organization. You’re measuring what people are prepared to have on the record.</p>
<p>Those are not the same thing, and the gap between them has a name: trust.</p>
<h2>The Uncomfortable Upside</h2>
<p>This is where I part company with most of the commentary, which has (understandably) settled on straightforward alarm.</p>
<p>Privacy horrors aside, there’s a good reason this archive is worth $12.5 million, and it isn't because nobody has ever seen a Teams message before.</p>
<p>Nobody outside large organizations has ever had particularly good evidence of how large organisations actually function.</p>
<p>Workplace tools are disproportionately built by small young companies where 12 people talk to each other constantly, and then sold to organizations with shift patterns, works councils, contractors, subsidiaries, six layers of sign-off and thirty years of documents nobody can find.</p>
<p><a href="https://www.reworked.co/digital-workplace/meta-shutters-workplace-now-what/">Facebook made this mistake</a> in its purest form: it modelled an enterprise product, Workplace, on how Facebook itself worked, and discovered that very few other companies on earth worked anything like Facebook. Microsoft remains perennially surprised by the existence of people who work for more than one organization, or hold two affiliations with the same one.</p>
<p>The result is software designed around an imaginary worker: salaried, digitally confident, permanently logged in, equipped with a laptop and one uncomplicated relationship with their employer.</p>
<p>Real organizations are considerably messier. Sketch out a process and what should be logical and linear swiftly looks like it was imagined by Jackson Pollock.</p>
<p>Until now, most of the evidence of that mess has been safely behind corporate firewalls. Product teams get interviews, surveys, analytics and carefully assembled customer advisory boards. What they don't get is the thing itself: years of mundane organizational life, complete with the rota arguments, duplicated work, unofficial escalation paths, inexplicable approval chains and that one person in Finance everyone knows you need to ask.</p>
<p>Convergence — every workplace platform gradually absorbing every other workplace platform's features — is partly what happens in the absence of that evidence. If you don't really know what the job is, build everything and hope.</p>
<p>Spirit's archive offers something different: not a use case but a world.</p>
<p>Something trained on it might finally understand a rota dispute, an escalation path, a sign-off chain, the fact that most people at work are not knowledge workers enjoying total autonomy over their day, nor are they always acting in the company’s or even their own interest.</p>
<p>And not only process. Almost everything a model has learned about how people write comes from text composed for an audience — published, edited, performed. This is something entirely different: half-sentences, shorthand, the message that only makes sense because of the one three days earlier. Not the polished public persona we present, but how we <em>actually</em> communicate. The Enron corpus gave the field email on its own. This offers email alongside chat, files and calendars, all connected — which happens to be exactly what the flight attendants are objecting to.</p>
<p>There’s no easy way to resolve the tension. The data is valuable because it is rich and specific. The privacy problem is that it is far too rich and specific to anonymize safely.</p>
<p>Both things are true, and that's what makes this an argument rather than simply a lament.</p>
<h2>What to Do</h2>
<p>Not another policy. Plenty of people are writing the governance checklist this week, and <a href="https://www.reworked.co/talent-management/what-googles-10-million-bid-for-spirit-airlines-data-means-for-every-employer">Reworked has already run a good one</a>.</p>
<p>I'm more interested in practice.</p>
<p>First, tell people plainly what is recorded, how long it persists and what can happen to it. Not on page 47 of an acceptable-use policy nobody has read since onboarding. Uncertainty drives avoidance, and institutional vagueness on this subject increasingly reads as evasion.</p>
<p>Second, make recording the reasoning behind important decisions a deliberate, named part of how work gets done.</p>
<p>Because that's the thing people will stop writing first. Not the decision itself. Not the project update. Not the sanitized summary generated afterwards. The messy, contingent, human explanation of why this option won and that one didn't.</p>
<p>And it will be the last thing anyone notices has gone.</p>
<p>Every organization that eventually finds itself with a decade of beautifully searchable Slack or Teams history and no institutional memory will have arrived there one perfectly sensible individual decision at a time.</p>
<p>Somewhere in Spirit's archive, someone complained about a roster change because Teams was simply where work happened. They weren't creating a dataset, they weren't preserving institutional memory, and they certainly weren't contributing an asset to a future bankruptcy estate. They were just doing their job.</p>
<p>For a decade, we designed workplace technology around the assumption that getting people to write more of that work down was unambiguously good.</p>
<p>The archive was always keeping track. What's changed is that people now have a reason to notice.</p>
