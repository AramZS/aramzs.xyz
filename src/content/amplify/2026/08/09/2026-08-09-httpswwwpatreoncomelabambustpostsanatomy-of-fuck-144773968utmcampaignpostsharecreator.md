<h3>To write dialogue, it’s important to understand three things:</h3>
<ul><li><p>How people talk</p></li><li><p>Exactly who your characters are</p></li><li><p>How to write an engaging conversation</p></li></ul>
<p>Writing engaging dialogue requires that the conversation feels real <em>enough</em> so that the reader isn’t taken out of the story by what feels like Siri and Alexa exchanging data, but not <em>so</em> realistic that it’s as boring and generally unproductive as most real conversations. That’s not a knock on human interaction, and I personally am very keen on naturalistic dialogue — I use it myself a lot — but a keen-eyed reader will see that conversations in real life tend to be a bit more… all over the place, than the ones in fiction, because conversations in fiction need to serve a purpose (either progress the plot, make us feel something, or advance our understanding of characters) and conversations in real life don’t. You can just talk shit with your best friend for three hours. <h3>To write an action scene, it’s important to understand three things:</h3>
<ul><li><p>How the action moves from moment to moment</p></li><li><p>How to make it readable to the audience</p></li><li><p>Who your characters are</p></li></ul>
<p>To write a decent action scene, you need to block the whole thing out in your head. You need to see the action in your head, and to do that you need to understand what makes action interesting. You need to understand that good action comes from pacing, that who is acting and who is reacting tells us who is controlling the flow of the event, that our point of view character is always doing one or the other. That you need to anticipate, build up, and then deliver on that anticipation. You need to make this readable to the audience, knowing that shorter sentences speed up time and longer ones often allow us to breathe but also slow things down, and that you can use line and paragraph length to make the reader subconsciously read faster, and then you <em>also</em> need to make sure the characters are still acting in-character, sometimes allowing us peeks inside their heads without destroying the pacing by using qualifying adjectives and adverbs that an impartial narrator wouldn’t use. This all sounds very technical, so let me write you an example.</p>
<blockquote><p>“You’re a fucking moron!” Anne shouted, raising the metal pipe. It was slick in her hands and she tightened her grip. <p>You see where this is going. Action: something or someone is doing something. Reaction: someone responds to the previous thing or experiences it. Back and forth. The action informs both the character’s motivations, tells the reader clearly and concisely what’s going on, <em>and</em> needs to flow well enough to keep us invested.</p>
<p>To summarize: to write characters you need to understand them, understand how people interact with each other, in a way that feels both organic and at the same time have a narrative throughline, progressing things in a deliberate direction.
