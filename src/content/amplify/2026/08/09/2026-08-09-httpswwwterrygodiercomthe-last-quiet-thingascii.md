<p>$400. Needs charging nightly. Since last Tuesday.</p>
<p>This watch costs four hundred dollars. It also tells time.</p>
<p>It also tracks my steps, monitors my blood oxygen, measures my sleep quality, logs my workouts, reminds me to breathe, reminds me to stand, nudges me to close my rings, alerts me to unusual heart rhythms, pings me with notifications from six apps, and dies every night.</p>

<p>One of these is a product. The other is a relationship.</p>
<h2>THE AWAKENING</h2>
<p>Here is something nobody says plainly: Sometime in the last twenty years, our possessions came alive.</p>
<p>Not all at once. Not dramatically. One by one, the objects in our lives opened their eyes, found our faces, and began to need us.</p>
<p>Your thermostat has opinions now. Your television requires a login. Your car updates itself overnight, and sometimes when you start it in the morning, the interface has rearranged itself, as if someone broke in and reorganized your dashboard while you slept.</p>
<p>Your earbuds won't play music until they've updated their firmware. Your refrigerator wants to be on your Wi-Fi.</p>
<p>None of this is broken. This is the product functioning as designed.</p>
<p>1950 — Toaster. You push the lever. Toast comes up. Done.</p>
<p>1990 — Television. You press power. You change the channel. Done.</p>
<p>2005 — Phone. You make a call. You hang up. Done.</p>
<p>2010 — Phone. You update it. You charge it. You configure it. You are never done.</p>
<p>2015 — Thermostat. It learns your schedule. It needs Wi-Fi. It has opinions. You are never done.</p>
<p>2020 — Car. It updates overnight. It has a subscription for heated seats. You are never done.</p>
<p>2024 — Everything. Everything needs you. You are never done.</p>
<p>For most of human history, you bought a thing, and it was yours, and it was FINISHED. That word is nearly extinct.</p>
<p>Nothing you own is finished. Everything exists in a state of permanent incompletion, permanently needing. Your phone needs updates, needs charging, needs storage cleared, needs passwords rotated. Your apps need permissions reviewed, terms accepted, preferences re-configured after every update. Your subscriptions need evaluating, need renewing, need canceling, need justifying to yourself every month when the charge appears. The purchase isn't the end of anything. It's the first day of a relationship you didn't agree to, with no clean way out.</p>
<p>You live in a house full of dependents.</p>
<h2>THE SHIFT</h2>
<p>You will pick up your phone eighty, ninety, a hundred times today.</p>
<p>Here is what nobody tells you about those pickups:</p>
<p>Dismissing a notification ...... 22%</p>
<p>Intentional use ................ 20%</p>
<p>Checking something that pinged . 18%</p>
<p>Replying to a person ........... 15%</p>
<p>Updating/configuring/fixing .... 12%</p>
<p>Unlocking, forgetting why ...... 8%</p>
<p>Managing a subscription ........ 5%</p>
<p>Screen time you chose: 35%</p>
<p>Screen time your devices chose for you: 65%</p>
<p>Most of your screen time isn't leisure. It isn't addiction. It isn't even a choice. It's maintenance.</p>
<p>Your phone is not a slot machine. It's a to-do list that writes itself.</p>
<h2>THE BLAME</h2>
<p>I need to say something about Screen Time.</p>
<p>When Apple introduced it in 2018, it was received as a concession: a gesture of corporate responsibility from a company that understood its product might be too compelling. The framing was careful, almost therapeutic. We want to help you understand your relationship with your device.</p>
<p>Here are your numbers. Here's how often you pick it up. Here's how your hours break down. Set a limit if you'd like. Take control.</p>
<p>It was, by every surface reading, an act of care.</p>
<p>This is the story your phone tells you about yourself every Sunday.</p>
<p>Screen Time gives you a report card. And if the grade is bad, the design makes one thing clear: That's a YOU problem.</p>
<p>It measures YOUR usage. Tracks YOUR behavior. Gives YOU a weekly report card. If the numbers are too high?</p>
<p>You picked it up too much.<br/>
You spent too long.<br/>
You failed your limit.<br/>
Try again next week.<br/>
Try harder.</p>
<p>Screen Time is a blame shift dressed in a soft font.</p>
<p>This Week, Your Devices Asked You For:<br/>
94 interruptions<br/>
37 decisions<br/>
4 hours of unpaid labor</p>
<p>How much of this was your idea?</p>
<h2>THE TRICK</h2>
<p>This is the trick, and once you see it, you see it everywhere:</p>
<p>THE INDUSTRY creates devices that need constant attention. THE DIAGNOSIS says you're addicted. THE TREATMENT sells you more products that need you. The wellness framing locates the problem inside you.</p>
<p>But what if you're not weak? What if you're not addicted?</p>
<p>What if you're just tired?</p>
<p>What if the exhaustion everybody feels isn't a moral failure but the completely rational response to being made responsible for an ecosystem of objects that never stop asking?</p>
<p>Nobody in a position of power is saying this. The reason is simple:</p>
<p>You're overwhelmed → Buy a wellness app → App needs an account, sends notifications, requires configuration → You're more overwhelmed → Try a digital detox program → Program has an app → …</p>
<p>They sold you the condition. Now they sell you the treatment. The treatment is another thing that needs you.</p>

<p>This is your personal infrastructure. You are the entire IT department.</p>
<p>Nobody architected this. It accreted — one device, one app, one free trial at a time — into a system no competent engineer would have designed on purpose.</p>
<p>In software, there's a term for what happens when shortcuts and deferred maintenance pile up: TECHNICAL DEBT.</p>
<p>You have fifteen years of it.</p>
<p>— The email address from college that 80 services still have on file</p>
<p>— The cloud storage where five years of photos may or may not still exist</p>
<p>— The password you reuse because managing unique ones across 100 accounts is a part-time job</p>
<p>— The smart home device running an app last updated in 2022</p>
<p>— The subscription you keep meaning to cancel</p>
<p>— The two-factor codes on a phone you no longer own</p>
<p>— The Bluetooth device list full of things called “Unknown”</p>
<p>— The login credentials saved in a browser you switched away from</p>
<p>— The free trial that became a subscription that became load-bearing infrastructure</p>
<p>You carry all of this below the surface. A low hum of open loops that never become urgent enough to resolve and never fully let go.</p>
<p>Then one day you get a new phone. And things break.</p>
<p>Somewhere around hour two, sitting on your couch, trying to re-pair your earbuds while your watch throws errors and your smart lock has locked you out of your own home, the feeling crystallizes:</p>
<p>I am doing a job.</p>
<p>Not metaphorically. A job with tasks and troubleshooting and problem-solving and no compensation. A job you didn't apply for and can't quit.</p>
<p>And — this is the part worth sitting with — a job that used to belong to someone else. A support team. An IT department. That labor didn't vanish. It was externalized onto you so gradually you didn't think to call it what it was.</p>
<h2>THE RELEASE</h2>
<p>I know how this is supposed to end. I'm supposed to tell you to simplify. Audit your subscriptions. Curate your devices. Own less.</p>
<p>I'm not going to do that.</p>
<p>Because that framing is the same trick wearing different clothes.</p>
<p><s>Be more intentional</s> — still your fault<br/>
<s>Practice digital minimalism</s> — still your fault<br/>
<s>Set better boundaries</s> — still your fault</p>
<p>The problem was never how many things you own. The problem is that OWNING means something it never used to. Everything you buy is the beginning of a relationship you'll be maintaining until one of you dies or gets discontinued.</p>
<p>What I actually want to say is simpler:</p>
<p>This is not your fault.</p>
<p>The tiredness is not a character flaw. The guilt, the sense that you should be handling all of this better, more gracefully, with less friction, that guilt was manufactured. It was placed inside you by an industry that profits from your participation and a wellness culture that profits from your shame.</p>
<p>Both need you to believe the problem is you.</p>
<p>It isn't.</p>
<h2>THE QUIET</h2>
<p>My Casio is on my wrist right now.</p>
<p>It's telling me what time it is. That's all it's telling me. It collected no data while I slept. It has no report to show me. It has no opinions about my health, my habits, or my attention. It is, in this moment, asking absolutely nothing of me.</p>
<p>And that absence, the peace of a thing that does what it does and then shuts up, feels like the most luxurious thing I own.</p>
<p>Not because it's retro. Not because it's minimal.</p>
<p>Because it's done.</p>
<p>It was finished the day it was assembled in 1989 and it will be the same watch tomorrow that it is today. It will never update. Never change its interface. Never ask me to accept new terms.</p>
<p>It is the last quiet thing in a life full of things that won't stop talking.</p>
<p>And some days I wear it just for the silence.</p>
<p>You're not bad with technology. Technology got bad at being finished.</p>
