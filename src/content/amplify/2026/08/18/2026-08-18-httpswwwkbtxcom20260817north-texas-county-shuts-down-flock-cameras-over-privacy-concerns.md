title: North Texas county shuts down Flock cameras over privacy concerns

<div><section><p>HOOD COUNTY, Texas (KBTX) - A North Texas county has shut down its network of Flock automated license plate reader cameras, saying the technology amounted to warrantless surveillance of residents going about their daily lives.</p><figure><figcaption><span>Hood County Constable John D. Shirley covers a Flock surveillance camera</span><span>(Hood County Constable, Precinct 2)</span></figcaption></figure><p>Hood County Constable John D. Shirley announced Saturday that he had finished unplugging and covering the county’s Flock Safety cameras at the request of county commissioners.</p><p data-fusion-component="IHOKUDZ6MZF47PUQMXSBBPQLRM"></p><p data-fusion-component="IHOKUDZ6MZF47PUQMXSBBPQLRM"></p><p>“These cameras were constantly scanning and recording regular folks just going about their day,” Shirley said in a Facebook post. “That kind of permanent, warrantless tracking doesn’t belong in a free society.”</p><p>Shirley said residents should not have to wonder whether their movements are being logged and stored. He directed Flock Safety to retrieve its equipment from the county.</p><figure><figcaption><span>Hood County Constable John D. Shirley covers a Flock surveillance camera</span><span>(Hood County Constable, Precinct 2)</span></figcaption></figure><p>The move comes amid growing national scrutiny of Flock Safety, an Atlanta-based surveillance technology company that operates a network of nearly 100,000 automated license plate readers nationwide.</p><p>In recent months, <a href="https://www.kbtx.com/2026/08/13/flock-announces-several-changes-after-months-investigatetv-reporting-continued-backlash/" rel="" target="_self" title="https://www.kbtx.com/2026/08/13/flock-announces-several-changes-after-months-investigatetv-reporting-continued-backlash/">reports</a> have revealed dozens of cases in which law enforcement officers misused the company’s license plate camera network, in some instances to stalk women. The reports prompted Flock CEO Garrett Langley to issue a <a href="https://www.kbtx.com/2026/08/14/flock-ceo-apologizes-misuse-surveillance-technology/" rel="" target="_self" title="https://www.kbtx.com/2026/08/14/flock-ceo-apologizes-misuse-surveillance-technology/">public apology</a>.</p><p></p><p>“I apologize. It kills me that [the victim] went through that,” Langley told CBS News. He defended the company’s technology but acknowledged that “we could have done more earlier.”</p><p>Following the backlash, <a href="https://www.kbtx.com/2026/08/13/flock-announces-several-changes-after-months-investigatetv-reporting-continued-backlash/" rel="noreferrer" target="_blank" title="https://www.kbtx.com/2026/08/13/flock-announces-several-changes-after-months-investigatetv-reporting-continued-backlash/">Flock announced several changes to its system</a>, including reducing the default data retention period for license plate information from 30 days to seven days, requiring a case number for all searches and implementing an automated tool to flag suspicious search activity.</p><p>The American Civil Liberties Union (ACLU) called the shortened retention period “a step in the right direction” but warned that the changes could amount to a public-relations move, adding that the true impact would depend on how the company’s long-term storage exceptions are implemented.</p><p>Critics have also raised concerns about racial and political profiling, non-law enforcement access to cameras and data privacy vulnerabilities. Because most jurisdictions do not require a warrant for police to search a license plate or track someone’s movements over time, some have questioned the constitutional legitimacy of the technology.</p><p>Flock Safety has not responded to Hood County’s announcement.</p></section></div>
