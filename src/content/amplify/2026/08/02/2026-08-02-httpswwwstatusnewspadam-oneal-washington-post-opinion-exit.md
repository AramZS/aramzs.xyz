<p>In the days leading up to his shocking exit last week, <strong>Adam O’Neal</strong> gave no indication he was on his way out as <strong>The Washington Post’s </strong>Opinion editor. In recent private conversations, <em>Status</em> has learned, he struck precisely the opposite tone, sounding triumphant and boasting that he had pushed the paper’s storied opinion section further to the right. People who spoke with O’Neal told <em>Status</em> he appeared energized by his work at the newspaper and were stunned when he abruptly announced his departure.</p>
<p>O’Neal was known for striking such confident tones in private. As <em>Status </em>previously <a href="https://www.status.news/p/washington-post-opinion-cuts-adam-oneal">reported</a>, he told confidants last year that he expected to remain at the <strong>Jeff Bezos</strong>-owned newspaper for decades—going as far as to declare that it would likely be his final job in journalism.</p>
<p>On Friday, his own prediction collapsed. By The Post’s account, O’Neal’s decision to exit was entirely his own. He portrayed it that way in his farewell memo, writing that he’d led the section “as it began a new chapter”—even if he didn’t stay to finish the book. Current chief executive <strong>Jeff D’Onofrio </strong>struck a similar tone in his own memo, thanking O’Neal for the changes he made. By that telling, everything was going according to plan. Then, on a quiet summer Friday afternoon, O’Neal simply decided it was time to move on.</p>
<p>Of course, there is likely more to the story. For one, the timing lines up with a couple of pressure points: O’Neal’s one-year mark on the job, and department budget proposals that <em>Status</em> has learned are due in just weeks, a process that typically brings sharper scrutiny of spending across the newsroom.</p>
<p>For their part, staffers received the official explanation shortly after O’Neal announced his departure. During an 11:30 am all-hands meeting, he told Opinion staffers that he was leaving for personal family reasons, I’m told. But his explanation only deepened the confusion inside the section, given how sharply it contrasted with O’Neal’s posture in the days and weeks prior.</p>
<p>O'Neal did not respond to a request for comment on his abrupt departure, nor did a spokesperson for Bezos. A Post spokesperson did not provide a comment.</p>
<p>Of course, O'Neal's willingness to aggressively execute Bezos’ vision for the Opinion section was precisely why he had been handpicked for the job by the billionaire owner and then-publisher <strong>Will Lewis</strong>. Before arriving at The Post, the 34-year-old had limited management experience, having worked as a correspondent at <strong>The Economist</strong> and an editorial writer at <strong>The Wall Street Journal</strong>. He also spent a rocky year as executive editor of <strong>The Dispatch</strong>, where his tenure drew internal <a href="https://www.status.news/p/adam-oneal-washington-post-opinion">criticism</a>, as <em>Status</em> previously reported.</p>
<p>Over the last year, O'Neal sought to <a href="https://www.status.news/p/washington-post-opinion-cuts-adam-oneal">transform</a> the Opinion section into the embodiment of Bezos’ mandate to champion "free markets and personal liberties.” He oversaw <a href="https://www.status.news/p/washington-post-opinion-exodus-adam-oneal">layoffs</a>, brought in conservative voices, expanded video content, and steered the department toward a more overtly contrarian identity—one that repeatedly drew backlash inside the newsroom. In his Friday memo, O’Neal specifically thanked both Bezos andD’Onofrio and argued that the strategy was beginning to pay off.</p>
<p>“During my first six months, Opinions users more than doubled their time spent on the site; editorial pageviews grew by triple-digits year over year; and both subscription starts and entrances from search ticked up,” he wrote.</p>
<p>Bezos, for his part, has pushed the newsroom to make data a more central part of its editorial decision-making. And it's fair to wonder whether O’Neal’s numbers held up as well behind closed doors as they did in his own telling.</p>
<p>That gap was most apparent in O'Neal's awkward push into video, an initiative that has struggled to gain meaningful traction. The Post invested in a studio setup for the opinion section's<strong> "Make It Make Sense"</strong> podcast, with the video gear alone costing the paper $80,000, as <em>Status</em> has <a href="https://www.status.news/p/washington-post-opinion-video-adam-oneal">reported</a>, as part of a broader effort to build a personality-driven video operation. The investment became a source of frustration among newsroom staffers after layoffs decimated their ranks in February. Despite the expense, the show's <strong>YouTube</strong> channel has attracted only about 1,600 subscribers since launch. Some employees have also questioned the <a href="https://www.status.news/p/washington-post-opinion-podcast-adam-o-neal">strategy</a> behind producing culture-war programming in an already crowded conservative media ecosystem. One staffer bluntly called the project "a total embarrassment."</p>
<p>In any case, O’Neal’s departure now leaves the Opinion section wondering what comes next—and whether Bezos’ mandated ideological overhaul will continue under a new leader. In his own Friday memo, D’Onofrio wrote that “over the coming weeks, we will work to transition Post Opinions leadership,” while praising O'Neal for making the section “more balanced in perspective, more financially sustainable and more accessible to readers than ever before.”</p>
<p>Multiple staffers told <em>Status</em> that they believe deputy opinion editor <strong>James Hohmann</strong> is among the most likely internal candidates to succeed O'Neal. But Hohmann’s prominent role alongside O'Neal on the "Make It Make Sense" podcast has led some staffers to wonder whether he would continue the same controversial experiments—or end them.</p>
<p>Meanwhile, in a notable move, The Post published its own <a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/business/2026/07/31/adam-oneal-departs-post-opinions-editor-after-year/">news story</a> on Friday announcing O’Neal’s departure, something the newsroom did not do when Lewis departed his role as chief executive and publisher earlier this year after the publication declared it would no longer cover itself.</p>
<p>The reaction to the story was telling. There appeared to be no love lost for O’Neal among many Post readers. By Sunday afternoon, more than 500 comments had accumulated beneath the article, with many celebrating the news. "Good riddance to bad rubbish," one commenter wrote. Another declared that “the destruction of the Post's opinion section took no time at all," while a third put it more bluntly: "No other way to put it, Adam, but you failed."</p>
