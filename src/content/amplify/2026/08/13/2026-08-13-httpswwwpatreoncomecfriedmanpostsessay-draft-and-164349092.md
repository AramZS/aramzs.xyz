<h1>[Essay Draft] - Swords Against, “unplayable” games, and the history of factual fictions</h1>

<p>I’ve recently become really excited by games and playful objects that claim to be “recovered.“ We can think of Elizabeth Little's <a href="https://liz-shrikestudio.itch.io/you-will-die-in-this-place-free-preview"><em>You Will Die In This Place</em></a> (2025) which is "framed as the reconstruction of a lost and unfinished indie game, pieced together from fragments of notes, design documents, and personal reflections." I recently wrote a forward to an upcoming project by Asa Donald, whose solo game <a href="https://www.backwardstabletop.com/spine/"><em>SPINE</em></a> (2025) is yet another example.</p>
<p>I've been fascinated with the blurriness between fact and fiction my whole career. My time in graduate school coincided with the literary world grappling with various hoaxes and frauds. This included <a href="https://www.bbc.com/culture/article/20190424-revisiting-jt-leroy-americas-greatest-literary-scam">JT Leroy,</a> the pen name -- and more -- of Laura Albert, a straight cisgender white woman who claimed to be a trans, HIV-positive teen runaway sex worker in order to add verisimilitude to her published fiction. She had her then-sister-in-law Savannah Knoop play JT for public appearances and readings, Albert herself present as either "JT"'s assistant Speedie, complete with a faux-British accent, or as friend Emily Frasier where her accent wouldn't escape scrutiny.</p>
<p>At the same time, I also happened to be up close to a similar act of authorial cosplay that has to this day never been made public outside of a tight ring of people, because it took place not in commercial circles, but fannish ones. And within academia my classmates in creative writing were debating the definition and best practices of the rising form of <a href="https://creativenonfiction.org/what-is-cnf/">creative non-fiction</a>, what latitude is allowed when drawing from real life.</p>
<p>And I was reading every 18th century novel I could get my hands on.</p>
<p>It's all more similar than you'd think. ...</p>

<p><a href="https://www.patreon.com/ecfriedman/posts/faq-ttrpgs-in-i-160216222">FAQ: TTRPGs in Higher Education: What I do &amp; don't do (and who DOES)</a></p>
<p><a href="https://www.patreon.com/ecfriedman/posts/faq-ttrpgs-in-i-160216222">June 13</a></p>
<p>Emails, I get emails.</p>
<p>It's really cool: I hear from all kinds of folks, doing all kinds of things. Sometimes they want me to be involved, or are looking for advice.</p>
<p>So this is the first in what may be a series of Frequently Asked Questions, where I prepare thoughtful and nuanced answers to queries I get a lot, in the hopes that I can help more folks. These will be completely open, and so I hope that folks feel free to use the comments to provide further help to those querying.</p>
<h3>A Note for Students</h3>
<p>For those interested in degree programs, <a href="https://ourglasslake.com/resources/">Bo Ruberg is still very useful</a>. You'll want to check on any individual program to see if folks have moved around (or their research interests have shifted), but it's still what I point to first.</p>
<p>For a variety of reasons, I don't actively recruit directly as a supervisor, except in very specific situations where my colleagues and I can build a solid supervising team. Our department has existing strengths that can intersect with Game Studies, but the institution does not have a clear suite of related courses and faculty.</p>
<p>That said, I have served as an external examiner or committee member for many institutions worldwide, in the US, UK, and NZ.</p>
<h2>What do we mean when we say "TTRPGs in Education"?</h2>
<p>"TTRPGs in Education" means a lot of different things. It can mean:</p>
<ul>
<li>
<p>studying game-related media</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>studying games themselves</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>studying the impact of games (at individual or collective level)</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>teaching people how to make games</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>using games as educational tools</p>
</li>
</ul>
<p>There's almost surely more, but most stuff fits under these umbrellas.</p>
<p>For those interested, Pascal Martinolli published a <a href="https://pmartinolli.github.io/JDR50bibliometrie/index-english.html">meta-analysis of Tabletop Roleplaying Studies in 2025 from 1980s-2024</a>, which gives a good birds-eye view of the field.</p>
<p>Also just out (and free to read Open Access) is a new book on <a href="https://books.uu.se/uup/catalog/book/55">Transformative Roleplaying Games</a> in the classroom.</p>
<p>So here's where I sit with each of these practices:</p>
<h3>Studying Game-Related Media and Playful Media</h3>
<p><strong>Skill Level: Expert</strong> <strong>Interest: High</strong></p>
<p>This is what I do, under the umbrella of "Playful Media." I am interested in the history and production of these kinds of works, the people who make them, etc.</p>
<p>I have particular interest in:</p>
<ul>
<li>
<p>Actual Play (the recorded, live-streamed, or theatrical practice of documenting roleplaying games)</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Documented Play (other forms of remembering specific gameplay sessions)</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Games criticism and journalism (history and practice)</p>
</li>
</ul>
<p>I am less focused on, but still interested in hearing about:</p>
<ul>
<li>
<p>Media "inspired" by games ( <em>Baldur's Gate, Stranger Things</em>, etc)</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Media devised via games like Japanese replays ( <em>Records of Lodoss War</em>, etc) or scripts devised using roleplaying games</p>
</li>
</ul>
<h3>Studying Games</h3>
<p><strong>Skill Level: Moderate-to-Advanced</strong> <strong>Interest: High</strong></p>
<p>I am profoundly interested in the study of games, though I would not claim as much experience in doing so. I am still catching up in a lot of ways in terms of gameplay experience, past theoretical debates, etc. In order to do my work it becomes necessary for me to do a lot of this. My work at the Folger will involve a fair amount of this work in Early Modern (pre-1800) games.</p>
<p>See also:</p>
<ul>
<li>
<p><a href="https://analoggamestudies.org/"><em>Analog Game Studies</em></a></p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Hamish Cameron and Dylan Horrocks's <a href="https://www.visualnarrativelab.com/">The Visual Narrative Laboratory</a> (Victoria University)</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Emily Morris, <a href="https://queerttrpgstudies.com/">Queer TTRPG Studies</a></p>
</li>
</ul>
<h3>Studying the Impact of Games</h3>
<p><strong>Skill Level: Moderate-to-Advanced</strong> <strong>Interest: Moderate-to-High</strong></p>
<p>This is something I'm wildly supportive of! And I have very little direct experience with. Contacting me will likely get you put in touch with different groups. I can strategize about how to reach the specifically-online parts of games scenes, and give helpful caution about the limitations of online-only research.</p>
<p>Work in this part of TTRPG/Analog Game Studies tends to intersect with sociology, psychology, as well as emotion studies.</p>
<h3>Teaching People How To Make Games</h3>
<p><strong>Skill level: Novice</strong> <strong>Interest: Enthusastic</strong></p>
<p>Another thing that I'm very interested in, but am verymuch in "beginner's mind," so I won't be as helpful as some other colleagues. I can point to folks like <a href="https://sharangbiswas.myportfolio.com/">Sharang Biswas</a>, <a href="https://vidityavoleti.com/">Viditya Voleti,</a> both of whom work as primarily analog-experts in programs that are heavily digital. "Game Studies" programs tend to start with analog but be dominated by digital.</p>
<h3>Using Games as Educational Tools</h3>
<p><strong>Skill Level: Varies</strong> <strong>Interest: Varies</strong></p>
<p>So this is where it gets a little complicated.</p>
<p>I am categorically uninterested in <strong>gamification</strong>, the process by which games are created to achieve specific learning effects. I don't think games really work like that, and when they do, I'm unclear on how different they are from other incentive structures. If you want information from me about gamification, I'm unlikely to be of much use, either directly or via contacts.</p>
<p>That said, I am more engaged and have experience including in my classes projects like <a href="https://cmichpress.com/product-category/physical-game/">Central Michigan UP's peer-reviewed games</a>. These games have support for classroom use and mappable Student Learning Outcomes but are intended (like other classroom materials) for engagement, analysis, critique, rather than content delivery. These games are both playtested and put through subject-matter peer review.</p>
<p>There is a ton of scholarship in this field -- the <a href="https://pmartinolli.github.io/JDR50bibliometrie/index-english.html">meta-analysis I cited at the start of this post</a> lists "learning" as the largest broad level subject heading among academic publications.</p>
<h3>TTRPGs and Psychology/Therapy</h3>
<p><strong>Skill Level: None</strong> <strong>Interest: None (Professionally)</strong></p>
<p>"Psychology" is the second most common field of "applied" TTRPGs, of which I suspect (based on anecdata, not systematic analysis) is heavily therapeutic -- a related cousin in terms of research, and definitely outside of my field. <a href="https://www.game-therapy.co.uk/">Game Therapy UK</a>, <a href="https://therapeuticgamemaster.com/">Geek Therapeutics</a> work in evidence-based approaches and continuing education, and <a href="https://www.ttrpgkids.com/2024/09/02/intentionally-therapeutic-ttrpgs-and-ttrpg-resources/">TTRPGKids has a list of related projects</a> (with caveats). Shaun Garea's <a href="https://grailgamingresearch.wordpress.com/">GRAIL</a> in Aotearoa/New Zealand is also involved in this work.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.patreon.com/ecfriedman/posts/field-notes-164751790">Field Notes: Adventuring Academy</a></p>
<p>August 3</p>
<p>Hello! You may be wondering how a professor gets on <em>Adventuring Academy</em>.</p>
<p>I "met" Brennan in 2022, when I was writing for <em>Polygon</em> and Dropout contacted me with a mid-season screener and an invitation to interview Brennan about it. That was unusual enough to pique my interest, and if you know <em>Neverafter,</em> you know which episode it was. ( <a href="https://www.polygon.com/23509083/dimension-20-neverafter-episode-3-brennan-lee-mulligan-interview/">Spoilers ahoy</a>). In 2024 we met for coffee that extended into lunch when I did field work in LA, and he's been one of my reliable contacts in Actual Play ever since.</p>
<p>During that first lunch, we arranged for me to visit The Dome with producer <a href="https://ebonyelaine.com/">Ebony Hardin</a> and <a href="https://thatsrickperry.com/">Rick Perry,</a> and in passing Brennan mentioned <em>Adventuring Academy</em>. I was braced for "LA people Say Stuff" and filed it away as a Nice Thing That Was Said, and yes-and-ed by recommending a colleague I'd recently met that Brennan had mentioned as "fucking funny." But I filed it away: my book was nowhere near done, for one.</p>
<p>In December of that year, I was on a call with Brennan where he said "of course you can..." to something and I retorted "you say 'of course you can' to outlandish things! You also said of course I should be on <em>Adventuring Academy!</em>"</p>
<p>"You should!"</p>
<p>"OK, so explain to me how it works..."</p>

This year any income that doesn't go right back out to other folks in the TTRPG and games field will allow me to having sliding scale speaking fees (or waive them) and support other public-facing projects that I'm developing where I need a little help from talented and skilled folks.</p>
