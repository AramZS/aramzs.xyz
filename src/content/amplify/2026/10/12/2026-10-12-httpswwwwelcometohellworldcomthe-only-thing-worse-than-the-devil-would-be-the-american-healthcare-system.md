<p>Sean T. Collins joins us again today to write about the recent series The Terror: Devil in Silver, whose central metaphor about being trapped in the "heath care system" in this country and dealing with mysterious malevolent forces will be familiar to all of us.</p>
<p>He also talks to showrunner Chris Cantwell (also the co-creator of the great Halt and Catch Fire) about trying to strike the right balance of evil and hope in the series.</p>
<p>"This idea of complacency is very resonant and potent today," Cantwell says. "You wake up every day, and here’s the situation at hand: How do I address that? Can I address that? Do I have any agency to do so? How do I just survive from the morning until when I can crash into the bed at night, and hopefully make it another day? Does that make me complicit in some way in the system that perpetuates over all our heads and grinds us up into meat fuel?"</p>

<h2>At-Risk Communities</h2>
<p><em>by Sean T. Collins</em></p>
<p><em>“I am done calling for help. I used to think this system was broken, and people out there just didn’t know. But now I realize nothing is broken. The system is working perfectly. That’s why they never fix it.”<br/>
—Coffee, The Terror: Devil in Silver</em></p>
<p>The Terror: Devil in Silver is like Franz Kafka’s The Shining. Adapted by novelist Victor LaValle from his novel The Devil in Silver in collaboration with showrunner Chris Cantwell (Halt and Catch Fire), this bleak and brilliant eight-episode series (on AMC, AMC+, and Shudder) is also about a small group of people trapped in an evil place. But it’s not a snowy winter that keeps its protagonists prisoner. It’s the NYPD and the American mental healthcare system. Snow melts. The system does not.</p>
<p>Devil in Silver stars Dan Stevens (Downton Abbey) as Peter “Pepper” Coffin, a heavy metal drummer from Queens entering early middle age in the <em>still wears a band t-shirt every day</em> way so many of us do. He’s full of energy, hence the Pepper nickname — air drumming, working his day job as a mover, trying to start up a drum-lesson side hustle. He’s everyone’s best friend. “I help people,” he explains helpfully.</p>
<p>Pepper’s a white guy with a Latina girlfriend, Marisol (Juani Feliz). She’s got a daughter he dotes on to a financially irresponsible degree, buying her a $4,000 drum kit on a whim and wiping out their savings in the process. He’s the kind of man who thinks he’s a hero because he can trick a kid into looking at him like one.</p>
<p>And he smiles a lot but he’s angry, this guy. He’s quick with his fists, hoping this too makes him look like a hero. He breaks up an argument between Marisol and her shitty ex by attacking the guy. Then he goes after the bystanders who try to break it up, and who happen to be a trio of undercover cops. Just like that, he goes from white knighting to getting arrested for assaulting an officer.</p>
<p>But it’s late, and the cops’ shift is almost over, and they’re tired, and they don’t want to fill out the paperwork. So — because they can, and because they have a deal with the place’s management — they drop him off at New Hyde Hospital, a run-down psychiatric facility in the only wing of a massive building that’s otherwise been completely closed down.</p>
<p>Pepper is placed on an involuntary 72-hour hold. He’s doped up with meds, knocked out cold by them, and marked noncompliant because he doesn’t wake up to take more meds. His mandatory stay is extended to two weeks. An escape attempt, a physical altercation, a bad attitude: The more he fights to get out, the longer he is forced to remain.</p>
