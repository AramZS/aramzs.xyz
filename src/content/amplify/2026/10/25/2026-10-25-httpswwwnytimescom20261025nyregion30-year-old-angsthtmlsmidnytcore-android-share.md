<p>Troy Closson, who covers young adults, spoke to more than two dozen economists, experts on adulthood and people in their early 30s.</p>
<p>A young woman in Queens celebrated her 30th birthday with cake, champagne — and an existential crisis over still living paycheck to paycheck.</p>
<p>A 30-year-old in Manhattan felt ready to consider marriage, but knew he couldn’t afford a wedding.</p>
<p>And in Brooklyn, a couple, at 32, confronted a generational conundrum: stay in their hometown as their parents age? Or move away to start a family?</p>
<p>Forget the kids.</p>
<p>The 30-somethings are not all right.</p>
<p>“Everyone I know around my age is feeling the weight of the burdens of this economy and what it takes to live a nice, comfortable middle-class life,” said Dan Iammatteo, 31, a marketing director in the Bedford-Stuyvesant neighborhood of Brooklyn.</p>
<p>And their angst is intensifying. People in their early 30s constitute the largest age cohort in the United States, and their frustrations have emerged as a potent force reshaping cities and their politics and demographics.</p>
<p>In New York, this age group turbocharged the ascent of the Democratic Socialists of America, propelling to office Zohran Mamdani, who turned 35 this month, and a slate of progressive allies to congressional primary wins.</p>
