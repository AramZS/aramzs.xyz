<p>An admission: I came to <em>You Can See Everything</em> almost blind. I knew little about Elizabeth Holmes and nothing at all about co-director Nathan Fielder (co-director Lance Oppenheim I knew from <em>Some Kind of Heaven</em> and <a href="https://humanizingthevacuum.wordpress.com/2026/10/13/to-catch-a-hypocrite-primetime/">the feature film </a><em>Primetime</em>). Holmes, a classic grifter who wheedled money out of <a href="https://www.theguardian.com/books/2023/jan/10/george-shultz-biography-theranos-elizabeth-holmes">doddering patriarchs</a> by hawking a blood testing contraption, is the subject of a three-hour documentary made with the full cooperation of her and partner Billy Evans in the hopes of, I don’t know, charming an audience with what she calls “radical transparency.”</p>
<p>With his greying parted hair and steady glower, Fielder turns out to be a relentless interlocutor if not inquisitor. After I watched <em>You Can See Everything</em> I acquainted myself with a couple episodes of <em>Nathan For You</em>, where the Canadian parodies the patter of marketers and salesmen. In this one he interrogates the marketers and salesmen themselves, exposing them as charlatans besotted with their bullshit because the bullshit compensates for a lack of conviction. There is no way to soak into <em>You Can See Everything</em>‘s generous run time without asking, “Holmes and Evans knew what they were getting into when they asked Fielder to film them, right? Right?” To chuckle as they get their comeuppance is as American as falling for the scam: Fielder and Oppenheim might have called their film <em>To Catch a Predator</em>.</p>
<p>But the metal level at which <em>You Can See Everything</em> works is less interesting than the filmmakers think. People born in the 21st century have grown up with phone cameras and social media posting videos of themselves laughing it up at their best friend Tyler’s birthday party. They know they’re performing for an audience; they’re selling gaiety and companionship; when they tag a location they’re literally promoting that business. <em>Of course</em> Holmes is promoting a version of herself, therefore the question about whether she actually said “They’re not playing me, they’re playing a character I created” to a <em>New York Times</em> reporter instead of “a character they created” doesn’t strike me as epochal (the newspaper stands by its reporting). I suppose it matters insofar as Holmes, if we believe the story, was aware about being self-aware. And Holmes, who at times resembles <a href="https://hannibal.fandom.com/wiki/Catherine_Martin">a young Brooke Smith</a>, gets the full Fielder treatment, imprisoned in suffocating closeups so that her frozen smiles call to mind Robert Blake’s mirthless grin in<em> Lost Highway</em>. As the countdown to the beginning of her 11-year prison sentence ticks (the film starts at the 34-day mark), her insistence on her innocence and on the continued viability of Theranos as she dotes on her and Evans’ infant children takes on the aura of prayers mumbled in the darkness.</p>
<p>“Weren’t you worried she was scamming you?” Fielder asks Evans early in the film. “A hundred percent, but what does she have to gain from me?” he responds with the assured perkiness of <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/17/movies/billy-evans-elizabeth-holmes-nathan-fielder.html">a scion of wealth</a> who for thirty years has probably gotten a 100 percent of what he wanted. Evans is the more familiar yet in a way more fascinating hustler in this film. A curly-haired fitness nut with manosphere good looks whose penis Fielder and Oppenheim include in a shower shot that discomforted my audience in that classic (and familiar) American way, Evans babbles about his and Holmes’ symbiosis: like John said about Yoko, they’re the same person. On and on this unblinking homunculus talks, convinced Theranos 2.0 has a future if they get it right.</p>
<p>A conventional documentary would’ve ended with Billy and his in-laws delivering Holmes to prison; but <em>You Can See Everything</em> doesn’t get going until the actress who played Holmes appears as Greek chorus and protagonist. Fresh off <em>The Dropout</em>, Amanda Seyfried carries herself as a famous person who can still buy a bag of Fritos at the Safeway. Fielder, with whom she has the uninhibited rapport of old friends, uses her as sounding board for his theories about Holmes and Evans, for after all Seyfried would have some insight into this person. This section pivots around a meeting before filming started at which Evans, Fielder, Oppenheim, and others negotiate terms. Holmes leaves the room; Evans demands a cut of the profits. But it’s not the real Holmes and Evans we watch but Seyfried and her <a href="https://humanizingthevacuum.wordpress.com/2025/12/11/the-testament-of-anna-lacks-the-fervor-of-its-subject/"><em>Testament of Ann Lee</em> costar</a> Christopher Abbott playing them as if in a high-gloss <em>Unsolved Mysteries</em> reenactment. Observing how eerily Seyfried magnifies Holmes’ coy blankness, I thought, <em>this is the Elizabeth Holmes whom Elizabeth Holmes hoped to be and never can be</em>; it’s as fascinating as Seyfried acting the part of Amanda Seyfried listening to Fiedler. The film comes to a head when at an improvised chill night at Evans’ Texas home with the kids Evans bares his teeth at last. He trashes Seyfried for her dishonest portrayal of Holmes in <em>The Dropout</em> and, as if we needed confirmation of his politics, for <a href="https://www.gq-magazine.co.uk/article/amanda-seyfrieds-harvest-moon">her comments</a> on the late Charlie Kirk. Thicker around the neck, punctuating sentences with sniffles, wiping his nose, Evans looks like he’s been hitting what George Harrison called the Bolivian marching powder. His delusions are no longer for grandeur — they’re the delusions of an obsolescent influencer.</p>
<p><em>You Can See Everything</em> will be a hit. My 11 a.m. screening yesterday was three-quarters full: unusual for a Saturday morning feature and unheard of for a documentary. The audience was hip to it; for the first time in memory we laughed at the right moments, including when good ol’ Bill defended Kirk. Stories about the doc’s structure and the continued interest in Holmes as personality account for the curiosity, I suppose. Fielder, Oppenheim, and editor Adam Locke-Norton held my attention for 174 minutes, no mean feat, even if Locke-Norton includes a hard cut too many to the Happy Family wading in the Pacific — we get it, they’re human beings. If Orson Welles’ <em>F For Fake</em> handled its similar ideas a half century ago with more formal play and with its director’s characteristic puckishness, its subject wasn’t as symptomatic of capitalism’s rot.</p>
