<p>Do you need the best of the Internet (Wikipedia, OpenStreetMap, Khan Academy, Stack Exchange, ETC) with local content <i>and</i> learning apps for all?</p>
<p>Customize an "Offline Internet" for your school, for your community — or for your very own family!</p>
<p>Listen to the <a href="https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/w3cszv1s">23min BBC podcast "How to put the internet in a box"</a> (2020-10-20) to learn how this works. See also a <a href="https://youtu.be/HkeS_EAIpv4">great community example in North India</a> (3min) from 2021. Internet-in-a-Box (IIAB) is used in medical clinics as well, as can be seen in this <a href="https://youtu.be/xI3Ed008Mxo">Dominican Republic video</a>.</p>
<p>Internet-in-a-Box brings the power of a free Digital Library of Alexandria to any community worldwide.</p>
<p><i>Whether costing $10 or $1000, an Internet-in-a-Box "learning hotspot" brings classroom/library devices to life, in ways that very deeply empower learning and community.</i></p>
<p>RECAP: To <a href="https://opensource.com/article/17/5/internet-in-a-box-raspberry-pi">create your own Internet-in-a-Box</a> [<a href="https://people.sugarlabs.org/~icarito/newsletter_sugarlabs/ProofSLNewsP4.psf.pdf">1</a>] [<a href="https://people.sugarlabs.org/~icarito/newsletter_sugarlabs/ProofSLNewsP5.psf.pdf">2</a>], look over a few <a href="https://wiki.iiab.io/go/FAQ#Where_can_I_see_live_demos_of_Internet-in-a-Box?">live demo examples</a> and check out our <b><a href="https://www.youtube.com/channel/UC0cBGCxr_WPBPa3IqPVEe3g">growing collection of videos</a></b>.</p>
<p>Internet-in-a-Box arose from One Laptop Per Child's original school server (2007-2012) and our <a href="http://olpcMAP.net">global grassroots community</a>, adding in the <a href="https://internet-in-a-box.org/#quality-content">quality content and learning apps</a> shared by the best educational systems worldwide.</p>
<p>Now anyone can do this, building up your local community's very own 21st Century digital library, using free and open source software.</p>
<p>READY, SET, INSTALL: Internet-in-a-Box (IIAB) is built by a <a href="https://internet-in-a-box.org/contributing.html">global network of professional volunteers</a>, inspired by <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_Laptop_per_Child">One Laptop Per Child's</a> famous laptops and their innovative <a href="https://sugarlabs.org">Sugar Learning Platform</a> — <i>but now with a much wider vision of serving all — both in schools and beyond.</i></p>

<h2>Where is Internet-in-a-Box used?</h2>
<p>Internet-in-a-Box (IIAB) is in use in dozens of countries, from smaller communities to large-scale institutional deployments.</p>
<p>Please see our regular global community updates at: <a href="https://twitter.com/internet_in_box">@internet_in_box</a></p>
<p>Examples include <a href="https://schoolserver.wordpress.com/2013/03/29/my-first-school-server-deployment/">orphanages</a> and schools in <a href="http://olpcMAP.net">Haiti</a>, schools across southern <a href="https://kidsoncomputers.org">Mexico</a>, rural communities and libraries across southwest <a href="https://ghanatogether.blogspot.com/">Ghana</a>, remote schools in <a href="https://100pilabs.school.blog/">Myanmar/Burma</a> and Cambodia, villages across <a href="https://medium.com/eka-foundation">India</a>, a girls science school in <a href="https://www.rwandagirlsinitiative.org/ggast/">Rwanda</a>, community centers in <a href="https://www.hyracbox.com/">Kenya</a>, a youth community in rural <a href="https://olpcbasecamp.blogspot.com/">Malaysia</a>, to <a href="https://www.wakoma.co/">South Africa</a> townships — as well as broad <a href="https://github.com/germanrs/xsce">Central American deployments</a> like <a href="https://fundacionzt.org/">Nicaragua</a>, Costa Rica, and the Dominican Republic.</p>
<p>Libraries and rural medical clinics are also avid users of IIAB, in places like <a href="https://healthpolicy.fsi.stanford.edu/research/guatemala-children-crisis">Guatemala</a>, the <a href="https://youtu.be/xI3Ed008Mxo">Dominican Republic</a>.</p>
<p>Many <a href="https://internet-in-a-box.org/contributing.html">contributors</a> are also using IIAB for more personal/grassroots and home library purposes too, helping refine our community product for increasingly diverse and larger impacts.</p>

However this "usb" folder is No Longer Required as of May 2018 / IIAB 6.5 (by default!) If however you prefer the old approach, please set <code>iiab_usb_lib_show_all: False</code> in <a href="https://wiki.iiab.io/go/FAQ#What_is_local_vars.yml_and_how_do_I_customize_it?">/etc/iiab/local_vars.yml</a> prior to installing IIAB.</li> <li>Note that all Linux users should be able to read and write to USB drives inserted into IIAB as of 2021-03-25 (<a href="https://github.com/iiab/iiab/pull/2715">PR #2715</a>). <p>Internet-in-a-Box (IIAB) is free and open source software that runs on many GNU/Linux <a href="https://github.com/iiab/iiab/wiki/IIAB-Platforms">platforms</a>:</p>
<ul><li><b>The Raspberry Pi 4 ($35+), Raspberry Pi 5 ($45+) and Raspberry Pi 500 ($90 computer-in-keyboard) are best for classrooms, in almost all cases!</b> Their internal Wi-Fi hotspot supports up to 32 student WiFi client devices (but see <a href="https://github.com/iiab/iiab/issues/823#issuecomment-662285202">#823</a> for the very latest Wi-Fi firmware info!) <i>RECOMMENDATION: Buy any Raspberry Pi with at least 2 GB RAM, especially if your Internet-in-a-Box will include <a href="https://github.com/iiab/iiab/tree/master/roles/kolibri#kolibri-readme">Kolibri</a>, <a href="https://docs.moodle.org/en/About_Moodle_FAQ">Moodle</a> or <a href="https://github.com/iiab/iiab/tree/master/roles/nextcloud#nextcloud-readme">Nextcloud</a>.</i> <ul><li>The older <b>Raspberry Pi 3</b> and <b>3 B+</b> ($35+) are also possible, despite their 1GB RAM limitation.</li> <li>Finally the <b>Raspberry Pi Zero W</b> ($10) and <b>Raspberry Pi Zero 2 W</b> ($15) work great as learning kiosks, despite their meager 512MB RAM (Internet-in-a-Box is used in rural medical clinics, and by families in their homes). NOTE that your microSD card should be prepared with IIAB software and provisioned with Content in a more powerful Raspberry Pi — then later insert the microSD into your Zero (2) W. (It continues to astonish us how powerful these tiny computers really are, the size of a cigarette lighter yet able to serve 32 students simultaneously, with their powerful built-in Wi-Fi!)</li></ul></li></ul>
<ul><li><b>Refurbished "PC" Laptops &amp; Thin Clients:</b> When recycled / upcycled conscientiously, quality donated laptops and PC's can work extremely well as your Internet-in-a-Box, becoming a "Learning Hotspot" or server for an entire developing world classroom or small school. If the <a href="https://wiki.iiab.io/go/FAQ#Can_I_create_a_Wi-Fi_hotspot_using_an_old_laptop?">laptop's internal Wi-Fi works as an access point</a>, this allows for a hassle-free self-contained unit, as is greatly preferred by most developing world schools (to control operational costs, spaghetti wiring, theft of router, etc). Conversely if the laptop's internal Wi-Fi <i>cannot</i> be used as an access point, an external Wi-Fi router can be made to work if truly necessary. In any case, make sure the laptop has a reasonably new battery, acting as a de facto <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uninterruptible_power_supply">UPS</a> (critically important in developing world schools where electricity comes and goes). Naturally: laptops with large hard disks and multiple GB of memory are preferred — definitely check that the laptop runs 64-bit Linux well, preferably with a BIOS that permits it to run while the screen is closed.</li></ul>
<ul><li><b>Mini PC's like the ASUS NUC (acquired from Intel in 2023):</b> Avoid classic/desktop PCs whenever possible, as they consume far too much electricity. However: tough, well-rounded, low-power Mini PCs (such as the NUC) world extremely well, and have proven very effective as school servers in the developing world since 2014. We prefer Mini PC's models that accept 2.5-inch SATA drives (HDD or SSD) and work with DC voltages up to 19V. WARNINGS: <ul><li>The internal Wi-Fi in most NUC computers is regrettably blocked from connecting to more than 12 Wi-Fi client devices. Compare this to the Raspberry Pi's mentioned above — whose internal Wi-Fi can support 32 simultaneous Wi-Fi client devices!</li> <li>Low-end NUC models only work right near 12V, and so are not reliable using lead-acid batteries that you find in most solar installations. Read the spec carefully, for the exact model you're considering!</li> <li>Finally: the NUC is not a fan-less device, but nevertheless has proved very reliable across diverse climates.</li></ul></li></ul>
<ul><li><b>Other Mini PC competitors:</b> <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micro-Star_International">MSI</a> units have proven very solid, and Zotac perhaps less so. Consider also Beelink's NUC clones, and <a href="http://www.gigabyte.us/Mini-PcBarebone">Gigabyte BRIX</a> (e.g. GB-BSI3H-6100) which likewise is almost an exact clone of the NUC. The BRIX allows internal Wi-Fi to scale beyond the NUC's annoying limitation of 12 connections maximum, if you insert an appropriate Atheros Wi-Fi module (typically &lt; $10) into the BRIX. <a href="https://schoolserver.wordpress.com/author/georgejhunt/">George Hunt</a> can answer questions around which Atheros Wi-Fi modules have been tested to work, as of February 2017. <i>Recap: scaling up internal Wi-Fi is sadly not possible with NUCs since 2015 (5th generation NUCs and onwards) as the NUC's Wi-Fi module is unfortunately soldered in.</i></li></ul>
<ul><li><b>ARM Mini PCs:</b> CPUs like <a href="https://www.compulab.com/">TrimSlice / Utilite CompuLab</a> and <a href="https://solid-run.com">Cubox by SolidRun</a> might one day catch up, providing much-needed community momentum and packaging. To date however we have been disappointed with their firmware, and the lack of availability of Linux kernels/drivers working with internal SATA / internal Wi-Fi (e.g. AP mode), <i>to truly make teachers' lives easier</i>. But check back as many more global deployments' experiences accumulate, and rugged low-end hardware increasingly emerges, on OrangePi or similar?</li></ul>
<p>MEMORY: Some do fine with 512MB RAM (even in 2024!) serving static content from the RPi Zero W. Others do just fine serving dozens of simultaneous video streams from an RPi3 (or 3 B+) with 1GB RAM. Finally, certain others require 2GB or 4GB from an RPi 4 — or 8GB or more within a Mini PC — when server-side loads become very intensive. For example if you are running an <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Learning_management_system">LMS</a>, experiencing a lot of OpenStreetMap traffic, running a Wikipedia Treasure Hunt with multiple students running full-text searches of Wikipedia — or offering streaming &amp; fileserver-like capabilities broadly to many students.</p>
<p>STORAGE: While there's increasing movement from hard disks to SSDs and microSD cards every year, many others still swear by tried-and-true hard drives. Certainly rotating media are the clear winner when TB (or multi-terabyte) storage is needed at a low price. In the end all storage technologies can fail, each in there own ways. So it's far more more important to start with the actual community's learning goals, engaging educators around their authentic content/interaction needs — and then narrow in on storage capacity/technologies later. Alternative: consider this earlier discussion of <a href="http://lists.laptop.org/pipermail/server-devel/2013-June/thread.html#6490">external USB hard disks and their possible risks</a>.</p>

<h2>What are the default passwords?</h2>
<p>For <a href="https://wiki.iiab.io/go/FAQ#What_can_I_do_with_E-books_and_Internet-in-a-Box?">KA Lite, Kolibri, Calibre, Calibre-Web, Nextcloud, Moodle, CUPS, Elgg, JupyterHub, Transmission, etc</a> the administrative user is <b>Admin</b> (don't forget the capital A) and the default/initial password is: <b>changeme</b> — WordPress as well, often uses this same account. For MediaWiki however, Admin/changeme2020 is the initial username/password.</p>
<p>An example is Calibre-Web's "teacher" account (Admin/changeme) which can add/remove and convert E-Books, and also change their metadata, at <a href="http://box/books">http://box/books</a>. Whereas Calibre itself (if installed, typically at <a href="http://box:8080">http://box:8080</a>) uses these 7 "student" accounts, to read or download E-Books: box/box, h/h, ht/ht, m/m, mx/mx, p/p or pe/pe (as of Internet-in-a-Box 7.0). If you have access to the Linux command-line, you can change Calibre's accounts by running: calibre-server --manage-users --userdb /library/calibre/users.sqlite</p>
<p>However for IIAB's own <b>Admin Console</b>[*] at <a href="http://box/admin">http://box/admin</a>, <a href="http://box.lan/admin">http://box.lan/admin</a> or <a href="http://10.10.10.10/admin">http://10.10.10.10/admin</a>, the username is <b>iiab-admin</b> and the default/initial password is: <b>g0adm1n</b> (note the numbers 0, 1). GNU/Linux administrators can also use this to login over ssh.</p>
<p><u>Change iiab-admin's password before connecting your server to the Internet.</u> You can do this within <a href="http://box.lan/admin">http://box.lan/admin</a> -&gt; Utilities menu — or at the command line, if you run: <code>passwd iiab-admin</code></p>
<p><i>[*] While the Admin Console can change iiab-admin's password instantly, it's important to understand that many Admin Console changes (within its Configure menu especially) require you click "Save Configuration" then "Install Configured Options" and then wait for this to complete. Monitor for Status "SUCCEEDED" under Utilities menu -&gt; Display Job Status (takes about 20 min on a Raspberry Pi 3, 3 B+ or 4, depending what changes you've requested!)</i></p>
<p>Finally, certain install-time passwords are listed in <a href="https://wiki.iiab.io/go/FAQ#What_is_local_vars.yml_and_how_do_I_customize_it?">/etc/iiab/local_vars.yml</a> — which overrides passwords listed in <a href="https://github.com/iiab/iiab/wiki/IIAB-Architecture#detailed-description-of-ansible-control-variables">default_vars.yml</a>. If you modify any of these passwords in local_vars.yml after installing IIAB, don't forget to then run Ansible, e.g. using "Install Configured Options" above! 2020-09-23 Warning: MySQL/MariaDB passwords cannot easily be changed after IIAB install (<a href="https://github.com/iiab/iiab/issues/2542">#2542</a>).</p>
<p><b>Admin Console:</b> Note that very occasionally it might be necessary to run <code>systemctl restart iiab-cmdsrv.service</code> if <a href="http://box.lan/admin">http://box.lan/admin</a> shows red errors upon login.</p>
<p>Warning: KA Lite's <b>Admin</b> password cannot be changed in a browser (<a href="http://box:8008">http://box:8008</a> can only change KA Lite's user passwords) so you need to run <code>kalite manage changepassword Admin</code> — help is available if you run: <code>kalite manage help changepassword</code></p>

<p>To set a static (fixed) IP address such as 192.168.1.99, use lines like the following in <a href="https://wiki.iiab.io/go/FAQ#What_is_local_vars.yml_and_how_do_I_customize_it?">/etc/iiab/local_vars.yml</a> — and then run <code>sudo iiab-network</code> :</p>
<p>By default this is a FALLBACK static (fixed) IP address, which requires an Ethernet cable and link-light showing. If that cable connects IIAB to an Internet router/cablemodem or similar (that is turned on) IIAB should receive a DHCP IP address within about 30 seconds. Or, if there is no DHCP on the wire, IIAB will set its own IP address (typically on eth0) to 192.168.1.99 per this example, about 45 seconds later.</p>
<p>Specifically, if you keep <code>wan_try_dhcp_before_static_ip: True</code> your /etc/dhcpcd.conf will contain: <i>(on any OS where IIAB <a href="https://github.com/iiab/iiab/blob/fcc8bcc127355ec74ff890522032113722d2597f/roles/network/templates/network/dhcpcd.conf.j2#L71-L88">sets up</a> /etc/dhcpcd.conf)</i></p>
<p>Or if you set <code>wan_try_dhcp_before_static_ip: False</code> your /etc/dhcpcd.conf will contain: <i>(on any OS where IIAB <a href="https://github.com/iiab/iiab/blob/fcc8bcc127355ec74ff890522032113722d2597f/roles/network/templates/network/dhcpcd.conf.j2#L71-L88">sets up</a> /etc/dhcpcd.conf)</i></p>
<pre> auto eth0
 iface eth0 inet static
     address 192.168.1.99
     broadcast 192.168.1.255
     netmask 255.255.255.0
     gateway 192.168.1.254
     dns-nameservers 192.168.1.254
</pre>
<p><i>WHEREAS on Ubuntu,</i> the <code>wan_try_dhcp_before_static_ip</code> variable is currently ignored (i.e. treated as <code>False</code>) and <a href="https://netplan.io/">Netplan</a> stores your static IP address settings from <a href="https://wiki.iiab.io/go/FAQ#What_is_local_vars.yml_and_how_do_I_customize_it?">/etc/iiab/local_vars.yml</a> into <code>/etc/netplan/01-iiab-config.yaml</code> as follows:</p>
<pre> network:
   version: 2
   renderer: networkd
   ethernets:
     enp0s3:
       addresses:
       - 192.168.1.99/24
       gateway4: 192.168.1.254

</pre>

<h2>What is local_vars.yml and how do I customize it?</h2>
<p>To customize your Internet-in-a-Box (IIAB), modify IIAB configuration variables within <b>/etc/iiab/local_vars.yml</b></p>
<p><i>It's best to do this prior to installing IIAB, when IIAB's <a href="https://download.iiab.io/">1-line installer</a> prompts you with "Edit /etc/iiab/local_vars.yml to customize your Internet-in-a-Box? [Y/n]"</i></p>
<p>Three common example local_vars.yml files are posted for comparison (<a href="https://github.com/iiab/iiab/blob/master/vars/local_vars_small.yml">SMALL-sized</a>, <a href="https://github.com/iiab/iiab/blob/master/vars/local_vars_medium.yml">MEDIUM-sized</a>, and <a href="https://github.com/iiab/iiab/blob/master/vars/local_vars_large.yml">LARGE-sized</a>). See also this <a href="https://github.com/iiab/iiab/blob/master/vars/local_vars_medical.yml">medical example</a> used by the <a href="https://meta.wikimedia.org/wiki/Wiki_Project_Med">Wiki Project Med Foundation</a> e.g. for <a href="http://med.iiab.me">http://med.iiab.me</a>. In addition see our bare bones example, very useful for <a href="https://github.com/iiab/iiab/blob/master/vars/local_vars_unittest.yml">unit testing of a single IIAB App</a>.</p>
<p>Customize your IIAB installation (and its selection of apps/services, etc) by proceeding as follows:</p>
<ol><li><b>Edit /etc/iiab/local_vars.yml using a text editor such as nano, vi, or emacs.</b></li> <li><b>If IIAB is already installed, you MUST then enact your changes:</b> <ul><li>Best Way: Run a (fast) surgical strike using <code>cd /opt/iiab/iiab</code> followed by <code>sudo ./runrole &lt;ROLE NAME&gt;</code> to run a single <a href="https://github.com/iiab/iiab/tree/master/roles">Ansible role</a> or <a href="https://github.com/iiab/iiab/wiki/Contributors-Guide-(EN)#female_detective-understanding-ansible">IIAB Stage</a>. </li> <li>If You Absolutely Must: you can force the running of <a href="https://github.com/iiab/iiab/blob/master/iiab-stages.yml">all Stages 0-9, WITH the 'network' role</a>. Use <code>cd /opt/iiab/iiab</code> followed by <code>sudo ./iiab-install --reinstall</code> <ul><li>DANGER: You may first want to delete <code>/etc/iiab/iiab_state.yml</code> (to apply more force, At Your Own Risk!)</li></ul></li></ul></li> </ol>

<h2>Is a "Rapid Power Off" button possible for low-electricity environments?</h2>
<p>Yes. Small medical clinics and electricity-starved remote libraries typically require this, given that Raspberry Pi computers do not have a physical Power Off button, and it's asking way too much for non-technical operators to power off using the Admin Console.</p>
<p>"<b>apache_allow_sudo: True</b>" is the default in <a href="https://wiki.iiab.io/go/FAQ#What_is_local_vars.yml_and_how_do_I_customize_it?">/etc/iiab/local_vars.yml</a> to make this possible.</p>
<p>To power off, any user can then click a URL like: <a href="http://box/js-menu/menu-files/services/power_off.php">http://box/js-menu/menu-files/services/power_off.php</a> (formerly <a href="http://box/common/services/power_off.php">http://box/common/services/power_off.php</a>)</p>
<p>The "Power Off" button can also be customized in IIAB's <b>Admin Console</b> (<a href="http://box/admin">http://box/admin</a> or <a href="http://box.lan/admin">http://box.lan/admin</a>) <b>&gt; Content</b> (menu) <b>&gt; Load Menu &gt;</b> (change the settings in) <b>Properties of Current Menu &gt; Save Menu</b>. More detail on the Content Menus <a href="https://github.com/iiab/iiab-admin-console/blob/master/roles/console/files/help/ContentMenus.rst">help page</a> and <a href="https://github.com/iiab/iiab/issues/1689">#1689</a>.</p>

Worse, those who try to force this with self-signed web server certificates find that everyday users (the world's poorest citizens, and others!) do not tolerate the complexity and hassle of scary browser pop-up overrides.</p>
<ol><li>Set <code>gui_port: 443</code> in <code>/etc/iiab/local_vars.yml</code> preferably prior to installing IIAB. (Or if IIAB software is already installed, modify and then run <code>/usr/bin/iiab-gen-iptables</code> so that your <a href="https://github.com/iiab/iiab/wiki/IIAB-Networking#firewall-iptables">iptables firewall</a> opens up port 443 instead of port 80.)</li> <li>After IIAB software is installed, edit <code>/etc/nginx/server.conf</code> to remove or comment out <code>server_name $hostname;</code> — instead set <b>your actual domain name</b> e.g. <code>server_name MY-DOMAIN.ORG;</code></li> <li>The above <code>certbot</code> command asks you a few simple questions to help arrange <a href="https://letsencrypt.org/2015/11/09/why-90-days.html">auto-renewal of your web certificate every 60-to-90 days</a>, before it modifies your <code>/etc/nginx/server.conf</code> (check this file carefully for any further manual modifications that you might require!)</li> <li>If you modified any NGINX (web server) configuration files, run <code>nginx -t &amp;&amp; nginx -s reload</code> to validate then "reload" NGINX.</li> <li>Test HTTPS access to your IIAB, by browsing to your actual Internet domain name, e.g. <a href="https://MY-DOMAIN.ORG">https://MY-DOMAIN.ORG</a></li> <li>If you later need to manually renew your web certificate (e.g. if its Internet access is very unreliable) then consider commands like: <code>certbot renew --dry-run</code>, <code>certbot renew --force-renewal</code> or simply <code>certbot renew</code></li></ol>

<p>3. On Linux or macOS, we strongly recommend the built-in command <a href="https://linuxconfig.org/how-dd-command-works-in-linux-with-examples">dd</a> if you are comfortable at the command-line.</p>
<dl><dd><ul><li><i>If you are copying to a different brand/model of microSD card that happens to be a few bytes smaller, 'dd' alone will fail!</i></li> <li>If so, you can solve this using the <a href="https://github.com/iiab/iiab-factory/blob/master/box/rpi/howto-mkimg.txt">min-sd and cp-sd</a> commands found <a href="https://github.com/iiab/iiab-factory/tree/master/box/rpi">here</a> (min-sd is the underlying magic that shrinks or truncates microSD cards, without any data loss).</li></ul> <ul><li>On macOS, 'dd' will run extremely slowly if you don't use macOS's own special syntax. So we recommend this macOS recipe, as an example:</li></ul> <dl><dd><ul><li>Insert a brand new microSD card</li> <li>Determine mount point of card: <code>diskutil list</code></li> <li>Unmount card (this presumes its mount point is /dev/disk2): <code>sudo diskutil unmountDisk /dev/disk2</code></li> <li>Copy source file (e.g. source.img) from Mac's disk to card: <code>sudo dd if=source.img of=/dev/rdisk2 bs=4m status=progress</code></li></ul> <dl><dd><ul><li><i>Helpful Tip:</i> The "r" device (e.g. <code>/dev/rdisk2</code> NOT <code>/etc/disk2</code>) is the raw one. It should let you write 1 byte at a time, and is ~20X faster.</li> <li><i>Helpful Tip:</i> If your macOS is older and <code>status=progress</code> doesn't work, you can type <code>ctrl-T</code> at the shell running dd to send it the INFO signal, to provide you a snapshot of how much has been transferred.</li></ul></dd></dl> </dd></dl></dd></dl>
<p>4. Consider friendly software <a href="https://etcher.balena.io/">balenaEtcher</a> for Windows, Mac and Linux — that should help you copy (clone) an entire IIAB microSD card to another.</p>
<dl><dd><ul><li><i>WARNING: Copying (cloning) WILL FAIL if the target card is a different brand/model, that happens to be even 1 byte smaller than the source card!</i></li></ul></dd></dl>
<p>4.1 On Windows 10/11, strongly consider <a href="https://github.com/filipxsikora/dotnet-disk-imager">dotNet Disk Imager</a> which is a "dotNet equivalent to Win32 Disk Imager, but more lightweight and with more features." [Several of us use this on Windows 8 and Windows 10, e.g. Tim Moody writes: "first time it stopped on the second partition after it asked me to format, like win32 imager...but it has a button to wipe the device and after I did that it completed"].</p>
<p>4.2 On Windows 7 or Windows 8 <i>(must be prior to Windows 10!)</i> consider the "Read" button in <a href="https://sourceforge.net/projects/win32diskimager/">Win32 Disk Imager</a> to back up an IIAB microSD to your Windows hard disk:</p>
<dl><dd><ul><li><i>If you are copying to a different brand/model of microSD card that happens to be a few bytes smaller, this will fail!</i> In which case try any of the alternate approaches listed above and below, or buy a larger microSD card.</li> <li>Before you begin the copy, specify a proper path and filename in the "Image File" textfield on top. We recommend you choose a filename ending with ".img"</li> <li><i>Copying <b>from</b> a microSD card will NOT work on Windows 10, which views the card as two drives, one of which Windows claims is not formatted (because it's Linux / ext4).</i> It appears Win32 Disk Imager will however copy from microSD's on all versions of Windows up to and including Windows 8.1.</li></ul></dd></dl>

<p>Here's our brief-but-growing participatory design archive / lineage:</p>
<ul><li>Our earliest work is based on <a href="https://wiki.laptop.org/go/School_server">One Laptop Per Child's original XS design and implementation</a> which arose in <a href="https://wiki.laptop.org/mediawiki/index.php?title=School_server&amp;oldid=23045">January 2007</a> to be used in <a href="http://olpcMAP.net">many countries</a>, thanks to John Watlington and Daniel Drake.</li> <li>Jerry Vonau greatly cleaned up XS(CE) networking, from approximately 2010 onwards, so that vital new hardware and networking topologies became possible.</li> <li>After the XS Community Edition grassroots team came together during the middle of 2012, using the XSCE name selected by George Hunt. Sridhar Dhanapalan wrote the <a href="https://docs.google.com/document/pub?id=1dnhU2F6EntepVXTgN8QpkME8fZVUuPjcCoMUfAVKbcc">Design Document</a> in September 2012.</li> <li>Field implementer Tony Anderson expanded on these ideas laying out some <a href="https://wiki.laptop.org/go/XS_Community_Edition/Use_Cases">Use Cases</a> in October 2012, while many others contributed to a broadening roadmap of <a href="https://wiki.laptop.org/go/XS_Community_Edition/Features">features</a>.</li> <li><a href="https://github.com/iiab/internet-in-a-box">Internet-in-a-Box</a> (also built by volunteer professionals, including Caltech and JPL software engineers) was founded separately by Braddock Gaskill in this same <a href="https://github.com/iiab/internet-in-a-box/commit/9c3a5ab5a7a19e9eb084cc0ddeac942d66dbaa69">mid-2012</a> timeframe, in Southern California.</li> <li>David Farning's Activity Central team supported several developers to work on XSCE during the 2013 and 2014 timeframe, including Anish Mangal, Anna Schoolfield and others!</li> <li>German Ruiz at Fundación Zamora Terán expanded support for XSCE in Nicaragua, Columbia, Dominican Republic and Honduras (etc), as the natural successor to OLPC's original XS.</li> <li>The XSCE project embraced the Internet-in-a-Box name and IIAB's underlying OpenStreetMap work increasingly from 2013 onwards, until formally adopting the Internet-in-a-Box name (IIAB) in early 2017.</li> <li>Beginning in 2014, the XSCE project greatly increased its content collaboration with Internet-in-a-Box (hence our new name), with Kiwix.org, and with RACHEL (Remote Area Hotspot for Educational and Learning) — in each case greatly assisted by Tim Moody.</li> <li>George Hunt and Jerry Vonau made our critical transition to Raspberry Pi (and Ubuntu, and Debian) possible, with foundational OS/infra/networking work in 2016 permitting 2017's breakthrough into so many more communities. Enabling so many grassroots implementations that could never have afforded traditional PC-class servers and associated computer lab facilities/upkeep.</li></ul>
