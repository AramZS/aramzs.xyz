<h2 data-gu-name="standfirst">In an extract from his new memoir, the screenwriter bids farewell to the show he helped save – and rails against the constant online leaks that made it so hard to write<br/>  ‘One person turned up at my house’: <a data-link-name="in standfirst link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/media/2026/oct/03/russell-t-davies-interview-doctor-who-fierce-fandom-love-grief">read an interview</a> with Russell T Davies</h2>
<p>Billie Piper surprised all of us. We knew Ncuti Gatwa was leaving, so I’d written the climax in which the Doctor, having saved the life of a baby in The Church on Ruby Road, Ncuti’s first episode, now departs by saving the life of a baby because the universe is at stake. But we had to make these final episodes not knowing whether the Disney deal would be renewed. That was nothing to do with us at [independent production company] Bad Wolf; it was down to conversations with Disney and the BBC. So we had no idea what was going on. Deep down I thought it wouldn’t. You could tell what I was thinking, because I was already creating new work: Tip Toe for Channel 4, and also setting up the dance version of It’s a Sin with Rambert. In fact, in some ways it would have been a nightmare for me if that third series had come along, but I would have managed it.</p>
<p>As the third series was in flux, the finale was intended to climax with an open-ended regeneration. So we shot that with Ncuti: he smiled and said his last line, and the screen melted into a golden glow … Except when we edited that together, it looked like the death of the show. We said, “Think of children watching that. It’s like a death. We can’t do that to them.”</p>
<p>By the time we reached the edit, it was pretty much confirmed that Disney had said no. Then, unfortunately, we had to wait months, as the BBC was in a difficult situation. The BBC is always slow to act in a crisis, just because it’s such a vast institution. And at the same time, people were leaving the BBC, like [chief content officer] Charlotte Moore, who’d always been one of Doctor Who’s greatest champions. But now she was gone. And the BBC never knows how to deliver bad news. In fairness, who does, really? And the BBC has vast press barons and rightwing media poised to attack every decision it makes, so no wonder it’s nervous. As a result, there was a delay of months when none of us could say anything and none of us could do anything, because they wanted to get all the right pieces in place to say how and why this show had faltered.</p>

<p>At the same time, there was the launch and transmission of series two. So you can see why there was a delay. We couldn’t launch a new tranche of episodes saying: “Oh, by the way, this deal’s falling apart.” I completely got that.</p>
<p>We were stuck in limbo with many months of inaction, while I sat there watching an ending that looked like too much of an ending. So I summoned a Zoom with all our execs, and I said: “We can’t transmit this.” We need someone to step in. We need someone to be there. Someone for him to physically regenerate into in front of our eyes. We decided the Doctor should regenerate into … Billie Piper!</p>
<p>We approached her and she was so delighted to do it, because she loves Doctor Who and she loves the team. We’ve all stayed great mates across the years, and Bad Wolf made Billie’s I Hate Suzie series. She gave us the fastest yes we could have had.</p>
<p>And it worked, because everyone paid attention to the show once more. I can’t begin to tell you how many people – in the streets, students here in Manchester, members of my own family – were talking about it. Two days after transmission, I went to Charlotte Moore’s farewell drinks in London. The great and good of television were there, and I couldn’t believe how many people asked me about Billie. Proper, seasoned television professionals who I never would have thought watch Doctor Who, all saying: “Billie, Billie, Billie!”</p>
<p>There was only one problem.</p>
<p>Like I said, everyone hates giving bad news. So when it came to announce the end of the Disney deal, the <a data-component="auto-linked-tag" data-link-name="in body link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/media/bbc">BBC</a> was very keen to announce the existence of a Christmas Special for 2026 at the same time, to confirm its dedication to the show. Now, I had my doubts, right from the start – a Christmas Special about what, who for, where’s the money coming from? – but for the first time in my life, I allowed my name to go on a press release that I actually felt kind of queasy about. That’ll teach me!</p>
<p>But I did have a story. I pitched it to the BBC, and they loved it. An olde-worlde English village intercut with a huge, mysterious spaceship, an outer-space festival called Winternox, and lots and lots of Daleks, with something very unexpected hidden away … Oh, yes, I liked it!</p>
<p>Then everything slowed down. Let’s face it, when a show isn’t in production, it slides down everyone’s lists. Production thrives on energy, and it’s dangerous when it dissipates. I didn’t even get a contract to write the Christmas Special until the end of March 2026, after it had been announced in October 2025.</p>
<p>And then I got very busy with Tip Toe, busier than I’d expected, because Channel 4 wanted to transmit it as fast as possible; it was so topical, and it had dialogue about Keir Starmer and Kemi Badenoch that risked being out of date if we didn’t hurry up. And then Billie got busy on that tremendous Netflix show, Wednesday, so time kept slipping away. We even wondered if it could become an Easter Special for 2027. For a while, I became obsessed with the notion of a Christmas story transmitting at Easter, because, as part of the plot, time was out of joint. The Doctor running around shouting: “It’s not really Christmas!” A bit meta, but fun.</p>
<p>But then I thought: listen to yourself. What are you saying? A Christmas Special at Easter, which is then leading to … what? At exactly the same time, plans sped up at the BBC. The biggest changes in TV happen when the bosses change, and now it happened again. Things had slowed down when Charlotte Moore left, but then the new boss, Kate Phillips, grabbed hold of Doctor Who with energy and vigour. Which I really appreciated, because when you’re in the top job at the BBC, you’ve got to deal with Trump and Netanyahu and Reform and a million problems every day, so it’s hard to put an old sci-fi show at the top of your list. But Kate did! Which gives me enormous confidence in the future of the show. But it impacted on us at the same time.</p>
<p>The BBC had always spoken about putting Doctor Who out to tender one day, because that fulfils the Royal Charter and Agreement demanding that all TV content engages with open-market competition. We’d always presumed the tender might lurk somewhere in the future, maybe 2028 or 2029. But suddenly it was decided to start the process in 2027. Right! Good! Great!</p>
<p>Except that leaves a question. What is this Christmas Special? There’s a huge era behind us, there’s a huge era ahead of us, what’s this thing in the middle? A single episode which is neither here nor there, neither fish nor fowl. It really existed to please the fans, except the irony is, it was exactly the sort of episode that fans would not be pleased by. On top of all that, the tender became a good exit point for me and Bad Wolf. Let’s face it, we weren’t going to come back for a third time! Also, I thought quietly to myself: I’ll spend a year working on this, but it’ll all be leaked before it gets on air.</p>
<p>Bear with me for a little tangent here. When it comes to science fiction, the problem of leaking online is now so vast and so pernicious it threatens to ruin everything. And it’s more ferocious than ever with Doctor Who. Every decision we ever made, every single plot twist, every single moment, all the way up to the appearance of Billie in the very last seconds of the last series finale, was leaked. It’s absolutely extraordinary, and I swear it did a lot to destroy the profile of the show. It introduces a cynicism among fans, because they’re not surprised by anything any more. Their response to a leak is weariness, an instant assumption that whatever has been leaked won’t work. They instantly imagine the worst version of it, and then, when they eventually see it, it inevitably confirms their bias. So we had to work for two years knowing that leaks were happening, and yet we were powerless.</p>
<p>The BBC managed to track down the person who was putting the information online, but it was a member of the public. They were probably friends with someone at some point in the delivery chain of Doctor Who, and we simply didn’t have the legal right to seize that person’s emails. I wish we did. This was my copyrighted material – why don’t I have the legal right to get that person arrested? But the BBC was always very scared of how that would look – a member of the public being prosecuted for simply breaking copyright; it would be like breaking a butterfly on a wheel.</p>
<p>To this day, we don’t know who the leaker was, which made us suspicious of every single person we worked with. There were some tricky conversations at work. But somewhere down the line, all these episodes go through a hundred technical processes in a hundred different suites, and I’m quite sure it’s just some random person sitting in the back of a room – not the person in charge, but the person whose job it is just to press play on every single thing we ever did, who’d then tell his mate, who’d then post it online. That person is still out there. They might be reading this and frothing with glee. Well done, mate, enjoy your masturbation. But we were powerless, absolutely powerless, to do anything. I think this small element of science-fiction fandom is actually beginning to affect the whole genre, especially now hatred can be monetised on YouTube. Doctor Who, Star Trek, Star Wars, they’re all being brought down by contempt. You can even see it happening outside sci-fi, where any second series is furiously at war online with fans of its first series. Look at it happening with The Pitt.</p>

<p>Fortunately, contempt is essentially uncreative. Those people will run out of steam. And look at how long I’ve been having ideas; I’m way ahead. Anyway. I went to the BBC on 6 May 2026 and said: “I don’t think we should be making this.” And I didn’t say this next part out loud, but I thought with all my heart: the BBC is in financial trouble; it’s laying off thousands of jobs in the newsroom, so should it really spend millions, with no co-production money, on a Doctor Who one-off that will soon be eclipsed by a whole new era? “Put your energy and imagination into the future,” I said. And to be honest, I think they’d been waiting for me to say it. We’d all been too polite with each other, them not wanting to let me down, me not wanting to let them down. But this new plan sets the show free. The future can be planned by a brand-new team. Clean and bold and bright.</p>
<p>Yes, that leaves us with a Billie puzzle, but c’mon, that’s easy. Doctor Who problems are simple to solve (remember, in 1996, it was proclaimed that the Doctor is half-human – that was fixed by everyone discreetly never mentioning it again). A new production team can either seize hold of that appearance happily, or – and this is more likely – they can explain the event away with exactly seven words. Painless. That’s not a problem, that’s a piece of piss.</p>
<p>So that’s my farewell. Goodbye, Whoniverse. God, that was fun! I wouldn’t have missed a single second of it. What a remarkable show.</p>
