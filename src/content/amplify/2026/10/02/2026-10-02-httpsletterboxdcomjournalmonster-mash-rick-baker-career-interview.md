<p>Rick Baker tells Marya E. Gates about his groundbreaking career in special-eﬀects makeup, from building Bigfoot for <em>Harry and the Hendersons</em> to collaborating with Eddie Murphy on <em>Coming to America</em> and <em>The Nutty Professor</em>.</p>
<p>There’s such magic that happens when an actor looks in the mirror through his eyes and sees this other face looking back at him.</p>
<p>—⁠Rick Baker</p>
<p>When you think about master makeup artist <a href="https://boxd.it/3YxR">Rick Baker</a>, what do you picture first?</p>
<p>Depending on who you ask, it could be the metamorphic makeup effects in <em>An American Werewolf in London</em> (1981), which broke new ground with its real-time creature transformation. It could be his protagonist-plumping contributions to <em>The Nutty Professor</em> (1996), which turned Eddie Murphy into all seven members of the Klump family. Then there’s his dazzling makeup for Tim Burton’s <em>Ed Wood</em> (1994), for which he seamlessly transformed Martin Landau into horror legend Bela Lugosi.</p>
<p>Or perhaps it’s his grotesque makeup effects for <em>Men in Black</em> (1997), which somehow convinced us all that Vincent D’Onofrio was actually an alien bug from space wearing a Vincent D’Onofrio suit. For me, though, it’s his empathetic makeup work on <em>Harry and the Hendersons</em> (1987), which allowed audiences to see Bigfoot not as a big, hairy monster but rather as a creature with as much soul as any of us.</p>
<p>All in all, Baker has been nominated for a whopping eleven Academy Awards for Best Makeup and—with seven wins to date—is the category’s record holder. Inspired during his childhood by the iconic horror characters he saw on television, like Frankenstein’s monster and Quasimodo, and by monster magazines, where he learned about early masters of the craft like Wally Westmore and William J. Tuttle, Baker began his career in the 1970s, working on schlocky B-horror movies—conveniently, for some of those projects, he already had a gorilla suit.</p>
<figure><div class="rw-embed-wrapper"><embed src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/G6f7by_UMNw" type="video/mp4"/></div></figure>
<p>At this year’s <a href="https://boxd.it/64Kt7">Locarno Film Festival</a>, where he was presented with the Vision Award for his work in “shaping some of cinema’s most fantastical creations,” Baker <a href="https://www.locarnofestival.ch/news/2026/pardo/real-monsters-talking-with-rick-baker.html">revealed to the festival’s <em>Pardo</em> publication</a> that those early childhood memories of watching monster movies awakened his inner artist.</p>
<p>“I would draw monsters, but I wanted to see one in person,” Baker reflected. “I wanted to be able to touch one. <em>Famous Monsters of Filmland</em> magazine taught me that there were people who did this for a living, so at age ten, I said, ‘This is what I want to do.’ Prior to that, I used to say I wanted to be a doctor, but really I wanted to be Dr. Frankenstein.”</p>
<p>The iconic creature transformation for <a href="https://boxd.it/29yk"><em>An American Werewolf in London</em></a>—still his best-known work—was only one instance in which that dream came true; it was shot, after months of preparation, in just a couple of takes. That all his work sculpting heads and storyboarding made for a sequence that was ultimately filmed in ten minutes was initially “disheartening,” he recalled, though seeing the film with his crew in a Westwood theater made him realize its effect. “When the transformation happened, people stood up and cheered,” Baker recalled. “So many of the transformations now are all CGI, and you can do really cool things that we couldn’t do, but I still haven’t seen something that impacted me like <em>American Werewolf </em>impacted people back then.”</p>
<p>Since then, Baker has thrilled audiences with all kinds of imaginative creations, working on films and television shows that run the gamut from horror classics like <a href="https://boxd.it/29uC"><em>Videodrome</em></a> (1983) to Michael Jackson’s <a href="https://boxd.it/3e76"><em>Thriller</em></a> music video (1983). The night after he received the Vision Award, <em>Journal</em> sat down with Baker to discuss his long and varied career making both monsters and men appear larger than life.</p>
<figure><div class="rw-embed-wrapper"><embed src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/tSou9A8uFB4" type="video/mp4"/></div></figure>
<p><strong>How was your event last night?</strong></p>
<p>I was overwhelmed. I was tearing up. It was really special. I thought about what I was going to say, but it just went out of my head when I was up there and saw all these people. It touched me, in a weird way. It was an experience I don’t think I’ll ever forget. My introduction felt so sincere. I’ve been introduced by people before who really have no idea who the hell I am and just read something off the Internet. That introduction seemed like it was really coming from the heart.</p>
<p><strong>I just turned 40, and the one present I asked for was the 4K of </strong><strong><em><a href="https://boxd.it/1ZqK">Harry and the Hendersons</a> </em></strong><strong>because that was my very first favorite movie when I was a kid.</strong></p>
<p>It’s one of my favorite characters that I’ve done.</p>
<p><strong>You won an Oscar for </strong><strong><em>Harry and the Hendersons</em></strong><strong>, but you also worked on</strong><a href="https://boxd.it/1X14"><strong><em>Gorillas in the Mist</em></strong></a><strong>(1988), </strong><a href="https://boxd.it/1XJ6"><strong><em>Mighty Joe Young</em></strong></a><strong>(1998) and other films involving apes. A lot of what makes those films so special is the emotion you convey through your suit designs. Obviously, it’s what gorillas are known for—their emotive faces. I’d love to hear how you accomplished that practically.</strong></p>
<p>I started building gorilla suits when I was a kid and on a budget, but I was a big Ray Harryhausen fan. I loved that he controlled what these characters did and gave them emotions. Doing makeup on myself and making faces all the time helped me learn from looking in a mirror. I also learned from watching Lon Chaney about what expressions work and what you can get from them.</p>
<p>For <em>Harry</em>, Bill Dear—who wrote the script and directed it—came to me before he had a deal. He said, “I’ve written a script with Bigfoot, who has to pretty much sell the movie,” and he said to me, “You give your characters a soul.” He said he hadn’t seen work like that from anybody else and that, because I give them a soul, he would really like me to do this movie. He didn’t have any money upfront, but he said, if I could do a design that he could put in the script, that he could then send to people, they could visualize Harry.</p>
<p>I did a drawing; it’s in the movie, after John Lithgow has to draw a scary one, then he draws a sweet one. John Lithgow was on the fence about whether he was going to do the movie or not, but he said, when he saw the drawing, he decided he was going to do it. Harry had to communicate with his expressions. We gave him human expressions. We watched dailies at lunchtime; I would watch John Lithgow, who was very expressive and so fun to watch, and I incorporated into Harry some expressions Lithgow had done.</p>
<p>As a wrap gift, Lithgow gave everybody a drawing that he’d made, which was the entire crew doing what they do. He had spent enough time to look at everybody, see what they do and create a little drawing of them. That was so special. When he signed mine, he wrote, “You’re the soul of<em> Harry and the Hendersons</em>.”</p>
<figure><div class="rw-embed-wrapper"><embed src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/X5ZSyTq0deI" type="video/mp4"/></div></figure>
<p>When Bill Dear was shopping the film around, it took a while. I was actually in England with my wife; we were finishing up with something on <em>Gorillas in the Mist</em>, and I got a call from Bill, and he said, “Good news. I’m just going to make <em>Harry</em>.” He goes, “There’s a little issue with Amblin.” I’d had issues with Steven [Spielberg] over <em>Night Skies</em>, [an unproduced film]. I said, “Does that mean somebody else is going to have to do it?” He goes, “No, no. Steven thinks you’re the right person to do it.”</p>
<p>That was good. I then said that [the suit actor] had to be my friend Kevin Peter Hall. I got Kevin his very first job. It was a film called <a href="https://boxd.it/QKE"><em>Without Warning</em></a> (1980); I said I wanted to be involved in the casting of the person who was going to play the alien, they had an open casting call, Kevin had just gotten off a bus from St. Louis and was in a casting newspaper, and so when this seven-foot-two Black guy walked in the room—who could move and act—I said “I want this guy.” We became fast friends; I told Bill Dear that Harry had to be Kevin.</p>
<p>At that time, Kevin was in a TV show called <em>The Misfits of Science</em>. He said to me, “Well, Rick, I’m committed to this, and I’m done wearing suits now that my face has been on screen.” I said to him, “This is the stupidest decision you’re ever going to make. You’re seven-foot-two. The only thing you’re going to get is basketball player parts.” Kevin hated basketball, because everybody thought he was a player; we did a casting call for giants, and we saw one guy who was seven-foot-nine, who basically couldn’t move. We’d actually cast somebody else when Kevin’s show got canceled, and we’d almost finished a suit for them, but I said, “I’ll make the suit again if we can get Kevin, and I won’t charge you any more money. I know he’s going to bring so much to the movie.”</p>
<p>I operated his face; there were a couple of us. So much of the movie took place in the kitchen, and they built it like a regular house, so there were no wild walls [that could be quickly detached and moved out of the way]. We had two cameras on crab dollies, and a Bigfoot and a whole family in a kitchen, and three people with remote controls trying to work his face—trying to see what Kevin was doing. After a while, all our brains just somehow connected, so that Kevin could improvise and we could follow along. That was a special movie for me.</p>
<p><strong>It’s the movie that made me really fall in love with movies.</strong></p>
<p>I wish it had done better commercially, though. I thought it was going to be my <a href="https://boxd.it/2a1G"><em>E.T. the Extra-Terrestrial</em></a> (1982)<em>. </em>I blame Steven for that. He didn’t want to show Bigfoot in commercials or on the poster.</p>
<figure><div class="rw-embed-wrapper"><embed src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/jcxnL9kx3iI" type="video/mp4"/></div></figure>
<figure><img alt="Poster for The Thing with Two Heads (1972)" src="https://s.ltrbxd.com/static/img/empty-poster-110-BCr__jme.png"/><figcaption>Poster for The Thing with Two Heads (1972)</figcaption></figure>
<p><strong>One of your earliest films was </strong><a href="https://boxd.it/1p7C"><strong><em>The Thing with Two Heads</em></strong></a><strong>(1972). </strong></p>
<p>I’m the two-headed gorilla!</p>
<p><strong>How did you get that gig?</strong></p>
<p>That came about because I already had one of my early gorilla suits. It wasn’t a complete suit, but I had a picture in my wallet of the head, and I held my hands up to make it look like a suit. I would show it to everybody. I did a film in South America that I almost died on a couple of times. I showed the photo to the stunt coordinator on that film. I said, “Look at my gorilla suit.” He was also the stunt coordinator on <em>The Thing with Two Heads</em>; the production guy asked, “Do you know anybody with a gorilla suit?” He said, “Yeah.” They called me in. I brought the head that I had made—that didn’t have a body—and they said, “Well, we need two. We need two heads, and we’re going to shoot in two weeks. Can you make a new head in two weeks?” And I was like, “Sure.” I had to build that suit in two weeks, by myself. And then I played the gorilla.</p>
<p><strong>This was before </strong><strong><em>An American Werewolf in London</em></strong><strong>, but was there a point early on where you first felt like you could keep doing this professionally?</strong></p>
<p>Well, I had hoped I could. It’s funny. When I was in my early twenties, I did a TV movie called <a href="https://boxd.it/lFe"><em>The Autobiography of Miss Jane Pittman</em></a> (1974). I was recommended by [makeup artist] Dick Smith. I wasn’t in the union, but they liked what I was doing. They needed to hire a union makeup artist, and they’d be the one getting paid, but they said I could do the work. The union makeup artist was Stan Winston. We went to a production meeting, and they started talking like we were both doing the makeup, and I said, “What the hell? I thought this was my show?” Stan says, “I can’t do it alone. We need to do it together.” I said, “<em>I</em> can do it alone.” But we ended up doing it together, and we won an Emmy for it, and I thought, “Oh, my career is made.” I had just gotten married—but then I didn’t work for a year after that.</p>
<p><strong>Wow!</strong></p>
<p>I had $6,000 I’d saved in the bank; when I was down to my last couple of dollars, I got <a href="https://boxd.it/1VwY"><em>King Kong</em></a> (1976), which paid me more than I’d normally get. It helped me realize that there’s not always going to be work, and that I had to be careful about money. In fact, I worked with some old-timer I think was a prop guy; he said, “Kid, don’t get a credit card; don’t buy anything unless you can pay for it.” It was good advice. So when I wasn’t working, I wouldn’t spend money.</p>
<p>It’s happened to me a couple times that I was on a production going along for a number of months, then they canceled it. A couple of times, I had to lay people off. These jobs pay people well, but they would say, “How am I going to pay my rent?” I would say, “You’ve been working for six months, getting $1,500 a week. How can you not have money?” So many people just get money and spend it like it is going to last forever.</p>
<p><strong>In Hollywood, you just never know.</strong></p>
<p>You never know. I just didn’t want to have to get a real job, you know? When I was between gigs, I was eating pork and beans and hot dogs.</p>
<figure><div class="rw-embed-wrapper"><embed src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/-0DsGmVbPyU" type="video/mp4"/></div></figure>
<p><strong>I’m glad you brought up </strong><strong><em>Miss Jane Pittman</em></strong><strong>, because I wanted to ask you about your work with age makeup, creating more human characters. You collaborated consistently with Eddie Murphy. I love the age progression you did on characters in </strong><a href="https://boxd.it/21zE"><strong><em>Life</em></strong></a><strong>(1999). What was it like collaborating with him over the years?</strong></p>
<p>My first film with Eddie was <a href="https://boxd.it/1Ygu"><em>Coming to America</em></a> (1988). At the time, Eddie was on top, so he had a huge entourage of people when he came to my studio, when I was going to do his life cast. John Landis came in and said, “You’re going to see more Black people today than you’ve ever seen in your whole life.” I go, “What do you mean?” He goes, “You’ll see.” It was a moving party. There were thirty people with him when I was taking his life mask. I cast the back of his head in plaster bandage, because that wasn’t important; I used alginate at the time, which sets pretty quickly. So, I’m mixing up the alginate, and when I’m about to put on his face, I looked up, and Eddie had completely pulled out the back half of his mask; he’s telling jokes and entertaining his friends. It’s like, “Ah!” So I shoved his head back in there and put the stuff on.</p>
<p>After he left, I said to John, “I can’t do this makeup at a party. I need Eddie in a room by himself. You have to talk to him about it.” And he goes, “I’m not going to talk to him about it. It’s your problem.” I go, “No, it’s your problem if I can’t do the makeup.” I called Eddie, and I explained it to him. I said, “I’m very focused. It’s very difficult. If I screw up an edge, that means I start over again. I can’t have you entertaining people.” He said, “Okay.” But then, after a while, he goes, “You’re really boring. Can I at least have a friend come in every once in a while?” And I said, “Okay, but they have to leave when I say they have to leave.” If you’re gluing on a mouth, the actor can’t be talking.</p>
<p>I like to do makeup tests before we film; otherwise, if it doesn’t work, the first day filming would be a really expensive makeup test. I told John I had to test the “Jewish man” makeup. The very first piece I put on Eddie, which was painted a Caucasian color, was a cheek; Eddie looked at himself in the mirror and he was moving his face around. I go, “This is a good sign. He’s seeing what it takes to make this work.” When I got him totally made up, he just said, “Rick, this is so much more real than I ever expected, but I don’t feel that I’m doing your makeup justice, because I do a stereotypical Jew.”</p>
<p>He said he wanted to improvise a scene with one of his friends in front of the mirror, so he could see what he was doing. I said that would be great. I videotaped it; I pointed the camera at the mirror, and he did this serious scene about an old Jewish man beaten up by Black kids. It’s totally different from what he did in the movie, but I thought, “This guy can really act.”</p>
<p>I loved that he had fun with the makeup. He joked that he could go to the mall in that makeup. And I said, “Yeah—but I don’t think they’ll take your credit card.” I enjoyed the experience of working with somebody who made the makeup look better and had fun with it. When we were filming the scene with the old Jewish man, Eddie got in a golf cart and drove around the studio, trying to sell scripts to people, trying to make dates with secretaries. John asked, “Where’s Eddie?” I’m, like, “He took off in a golf cart.”</p>
<figure><div class="rw-embed-wrapper"><embed src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/Hqc52C1aFAs" type="video/mp4"/></div></figure>
<p><strong>How did that collaboration evolve on <em><a href="https://boxd.it/1YXI">The Nutty Professor</a></em>?</strong></p>
<p>When <em>The Nutty Professor</em> came along, it was different because it was during a lower point in his career. He didn’t have as big of an entourage, and he was actually nicer to everybody. He plays this overweight guy, Sherman Klump, and then he has this formula and becomes Buddy Love. Executives were saying, “Maybe we shouldn’t go back to this Jerry Lewis routine and put buck teeth in, because overweight people may be offended by this movie.” And I said, “Did you read the script? It’s all about what’s inside, not what you look like. That’s the whole point of the story.”</p>
<p>We did a test. Eddie had fun with it. The fact he was playing all those characters meant, even if a scene would normally take a day to shoot, if you had five characters he was playing in it, he could only be one a day, so it was going to take five. The accountant said we should just get four other actors; they didn’t understand what Eddie was going to do. I called up Eddie, and I said, “We have to show them.”</p>
<p>We had got the very first mother appliance out of the mold, and we had the suit almost made, with his padding. I made him up again in front of the mirror, and I was going to videotape him. He said, “Rick, I don’t know how I’m going to play this yet.” And all it took was for me to say, “Well, tell me about your son Sherman.” Then he would start saying stuff and trying different voices, and he finally arrived at what the mother was going to be. He did some hysterical stuff. A couple days later, we did the grandmother. He knew how he was going to play that, riffing on Moms Mabley. I did two hours of video of him; we cut some stuff together, and I said to the studio, “You watch this, and you tell me you want to cut this out of the movie.”</p>
<p>What really impressed me was when they were going to do a master shot of everybody around the table; they were going to have two sets, and one was a hot set that was going to be for split screens of Eddie. I said I could make makeup doubles for the master shot to save money there, and Eddie could come in as the mother; so we had doubles, they had actors off-camera reading scripted lines and Eddie had an earwig. I had headphones on and was sitting next to the director and was looking at the monitor. You could hear people off-screen. Eddie was responding to the scripted lines and then, all of a sudden, he would say something that didn’t make any sense. The director and I both looked at each other in confusion. We didn’t realize what he was doing until three days later when he prayed to the brother. What he did was he had improvised what he thought the brother was going to say, in response to what the character he was playing that <em>day</em> had said. Who has a brain like that?</p>
<p><strong>That’s amazing.</strong></p>
<p>He was fun to work with.</p>
<figure><div class="rw-embed-wrapper"><embed src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/M-7zy_W6T28" type="video/mp4"/></div></figure>
<p><strong>I’ve read that you used some Lon Chaney techniques for Vincent D’Onofrio’s makeup for his character, Edgar, in </strong><strong><em><a href="https://boxd.it/2a0I">Men in Black</a> </em></strong><strong>(1997), which blew my mind. How did you learn about Chaney’s approach? Was it in magazines like </strong><strong><em>Famous Monsters of Filmland</em></strong><strong>?</strong></p>
<p>Yeah, exactly. It wasn’t all Lon Chaney techniques, but it was not totally modern foam rubber stuff either. I had done a design I really liked, and I’d worked with Vincent on<em> <a href="https://boxd.it/2adM">Ed Wood</a></em> (1994). I thought, “Actors aren’t going to let me do this to them,” but I was really in love with the design. I called up Vincent and I said, “Can you come to my studio? I want to show you what I want to do and talk to you about the makeup process, because if you don’t think you can handle it, I’ll try to change it.”</p>
<p>One of the things I did was glue Vincent’s eyes down, like in <a href="https://boxd.it/1C"><em>London After Midnight</em></a>(1927), where Chaney had those metal rings around his eyes. When I told him I was going to glue his eyes down, I said that I had done it to myself, and that it’s not the most comfortable... I showed him, and I pulled his eye down. The first thing he said was, ‘Can you pull it down farther?’ I thought, ‘Okay, I love this guy.’ So I glued silk organza to him, stretched his face around, glued it down—then told him I was going to put plumper dentures in his mouth. He said, ‘Anything you want to do.’ He asked if I could build a whole suit for him, that would make it hard for him to move. I said I thought that would be a little too much. [Laughs] But he never complained.</p>
<p>Actors always say, “Oh, it took eight hours.” There were a couple of makeup looks that took six hours; it was a long time to sit. But he never, ever complained. Barry Sonnenfeld, the director, did a table read, and Vincent read the lines flat. Barry called me up and said, “I’m going to recast Edgar.” I went, “What are you talking about?” And he goes, “We did a table read, and Vincent can’t act.” I go, “It’s a table read. He’s reading. When the camera’s on you, it’s different.” He still said, “No, no. I’m going to recast him.” I said, “You can’t recast him. Nobody else will let me do this makeup. I know that he’s going to be able to do this. Please, please, please, please.”</p>
<p>Finally, he said, “If you fuck up my movie, I’m going to kill you.” [Laughs] And we didn’t know how he was going to play it until the first day. I was a little bit scared, but I had faith. On set, I could see what Vincent was doing, and I could see the director, and as soon as he started doing his Edgar character, I got a big ass smile on my face, and I looked at Barry Sonnenfeld, and I gave a thumbs up and went, “Yeah.”</p>
<p><strong>It’s one of those performances. I watch it and I’m like, “This is why actors should get Oscars for horror performances,” because who else could do that?</strong></p>
<p>Yes! Jeff Goldblum should have gotten an Oscar for <a href="https://boxd.it/1YIe"><em>The Fly</em></a> (1986).</p>
<figure><div class="rw-embed-wrapper"><embed src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/cf9j7g_qBZU" type="video/mp4"/></div></figure>
<p><strong>I wanted to ask you about </strong><a href="https://boxd.it/3v1u"><strong><em>Maleficent</em></strong></a><strong>(2014), which was your last film.</strong> <strong>I’ve read that Angelina Jolie had requested you specifically to come help turn her into a monster.</strong></p>
<p>It really came more from Toni G., who is her personal makeup artist; she had worked with me on <em>The</em> <em>Nutty Professor</em> and <em>Life</em>. She said they “had to get Rick.” I was concerned about working on somebody so beautiful. I didn’t want to put pieces on her face, but she wanted it. She’s the one who suggested sharp cheekbones; she actually wore a nose, which people didn’t realize. Also, the horns were a big issue. I told her, after wearing the stuff myself, that we had to make them removable and light. I wasn’t sure how that was going to work with her. She came out and spoke to me about it.</p>
<p>At that point, I had my big studio, which had a fancy lounge where I would sit and talk with people. She came with a couple of her kids and a nanny. When we were talking, one of her kids said, “I want something to drink,” and Angie, mid-sentence, turned around and said, “Say please.” I thought, “Oh, wow—she is a mother.” I designed and we made the stuff, but Toni G put it on her with Arjen Tuiten, a Dutch makeup artist I had worked with, whom I really liked. My involvement in <em>Maleficent</em> was the design and the sculpting of the originals.</p>
<p><strong>So much is done today with motion-capture, but you still have films like Guillermo del Toro’s </strong><a href="https://boxd.it/Ecsg"><strong><em>Frankenstein</em></strong></a><strong> (2025) using old-fashioned makeup effects and getting terrific performances out of actors like Jacob Elordi. What do you see as the future of traditional makeup effects?</strong></p>
<p>When CG first came out, it seemed like we were going to be ancient history, but I personally felt that there would always be makeup, because there’s such magic that happens when an actor looks in the mirror through his eyes and sees this other face looking back at him. That happened to me when I first started doing makeup and helped me overcome my shyness—because it wasn’t me anymore.</p>
<p>I think, with traditional makeup, you get a performance out of an actor that you’ll never get with dots on his face. Same with a set. You walk on a set and you know where you are; when you’re using a green screen, you don’t have a clue. I think some of the best makeup ever is being done right now. There are so many great people all around the world, doing incredible makeup. I think it will continue. I think actors need it. When you have good makeup on a good actor, it’s magic. It’s so fun for me to see something, that was clay that I pushed around, looking alive. And it’s thrilling for me when an actor embraces it.</p>

<p>When I did <a href="https://boxd.it/29ps"><em>Planet of the Apes</em></a> (2001) with Tim Burton—which is a horrible movie with some good makeup—I said to Tim, as we had very little pre-production time, I wanted every actor to come to me before they signed their contract. I wanted to realistically tell them what their life was going to be like. I had done a test on myself, and I did another test on one of the guys in my shop with these big dentures. I knew the actors would say they couldn’t talk with them in. I’d say to them, “We’re going to make practice pairs. If you practice with these, you will be able to.”</p>
<p>I told them they would have to sit in the makeup chair for three hours in the morning, with an hour of removal time. I said, “Over the whole course of the day, I’m going to be looking at you, like an object, usually at the corner of your mouth, and poking you.” I asked, “Do you <em>really</em> want to do this? Because I don’t have time to redo this at the last minute for somebody.” I truly expected to have an army of apes come after me at the end of the movie and try to kill me—but I never had complaints from anybody. Everybody accepted the job and knew what they were getting into; they were great about it.</p>
<p>The funny thing, too, when I did the test and talked to Tim and showed him my test, was that I said, “You don’t want to cast somebody with a nose like mine.” I did some designs on people that I thought were good; Ron Perlman was one of them, actually. He cast Tim Roth—whose nose is even bigger than mine—saying he’d bring something special to the film, which he really did. When I gave that makeup speech to Tim, he was the only actor that said, “Is there anything I can do to help speed up the process?” I said, “Yes, if you sit in the chair and don’t move—don’t do your taxes, don’t talk on the phone, just sit there like a mannequin—then you’ll be done in two hours.” He was great. Helena Bonham Carter, who I loved, hers took a little longer, because she was all over the place. But she was really sweet. It does help when you have cooperation from an actor.</p>
<p><strong>I just wanted to close by saying, when I was a kid, I loved the ’80s </strong><strong><em>Beauty and the Beast </em></strong><strong>television series with Ron Perlman, which I didn’t know you’d worked on.</strong></p>
<p>That’s another one where I was involved in the casting so that the lead actor would know what he was getting into before he signed his contract. I knew Ron had some experience with prosthetics. He was game. He leaned into the makeup, because with that show he became a sex symbol. The makeup made him feel sexy.</p>
