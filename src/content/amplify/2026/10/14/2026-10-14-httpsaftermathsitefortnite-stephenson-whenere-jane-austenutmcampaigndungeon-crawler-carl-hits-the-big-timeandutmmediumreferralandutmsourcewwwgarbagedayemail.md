<p>I have covered <em>Fortnite </em>for years, and if there’s one thing I’ve heard time and again, it’s that the kids long for more classic literature in the dizzying zoo of crossovers the game has become. Sci-fi author Neal Stephenson is giving them what they want, with the announcement Thursday that his company Whenere is bringing Jane Austin’s <em>Pride and Prejudice </em>to <em>Fortnite</em>.</p>
<p>Stephenson has been <a href="https://nealstephenson.substack.com/p/whenere"><u>working on Whenere</u></a> (a portmanteau of “when” and “where” that I have misspelled in a variety of creative ways in the draft of this blog) for a few years now, which <a href="https://whenere.world/?ref=aftermath.site"><u>describes itself</u></a> as a “web-based script editor to help you create immersive, cinematic stories that can be watched directly within <em>Fortnite</em>.” <em>Pride and Prejudice </em>is the company’s first focus, and it won’t be recreating the story wholesale, instead creating a setting for fans to tell their own stories in places like the book’s Netherfield Park. Using the Unreal engine and Whenere’s platform, users will be able to select a location the company is building, script movement and add dialogue (self-written or with the assistance of AI), then publish it on Whenere’s <em>Fortnite </em>island. According to a <a href="https://cloud.google.com/transform/whenere-dreamlands-gemini-jane-austen-fortnite-future-of-fan-fiction?ref=aftermath.site"><u>Google Cloud </u></a>blog, the project will be “part storytelling, part filmmaking, part roleplaying, where users get to be both creator and audience simultaneously.”</p>
<p>Google has a blog about this because AI is all over this thing: Whenere is using Dreamlands, an AI tool powered by Google’s Gemini, to “generate much of the furniture and décor and maintain historical accuracy,” according to an interview the team gave with <a href="https://gamesbeat.com/how-a-3-person-team-is-using-ai-to-recreate-pride-and-prejudice-in-fortnite-exclusive/?ref=aftermath.site"><u>GamesBeat</u></a>. Artist and company co-founder Karen Laur told GamesBeat, as an example, that AI was particularly helpful for making sure the wood on the copious number of chairs needed for a Jane Austen recreation was all the same, saying, “That would have been really hard for us to duplicate.” Stephenson told Google that “This can be a place where AI is augmenting and enhancing creativity, not replacing it.”</p>
<figure><div class="rw-embed-wrapper"><embed src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/ZdBRAz9yvro?feature=oembed" type="video/mp4"/></div></figure>
<p>Whenere likens the whole thing to fan fiction, with its site saying that classics like Austen are “one of the most popular fanfiction genres in the world, giving us an eager community of creative writers ready to bring their stories to life.” Austen is also, the company notes, in the public domain; Stephenson mentions to GamesBeat that his own books are “tied up in Hollywood rights.”</p>
<p>Now that we’ve got the basics out of the way, let’s discuss: What the fuck is this and who is it for? Stephenson is <a href="https://www.wired.com/story/plaintext-neal-stephenson-named-the-metaverse-now-hes-building-it/?ref=aftermath.site"><u>the inventor of the term “metaverse,”</u></a> which was briefly Epic CEO Tim Sweeney’s obsession before he <a href="https://aftermath.site/fortnite-epic-layoffs/"><u>pivoted</u></a> to deciding the company should be about <a href="https://aftermath.site/fortnite-kingdom-hearts-sonic/"><u>entertainment crossovers</u></a> and <a href="https://aftermath.site/epic-fortnite-ai-art/"><u>AI</u></a>. Whenere’s <em>Pride and Prejudice </em>is right at home with that goal, with a hearty dose of user-generated content–<a href="https://gamesbeat.com/epic-games-has-paid-out-1-billion-to-creators-since-launching-unreal-editor-for-fortnite-in-2023/?ref=aftermath.site"><u>a growing draw of <em>Fortnite</em>, though also a cost to Epic</u></a>–to boot. And <em>Fortnite</em> has even previously featured literature, bringing <a href="https://kotaku.com/fortnite-x-books-you-read-in-high-school-1846991574?ref=aftermath.site"><u>Brandon Sanderson’s <em>Mistborn </em>series</u></a> to the game in 2021.</p>

<p>But it seems unlikely that many <em>Fortnite </em>players are clamoring to experiment with a book their high school English class is forcing on them, and it seems even more unlikely that Austen fanfic creators make up a big part of the <em>Fortnite </em>player base. Austen fic is <a href="https://www.mprnews.org/story/2015/03/17/books-austen-adaptations?ref=aftermath.site"><u>big business</u></a>, surely at least in part given the public domain of it all, and to its credit Whenere makes no bones about that being part of its motivation. But as Stephenson wrote in his <a href="https://nealstephenson.substack.com/p/whenere"><u>early Whenere blog</u></a>, Austen’s world is also ripe for fan creations, with characters and settings that can be used to tell any number of side stories. Whenere’s blog notes that Austen’s “focus on ‘micro-dramas’ — characters talking and moving in rooms–lets us perfect our performance systems without needing complex action combat or custom creature animations.” (GamesBeat notes that there won’t be <em>Fortnite</em>-style shootouts.)</p>
<p>There’s still the fundamental hilarity of <em>Fortnite </em>characters wandering around a Regency-era estate, even if they can’t boogie bomb each other at a dinner party. But I’m not sure that particular humor is going to be a draw to Austen creators serious enough to show any of the real possibilities of the platform. And the AI of it all is sure to ignite <a href="https://fansplaining.com/heated-rivalry-is-at-the-center-of-fanfics-ai-reckoning/?ref=aftermath.site"><u>even more tensions</u></a> among fanfic authors–beyond the possibility of using AI to write dialogue, the AI-generated assets, however much research Laur tells GamesBeat has gone into their prompts, could be a turnoff as well.</p>
<p>Some of the fun and craft of fanfic, in my experience (I will not be taking questions) came from finding my own words for all the details I’d seen on TV or in movies. I imagine some of the fun of writing Austen fic is getting to describe the dresses and furniture and homes, something Whenere has not just done for creators here, but done with AI. That just leaves the “micro-dramas,” which, by nature of being in <em>Fortnite</em> and featuring its characters, feel like they can’t be anything more than a blunt joke. Characters being out of place is a universe of fanfic genres in its own right–characters go to high school, work at coffee shops, and travel through time–but again there’s none of the particulars of that here, none of the hands-on work (what would Mr. Darcy wear if he were an emo teen in the 2000s?) that I imagine make that fun to create.</p>
<p>I’m aware of taking Whenere’s proposition at face value, when this Austen experiment is more of a test balloon for the platform’s functionality and audience appeal. <em>Pride and Prejudice </em>is a weird universe to start with, but I suppose it’s at least a cost-effective one in terms of IP rights. Presuming low adoption or creators <a href="https://aftermath.site/fortnite-ai-darth-vader-star-wars-epic-games-sag-aftra/"><u>making characters say slurs</u></a> doesn’t permanently sour what the company is going for, it’s definitely the kind of thing that could make sense for something like Epic’s <a href="https://aftermath.site/disney-epic-fortnite/"><u>Disney partnership</u></a>.</p>
<p>But it’s precisely the kind of weird, unnecessary thing that makes both AI and the metaverse such a turnoff: something no one needs or is asking for, the clashing tech making it inescapably ugly (at least on a non-<em>Fortnite </em>platform like Second Life you could make the dresses and furniture yourself), and any professions toward honoring creativity immediately soured by the inclusion of AI. It’s a bad version of a thing fanfic writers are already doing with their own hands and imaginations. It lacks the real ambition of something like shuttered MMORG <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ever,_Jane?ref=aftermath.site"><em><u>Ever, Jane</u></em></a> or the unpredictability that made <a href="https://aftermath.site/grand-theft-hamlet-review/"><em><u>Grand Theft Hamlet</u></em></a> both funny and human, leaving it nothing but another bit of AI hype and Epic IP-hoovering.</p>
<p>Whenere is planning a closed playest, and there's no public release date yet.</p>
