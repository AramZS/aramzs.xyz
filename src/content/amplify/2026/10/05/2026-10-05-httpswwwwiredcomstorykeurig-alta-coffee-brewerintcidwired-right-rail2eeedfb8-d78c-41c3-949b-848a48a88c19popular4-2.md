<p>The Alta is Keurig's first single-serve <a href="https://www.wired.com/gallery/best-coffee-makers/">coffee maker</a> that uses plastic- and aluminum-free coffee pods—finally launching <a href="https://www.wired.com/story/keurig-k-rounds-coffee-pods-keurig-alta-coffee-maker/">after years of development</a>. If Alta takes off, we are likely seeing the beginning of the end for the disposable <a href="https://www.wired.com/story/gadget-lab-podcast-622/">K-Cup</a>, which launched in 1998.</p>

<p>Whatever your thoughts are on how the <a href="https://www.wired.com/story/how-to-make-better-coffee-at-home/">perfect cup should be made</a>, for nearly 50 million homes in the US (and not counting the many more in offices), Keurig is coffee. For almost 30 years, we’ve been trained to ditch the old <a href="https://www.wired.com/story/technivorm-moccamaster-drip-coffee-java-base/">drip-coffee maker</a> for disposable single-serve pods. But now, Keurig wants us to leave (eventually) the K-Cup and embrace the AltaRounds pod, a compostable, compressed coffee round that works the same: Drop it into the machine, press a button or two, and out comes joe.</p>
<p>During a presentation in New York City, Phil Drapeau, Keurig’s senior vice president of Future Coffee Systems, said customers wanted more than brewed coffee. They wanted to make fancy espresso drinks, from simple cappuccinos to salted caramel concoctions I sampled at the event (<a href="https://www.wired.com/2016/09/starbucks-pumpkin-spice-latte-back-mess-brain/">PSL fans, rejoice</a>). But to do so, Keurig needed to invent an entirely new system.</p>
<p>“We wouldn’t be able to do any of that with K-Cup,” Drapeau says. “It will fail. You have to brew it at the right temperature and extract. You can’t be constrained by the K-Cup system.”</p>

<h2>Robot Barista</h2>
<p>If the K-Cup was about bringing Dunkin’ home, then the Alta is about mimicking brewing third-wave coffee in the kitchen. Keurig looked at how coffeehouse baristas brew espresso, and how it can simplify the process by applying the K-Cup experience.</p>
<p>With the AltaRound, you don’t need to measure and grind beans, tamp, or adjust pressure and temperature. The round is covered with a plant-based seaweed wrap that ensures the coffee stays compressed and fresh. After you insert it, pick a drink via the touchscreen, and the machine punctures the AltaRound and applies 10 bars of pressure to extract the liquid. Spent AltaRounds are dumped into a container.</p>

<p>The first-gen Alta <a href="https://www.wired.com/gallery/best-coffee-makers/">coffee machine</a> is sleek but enormous. It would take up almost the same space as my existing Keurig, <a href="https://www.wired.com/gallery/best-coffee-grinders/">coffee grinder</a>, and <a href="https://www.wired.com/gallery/best-espresso-machines/#6154b0ad5e708252dfa81d3c">Breville Bambino</a>. On the plus side, it has a built-in <a href="https://www.wired.com/gallery/best-milk-frothers/">milk frother</a> for drinks like lattes—it’s a separate process (a Keurig rep told me someone successfully made latte art with it)—and a built-in K-Cup brewer too. Keurig says this lets users keep brewing coffee styles not yet available in the Alta or if they want something quick. (At launch, AltaRounds come in single espresso, double espresso, and regular coffee.)</p>
<p>The machine is wonderfully easy to use, but the process from inserting the AltaRound to drinking takes some time. It's still faster than using a <a href="https://www.wired.com/gallery/best-espresso-machines/">traditional espresso maker</a>, and you do get flavorful coffee with that must-have crema on the surface. A fellow journalist/coffee enthusiast didn’t find it special, but for a K-Cup drinker like me, it’s a game changer.</p>

<p>Beyond sustainability, the Alta’s technology echoes issues that plagued the K-Cup. To tell the machine which pod you inserted and to adjust the right settings, each AltaRound has a QR code that the brewer scans. In the past, Keurig ran afoul with consumers and advocates when it tried <a href="https://www.wired.com/2015/05/keurig-k-cup-drm/">to implement a printer-toner-style DRM</a> that would have shut out third-party pod makers. For now, Alta users can choose AltaRounds flavors from Keurig and Peet’s, both owned by <a data-event-boundary="click" data-event-click='{"pattern":"ExternalLink"}' data-in-view='{"pattern":"ExternalLink"}' data-include-experiments="true" data-offer-url="https://www.keurigdrpepper.com/" href="https://www.keurigdrpepper.com/">the same parent company</a>. Will Keurig allow other makers into the Alta system down the road, as it eventually did with the K-Cup? Time will tell if this is the pod revolution we’ve been waiting for.</p>
