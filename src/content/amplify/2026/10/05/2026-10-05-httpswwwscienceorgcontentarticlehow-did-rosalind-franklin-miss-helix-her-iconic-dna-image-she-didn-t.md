<h1>How did Rosalind Franklin miss the helix in her iconic DNA image? She didn’t</h1>
<h2>New analysis refutes James Watson’s claim he was first to recognize significance of famed photograph 51</h2>
<ul>
<li>5 Oct 2026</li>
<li>11:00 AM ET</li>
<li>By <a href="https://www.science.org/content/author/tim-appenzeller" title="Tim Appenzeller">Tim Appenzeller</a></li>

<p>“The instant I saw the picture my mouth fell open and my pulse began to race.” So wrote James Watson in <em>The Double Helix: A Personal Account of the Discovery of the Structure of DNA</em>, his controversial account of how he and Francis Crick deciphered the structure of DNA in 1953. The image, now known as photograph 51, showed an elegant, symmetrical pattern of spots formed by x-rays diffracted from strands of DNA—the fingerprint of the molecule’s helical structure. Rosalind Franklin, an x-ray crystallographer at King’s College London (KCL), had produced the image in the spring of 1952. But Watson’s 1968 book, amid other slights and sexist language directed at Franklin, implied the flash of insight was his alone.</p>
<p>Popular accounts still routinely suggest Franklin missed the significance of the photo. She never had a chance to challenge Watson’s dismissive narrative; she died of cancer in 1958, a decade before his memoir was released and became a bestseller. But a new analysis of her notes on another x-ray image, which she made just days earlier using the same DNA sample, offers a firm rebuttal. Science historian Alistair Sponsel of the Science History Institute and crystallographer Brian Sutton of KCL argue Franklin knew the earlier photo had revealed a helix—the epiphany Watson claimed for himself—and that is precisely why she made a second, more refined version, photograph 51.</p>
<p>Watson’s slight took hold partly because Franklin’s lab notes, usually thorough and meticulous, say little about photograph 51. But in <a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10739-026-09866-7">a paper published today</a> in the <em>Journal of the History of Biology,</em> Sponsel and Sutton draw attention to the earlier image, photograph 49, showing she made extensive notes about it despite a cosmetic defect. She recognized it suggested a helical structure and used it to calculate, for the first time, the length of each turn in the spiral—34 angstroms—and the number of bases “per turn of helix (if there is a helix).”</p>
<p>No wonder Franklin recorded little in her notes about photograph 51, Sponsel and Sutton argue. She saw no need to repeat herself. Franklin “knows exactly what’s in front of her,” says Betty Smocovitis, a science historian at the University of Florida and co-editor of the <em>Journal of the History of Biology</em>.</p>
<p>Others who study the history of DNA research agree. “It is a strong refutation of claims that imply that Franklin didn’t know what she had, or that Watson saw something she and [her student Raymond] Gosling didn’t,” says Nathaniel Comfort, a science historian who is working on a biography of Watson. Smocovitis puts it more starkly: Sponsel and Sutton’s analysis “adds justice, that’s what it adds.”</p>
<p>The new paper does not challenge Watson and Crick’s achievement. Their model of DNA, rushed out in a brief article in <em>Nature</em> after Watson caught his glimpse of photograph 51, went well beyond Franklin’s understanding. Its two strands running in opposite directions and zipperlike arrangement of complementary base pairs explained how the molecule could carry genetic information and be copied each time a cell divides. “The fact that it was a helix, you know, was not the most exciting aspect of this structure,” says Soraya de Chadarevian, a science historian at the University of California, Los Angeles. Indeed, by 1952, other researchers striving to make sense of the molecule also suspected it was helical.</p>

<p>But Franklin’s silence in her notes about the image Watson would make famous nagged at Sutton and Sponsel. “What we are trying to explain is Franklin’s motivations in real time for taking and then seemingly ignoring photograph 51,” Sponsel says. “We wanted to understand the events from her perspective.”</p>
<p>They found answers by going back to her lab books, held at the University of Cambridge, and her photography equipment at KCL. She and Gosling used a simple brass device not much bigger than a fist that held small pieces of x-ray film and, fixed across a small opening in the front of this “camera,” a slender fiber drawn from a solution of DNA. They aimed a fine beam of x-rays into that hole for hours or days to produce each image.</p>
<p>Photograph 49 captured the sharpest diffraction pattern, but it was flawed. The pattern appeared off center and truncated at one edge. The film mount inside the camera had slipped, Sponsel and Sutton realized.</p>
<p><img alt="Rosalind Elsie Franklin looking through microscope" src="https://www.science.org/cms/asset/68b0a1e0-ad64-4951-948e-11d69dbfd53f/_20260904_nid_photo49_secondary.jpg"/></p>
<p>Rosalind Franklin “was an experimental perfectionist," says crystallographer Elspeth Garman. Franklin: Universal History Archive/Universal Images Group via Getty Images</p>
<p>The flawed image was good enough to reveal key details of the helix to Franklin. But she did not think it was good enough to publish. So, within hours she set out to redo it with the same DNA sample. “She was an experimental perfectionist,” says Elspeth Garman, a crystallographer at the University of Oxford who has written about Franklin. “That’s just what I would do if I had a big result.”</p>
<p>The result was a publication-quality photograph she saw no need to comment on in her lab book. “The fact that it was a demonstration image, for publication, should put to rest claims that photo 51 held the magic data unknown, unseen, undreamed-of before,” Comfort says. “Photograph 51 was deliberately taken to better illustrate the phenomenon [she and Gosling] already recognized and had made measurements on.”</p>
<p>Yet Franklin’s reticence about the image helped sustain the myth that she had not understood her own data—as did the fact that she didn’t immediately follow up on it. “People have asked us, ‘Why didn’t she drop everything and publish photo 51 the moment she took it?’” Sponsel says.</p>
<p>The reason, they say, is that Franklin was trying to understand different forms of the molecule before publishing. For photographs 49 and 51 she had used a water-rich “wet” version that yielded only a simple diffraction pattern. But then she shifted focus to a crystalline form of the molecule that produced far more detailed patterns—but turns out to exist only in the laboratory. Sutton says he understands why she spent months on it, quipping, “I’m a crystallographer, so I want data-rich photos. I can’t do much with a photograph that only has six spots on it.”</p>
<p>It was almost a year before she resumed puzzling out the structure of wet DNA. But by then Watson and Crick, drawing in part on Franklin’s data, were busy building the metal ball-and-stick model that would soon reveal the molecule’s full form and function. They were on the way to their 1953 publication, the Nobel Prize, and, for Watson, the chance to tell the story his way.</p>
