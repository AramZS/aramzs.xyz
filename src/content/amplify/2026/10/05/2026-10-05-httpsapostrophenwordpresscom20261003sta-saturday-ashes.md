<p>Hey all! It’s Saturday, and while I have finished my re-watch of “Wolf in the Fold” I spent overnight for the last week trying to keep my husky’s digestive system from going kablooey, and I’ve gotten nowhere on the write up.</p>
<p>So, this week I’m offering up a Mission Brief for <a href="https://modiphius.net/collections/sta-2e-core-products">Star Trek Adventures</a> (the awesome Trek tabletop role playing game from Modiphius) based on an episode I ran my players through, very, very loosely tied into the TNG episode “The Game.”</p>
<p>Mission Briefs are one of my favourite things you can nab from <a href="https://modiphius.net/">Modiphius</a>—an outline with three rough acts for an adventure that gives you the basics you can run with for a full adventure. They offer <a href="https://modiphius.net/search?q=mission+briefs&amp;options%5Bprefix%5D=last">a great number of them</a> (themed) on their webstore, and I’ve turned more than a few of them into full-fledged episodes in my various campaigns, so I thought I’d return the favour again with something for your gaming table.</p>
<p>Welcome to the Phoenix Cluster.</p>

<p><strong>Suggested Era of Play: The Next Generation era</strong></p>
<p><strong>Suggested Spotlight Role: Science Officer and Engineering Officer</strong></p>
<p><strong>Synopsis</strong>:</p>
<p>The Phoenix Cluster was supposed to be surveyed for the first time in 2368 by the Enterprise D, but an attempted takeover by Ktarian extremists left the survey barely begun and incomplete. The crew’s ship is assigned to spent two weeks in the area, which includes a variety of stars close proximity, including a protostar, a T Tauri star, two main sequence stars with class M planets, a red giant, and even a white dwarf. Those six sites alone would be of enough interest individually to study for multiple weeks, and will leave commanding officers juggling massive demands on the various sensor arrays. There are misunderstandings, and with everyone working hard, certain odd temporal effects (like deja vu, people “misremembering” the order of conversations or schedules) aren’t noticed right away.</p>
<p>Once the ship arrives and the crew get started, they pick up signs of humanoid civilization on both class M planets, but no humanoid lifesigns. On one planet, the humanoids seemed to have been pre-industrial, while on the other, signs point to a much more technologically advanced species (though it’s unclear if they were warp-capable). While away teams deal with those first mysteries, the ship encounters something even more impossible: a <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_dwarf">Black Dwarf</a> (a theoretical stage in a white dwarf star’s lifetime that <em>shouldn’t</em> exist yet, as the universe isn’t old enough for it to have occurred yet).</p>
<p>With temporal effects growing more prevalent, the crew must unlock the secret of these seven phenomenon—specifically, that they are all different moments in time from the life of a single star, fragmented into the present day separately. When the humanoids on the Class M planet realized their star was about to expand to a red giant stage they attempted to send themselves into their own planet’s distant past to buy themselves more time, and instead created this temporal effect—and trapped themselves out of synch with any time. The players, now trapped in these seven shifting time periods with no way out of the cluster, must find the technology on the advanced Class M planet and align all seven timelines (and stars) into what should be the singular current correct one—the Red Giant stage—which will also return/have-returned the planet’s population to the distant past as designed. This also gives them the time to evolve and craft the slower, sleeper-style ships they used that took them to the next planetary system before their star expanded.</p>
<p><strong>Major Beats</strong>:</p>
<ul> <li><strong>Discoveries in the Phoenix Cluster</strong>—The six major stellar phenomenon—including two Class M mysteries—split the attention of the crew, and minor temporal issues end up not being noticed right away. Officers interested in archeology might be assigned to the proto-industrial ruins, while more technological minded might be sent to the higher-advanced world. Finding linguistic connections between the two changes things, as does locating what appears to be technology from the second world on the first—how did it get there?</li> <li><strong>Older than Time</strong>—Before too much can be done with the new information, the ship lurches, caught unaware by a Black Dwarf—a star that theoretically should not exist, as the universe is as yet too young. Closer to the Black Dwarf, the temporal effects begin to become more disruptive—and potentially more informative—and the crew need to co-ordinate multiple departments, Away Teams, and theoretical scientists to figure out what they’re really looking at: seven instances in the life of a single star, somehow playing out separately in local space. Also, they can’t seem to leave—when the ship reaches the oort cloud of one star system, they appear in one of the others, trapped in a “temporal heptahelix.” </li> <li><strong>The Great Escape</strong>—The crew discover the fractured concurrent timeline effect of this star was created by the humanoids via temporal technology they intended to use to escape the collapse of their star by taking their population into their own distant past—a device powered by the expansion of their star—but something went wrong and instead the star fractured into these seven iterations of its own life. To escape, the crew will have to fix the device on the technologically advanced Class M world while “aligning” it properly with the “receiver” on the Class M world in the distant past—which will restore the seven stars into the singular “now” they should be (the red giant) and (hopefully) pull their ship along with it.</li> </ul>
<p><strong>Minor Beats</strong>:</p>
<p>Throughout their time in the cluster, the crew are granted temporal hiccough-like visions of their past and future, or experience things slightly out of order—the GM can use these to explore character pasts or plant seeds for future events (and perhaps even show the crew something they wish to stop from happening, currently set to occur at some future date). As their time in the seven systems grows, increasing the complication range on tests to create the impact of these distracting blips.</p>
<p><strong>Key Non-Player Characters</strong>:</p>
<p>At the start, multiple stellar cartographers, archeologists and anthropologists, and other scientists will be bothering the command crew for more sensor access, and it won’t be possible to make everyone happy—but it’s a great way to introduce new NPCs or Support Crew. Once things with the temporal anomalies speed up, putting a favourite supporting character or love interest in harms way to the increasing volatility of temporal effects can motivate the crew—and dropping hints of future events or future NPCs can give the crew a glimpse of their future (or the people important in it).</p>
<p><strong>Conclusion</strong>:</p>
<p>The crew, after restoring the single star to its current state—the red giant, with the planet destroyed—will find a nearby system is now colonized by the very humanoids whose technology they just aligned—clearly their plan to escape into the past ended up working, and they had time to develop the technology they needed to colonize another world when their own star expanded and engulfed their original homeworld. They did it with sleeper ships, however, there’s still no sign of warp capability, so the crew will have to simply settle for knowing what they’ve done. Perhaps the crew also gain some unexpected “time off” via the temporal reset dropping them a few days in their own relative past, and no one outside of their ship having any record of ever coming to the Phoenix Cluster (which the rest of Starfleet and the Federation now doesn’t know ever existed)—Starfleet believes the crew’s mission was a discrete scanning/anthropological update on the pre-warp species.</p>
