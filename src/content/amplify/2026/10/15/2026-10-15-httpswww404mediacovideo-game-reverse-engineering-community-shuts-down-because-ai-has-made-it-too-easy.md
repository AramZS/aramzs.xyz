<p>A website that was dedicated to the human endeavor of reverse engineering, “decompiling,” and modifying video games <a href="https://decomp.dev/?ref=404media.co"><u>is shutting down</u></a> because people using AI to clone and decompile games have fully overrun the community and are far outpacing what human software engineers alone can do. The news comes as AI-coded “clones” of popular enterprise software like the Adobe creative suite and Microsoft Office have gone viral and are seemingly popping up every day.</p>
<p>The website decomp.dev said Thursday that it is shutting down because “the community aspect of decompilation has been broken” by AI, and that projects that once required intense collaboration between people with different skills can now be done trivially with AI. The administrator of the website said that it has become impossible to keep track of which projects are primarily human-led and which ones are done by AI.</p>
<p>“Historically, matching decompilation has been a brutally difficult, time consuming task, on the scale of years for any moderately-sized game with a number of active contributors,” the pseudonymous admin of decomp.dev, who goes by Encounter, wrote on the site.</p>

<p>But recent frontier AI models like GPT-6-Astra have made it trivially easy to decompile a game: “They are significantly better at following complex directions, cross-referencing resources for symbols or debug information, and writing natural code with enough guidance,” Encounter wrote. “It is now clear that, with the right guidance, human input, and code reviews, AI can expedite the decompilation process in a significant way. What is also now clear is that the community aspect of decompilation has been broken.”</p>
<p>Decomp.dev was a collaborative website that hosted file libraries and tools for reverse engineering, re-coding, and decompiling existing video games, meaning taking an existing game and recreating its source code. Decompiling a video game can be done for a variety of reasons and is popular in a few different niches of video game fandom. Speed runners decompile video games to find potential glitches or advantages they can use to beat a game faster; hobbyists decompile video games to port them to new platforms or to emulate them (for example, making Super Mario 64 run natively on PC); modders decompile games so they can more easily modify them; archivists decompile games to recreate code that may be lost; and some people decompile games just for the challenge.</p>
<p>Encounter wrote that the use of AI in the community has sped things up to the point where it has become overwhelming to track human-led projects, and people using AI have taken existing, human-led AI projects and have created “hostile forks” of them, where open-source code written by humans is taken and “finished” by AI: “These kinds of hostile forks put little to no effort into meaningfully contributing to existing projects, tearing apart our community fabric.” Encounter added, “anyone with an AI subscription can manage to create a port or static recompilation that functions at a basic level […] While community members put in the effort to decompile a game, opportunists have frequently raced to release a half-baked, barely-functional port from it.”</p>
<p>“decomp.dev was originally designed to gamify decompilation: watching the progress go up was a fun way to stay motivated when every fraction of a percent took real human effort,” they added. “Today, that same idea has warped decompilation into a hollow numbers game, exacerbated by folks who just want to burn tokens to make number go up.”</p>
<p>The end of decomp.dev is symptomatic of a broader, potentially destabilizing trend in AI. It has become trivially easy for people to use AI to make functional clones of software that live in a copyright grey zone. The <a href="https://www.reddit.com/r/decomps/comments/1x66xrd/decompdev_is_finished/?ref=404media.co"><u>explosion of decomp projects</u></a> on Reddit, for example, highlights how easy it has become to reverse engineer, clone, and mod software with AI.</p>
<p>Over the last few days, we have seen various mostly-functional AI-coded clones of Adobe’s Creative Cloud products and the Microsoft Office suite, both of which ordinarily cost hundreds of dollars a year. Software developers and small businesses that sell software as a service — the dominant business model of Silicon Valley for the last decade — are seeing job opportunities dry up and <a href="https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50093180&amp;ref=404media.co"><u>say they feel malaise watching the speed of AI progress</u></a>. And small video game developers say their <a href="https://www.404media.co/ai-made-cloning-games-easier-than-ever/"><u>projects are regularly getting stolen</u></a> by “clean room” AI clones of their games, in which the original code of a piece of software is never looked at.</p>
<p>“All I am hearing from software engineers is that software-as-a-service is dying as a business model,” Mike Nolan, who studies the politics of open source software, told 404 Media about the recent spate of AI-driven clones. “It’s becoming so easy to recreate the functionality that you want [with AI.]”</p>
<p>Earlier this year, <a href="https://www.404media.co/this-ai-tool-rips-off-open-source-software-without-violating-copyright/"><u>Nolan launched Malus.sh</u></a>, a working piece of software that is designed to “recreate” open-source software from scratch without violating copyright. As we reported back in April, Malus was designed to highlight the gaps in copyright law and open source licenses that do not protect software developers, and to “satirize” the process by which people using AI are profiting off of those gaps.</p>
