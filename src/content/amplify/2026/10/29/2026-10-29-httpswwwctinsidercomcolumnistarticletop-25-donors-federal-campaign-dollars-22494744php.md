<div><article><div data-block-type="text" data-dropcap="false"><p>Human evolution did not prepare us for the number "one trillion." We can conceptualize 10 easily. We have a sense of what 100 is, or 1,000. But then our comprehension breaks down. What is a hundred thousands, really? A thousand thousand? </p></div><div data-block-type="text" data-dropcap="false"><p>Try to guess what time it will be a thousand thousand seconds from noon on Oct. 27. Did you guess that it will be after midnight on Nov. 8? I suspect not.</p></div><p></p><p></p><div data-block-type="text" data-dropcap="false"><p>Because of this innate limitation, it's hard for us to really understand what it means that entrepreneur Elon Musk is spending hundreds of millions of dollars this year in an effort to protect Republican power in the federal government. I looked at this question a bit two weeks ago, <a data-link="native" href="https://www.ctinsider.com/columnist/article/elon-musk-366-million-political-spending-22473251.php">exploring the extent</a> to which this year's midterms really can be summarized as a contest between monied interests and popular opinion. But the evolution of the campaign since then — and a viral chart that made the rounds — suggested it was a subject worth revisiting.</p></div><div><div><p>Article continues below this ad</p></div><p data-block-type="ad" data-large-id="Inline-1" data-small-id="A300" data-unit="hnp-ctinsider.com/article_dtc"></p></div><div data-block-type="text" data-dropcap="false"><p>Before we get to that chart, you should understand that the nature of federal campaigns has shifted dramatically over the past 20 years. Particularly in the wake of the Supreme Court's Citizens United decision, the amount of money being spent to influence the outcome of congressional and presidential elections has surged. Even if we adjust past years for inflation, the amount of money being spent now is literally incomprehensible.</p></div><div data-block-type="text" data-dropcap="false"><p>Consider just the itemized individual contributions made so far this cycle. What's 5.35 billion seconds from noon on Oct. 27? You probably also didn't guess that it is the afternoon of May 21, 2196. And that's just the itemized contributions, not the ones smaller than $200 that don't need to be broken out by donor. (Another part of the surge in itemized contributions is a function of the internet and groups like ActBlue or WinRed that result in more reporting of smaller-dollar gifts.)</p></div><div><div><p>Article continues below this ad</p></div><p data-block-type="ad" data-large-id="Inline-2" data-small-id="NTV7ARD" data-unit="hnp-ctinsider.com/article_dtc"></p></div><div data-block-type="text" data-dropcap="false"><p>As the amount of money being contributed has risen, so has the percentage of that spending that comes from a small number of megadonors. In the 2000 election, less than 2% of the itemized contributions came from the 25 donors who contributed the most overall. So far this cycle, it's nearly 20% — about 1 out of every 5 dollars.</p></div><div data-block-type="text" data-dropcap="false"><p>One out of every 20 of that total has come from Musk.</p></div><div data-block-type="text" data-dropcap="false"><p>(There's a caveat worth mentioning: The FEC’s bulk data, the source for this analysis, runs weeks behind its filings. For Musk, I’ve added contributions from his political action committee America PAC that the FEC hasn’t yet processed, along with gifts made through entities that filings attribute to him. Recent contributions from other donors may not yet be included.)</p></div><div><div><p>Article continues below this ad</p></div><p data-block-type="ad" data-large-id="Inline-3" data-small-id="Inline-1" data-unit="hnp-ctinsider.com/article_dtc"></p></div><div data-block-type="text" data-dropcap="false"><p>The <a data-link="native" href="https://x.com/MikeNellis/status/2114708792310964627">aforementioned viral chart</a> compares Musk's lifetime spending on elections with that of philanthropist George Soros, long a right-wing avatar of billionaire influence. In short, it shows that Musk's lifetime spending now surpasses Soros'.</p></div><div data-block-type="text" data-dropcap="false"><p>On the metric I'm using for this analysis, that's not quite true. Musk has now spent nearly what Soros has in individual contributions since the 1980 election cycle, after adjusting Soros' past contributions for inflation. Without that adjustment, Musk is ahead.</p></div><div data-block-type="text" data-dropcap="false"><p>(The 25th and 26th contributors on that chart? Connecticut's own Linda McMahon and Steve Mandel.)</p></div><div><div><p>Article continues below this ad</p></div><p data-block-type="ad" data-large-id="Inline-4" data-small-id="Inline-2" data-unit="hnp-ctinsider.com/article_dtc"></p></div><div data-block-type="text" data-dropcap="false"><p>But Musk's lifetime political spending has almost entirely occurred since the last midterm election. In the 2024 cycle alone, he invested more money in federal political committees than donors from the states of Alaska, Delaware, Hawaii, Idaho, Maine, Mississippi, Montana, New Hampshire, North Dakota, Rhode Island, South Dakota, Vermont and West Virginia combined. He spent more than all of the donors in 42 individual states.</p></div><div data-block-type="text" data-dropcap="false"><p>This year, the imbalance is shaping up to be even more dramatic. Even with incomplete data from the third quarter of 2026, he's already spent more than all of the contributors in 46 states, including Connecticut, and more than the 18 least-contributing states combined.</p></div><div data-block-type="text" data-dropcap="false"><p>Speaking of Connecticut: Donors from this state have contributed more than $1 billion to federal political committees since the 1980 cycle.</p></div><div><div><p>Article continues below this ad</p></div><p data-block-type="ad" data-large-id="Inline-5" data-small-id="Inline-3" data-unit="hnp-ctinsider.com/article_dtc"></p></div><div data-block-type="text" data-dropcap="false"><p>Adjusted for inflation, that's about $1.56 billion over 24 election cycles.</p></div><div data-block-type="text" data-dropcap="false"><p>In the past two cycles, Elon Musk has given more than one-third of that amount. Put another way, for every five dollars that Connecticut residents have contributed since the Jimmy Carter administration, Musk has given nearly two in the past three years.</p></div><div><div><p>Article continues below this ad</p></div><p data-block-type="ad" data-large-id="Inline-6" data-small-id="Inline-4" data-unit="hnp-ctinsider.com/article_dtc"></p></div><div data-block-type="text" data-dropcap="false"><p>His trillion-dollar net worth (give or take, depending on the day) may be hard to grasp. But his giving, relative to entire states of people, is not.</p></div><figure><figcaption><span><div data-server-side-rendered="true"><p>Tesla and SpaceX CEO Elon Musk, left, receives the National Medal of Science from President Trump during the Golden Age of American Innovation Summit at the Institute of Peace on Oct. 8 in Washington, D.C.</p></div></span><span><span>Win McNamee/Getty Images</span></span></figcaption></figure><p></p><p></p><p></p><p></p><p></p><p></p><p></p><p></p></article></div>
