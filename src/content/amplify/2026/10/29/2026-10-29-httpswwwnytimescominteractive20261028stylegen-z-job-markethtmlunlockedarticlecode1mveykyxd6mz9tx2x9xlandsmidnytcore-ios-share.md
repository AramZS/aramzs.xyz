<p>Hundreds of applications later, even being rejected by an actual human being counts as a victory.</p>
<p><strong>A 22-year-old with an undergraduate degree in biology</strong> has applied for 511 jobs and counting. She was recently rejected from a role at Whole Foods after being “interviewed” by a chatbot named Sage.</p>
<p>A 23-year-old applying for administrative jobs in Texas keeps being asked to videotape himself, alone in his apartment, answering questions about his qualifications.</p>
<p>A 23-year-old in Kansas started applying for jobs at fast-food joints and at movie theaters after failing to find work as a paralegal. He answered the phone for one job interview and realized he was speaking to an A.I. agent.</p>
<p>The New York Times recently asked members of Gen Z to write to us about what it feels like to be searching for a job right now. They described a process that is lonely, opaque and impersonal, in which it is hard to tell whether one is being rejected by a hiring manager or an A.I. screener.</p>
<p>The problem is not just that hiring has <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/02/business/economy/jobs-report-unemployment.html">slowed across the labor market</a>, or that the specter of A.I. is <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/16/business/ai-raises-hiring.html">threatening entry-level positions</a>, they said. It was that they could not get a real, live human being to speak to them. Ever. About anything.</p>
<p>The same language came up over and over in their responses: They felt as if they were throwing their applications into “the void.” Some of them opted for slightly different terminology — a “black hole” or an “abyss” — but they painted a similar picture of the application process as a swirling vortex where aspirations and cover letters vanish, unlikely to be retrieved.</p>
<p>“We’re here yelling, ‘I will take the job, I’ll take any job you give me!’” said Grace Dunning, 24, who lives in Seattle. After graduating last year with a degree in neuroscience from the University of Washington, she struggled to find work for six months.</p>
<p>Most rejections came in the form of silence. “It starts to feel like you are talking to someone who is not even listening,” Dunning said.</p>
<p>“I felt very alone.”</p>
<p>“I barely hear back.”</p>
<p>“I feel like my applications are getting sent into a void.”</p>
<p>“I get weeded out before I can even be seen by a real person.”</p>
<p>“There’s no way to talk to anyone, you’re just floating in space.”</p>
<p>“No real person is looking at my cover letter or résumé.”</p>
<p>“It feels like you’re talking to a machine.”</p>
<h2>Ghost Jobs and One-Way Interviews</h2>
<p>It’s not that everyone is jobless. The unemployment rate for recent college graduates is <a href="https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/college-labor-market#--:explore:unemployment">5.7 percent</a>. Yet many of these recent graduates are making lattes or bagging groceries — not the type of work they expected to be doing with their costly degrees. They aren’t sure that things are going to get better, either. The number of 18- to 29-year-old Americans who believe they will be better off than their parents is in <a href="https://iop.harvard.edu/youth-poll/latest-poll">sharp decline</a>.</p>
<p>These <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/06/05/business/college-graduates-job-market.html">young job-seekers</a> are constantly hearing about past <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/05/20/business/economy/1991-graduates-job-hunting-advice-children.html">bad job markets</a> from family members who are eager to offer suggestions. (Have they tried networking?) Yet they can’t help bristling when they hear people suggest that their generation is lazy. They want anyone delivering a well-meaning lecture to know that the process of getting rejected from hundreds of jobs is eerier than it was for previous generations.</p>
<p>“You can’t do the classic, ‘Give them a firm handshake and say you’re ready to start on Monday,’” said Imani Stone-Thompson, 29, who lives in Rochester, N.Y. “That hasn’t worked for a really long time.”</p>
<p>She echoed what many people said: There are no phone numbers to call, no publicly accessible hiring managers to charm with a personalized email. Instead, there is what feels like a wall of tech that needs to be pierced in order to reach a human being.</p>
<p>Stone-Thompson said she applied to nearly a thousand jobs after graduating with dual master’s degrees in brain sciences last year. While living with family and working part time in a makeup store, she applied to science-related roles through LinkedIn, then messaged people who worked at each company asking if they could speak with her about the position. (No dice.) She even put the old-school wisdom to the test by showing up to potential employers in person with a hard copy of her résumé. She was told to apply online.</p>
<p>“If you never receive any feedback, any comment, any response to anything that you do, it feels very empty and hollow,” she said.</p>
<p>Stone-Thompson eventually landed a role doing data management for a research foundation that she enjoys. But others are not so lucky. In a <a href="https://www.greenhouse.com/newsroom/6-in-10-job-seekers-are-rethinking-how-they-write-to-avoid-sounding-like-ai-even-as-most-use-it-in-their-search">recent survey</a> by the hiring platform Greenhouse, 38 percent of Gen Z applicants said they were often or almost always rejected from jobs without a conversation with anyone at the company.</p>

<p><strong>Today’s job-seekers typically</strong> submit applications through online job boards where they can see the number of people who have already applied — an immediate source of discouragement. They worry about wasting time applying to so-called <a href="https://www.forbes.com/sites/michelletravis/2026/05/26/ghost-job-ads-are-latest-employer-tactic-targeted-by-state-lawmakers/">ghost jobs</a>, the term they use for roles that are already filled or do not exist. Such listings are so common that <a href="https://www.wsj.com/lifestyle/careers/ghost-job-ads-are-getting-so-bad-that-lawmakers-want-to-ban-them-2580bc3e">several state legislatures</a> are considering bills that would crack down on them.</p>
<p>These applicants are painfully aware that, by some estimates, as many as <a href="https://www.weforum.org/stories/2025/03/ai-hiring-human-touch-recruitment/">90 percent of companies</a> use artificial intelligence in the hiring process. They assume that most of their résumés are being fed into <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/01/21/business/ai-hiring-tools-lawsuit-eightfold-fcra.html">applicant-tracking systems</a>, a type of software that scans job-seekers’ credentials and determines whom to advance in the hiring process, potentially to an actual person.</p>
<p>Lydia Crim, 29, said she spent hours on an application for an executive assistant job in April that required her to write several detailed responses. She got a rejection email from the company about two minutes after hitting submit.</p>
<p>“No human actually rejected me,” said Crim, who lives in western New York and works in higher-education administration. The result has been a breakdown of trust in the hiring process, she said: “It feels like something I’m coming and doing in good faith, and then these companies are putting up a wall that I can’t clear.”</p>
<p>Some job-seekers have responded with <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/08/14/magazine/ai-chatbots-internet-communication-loops.html">A.I. strategies of their own</a>. Many write the first draft of their résumés themselves, then ask a chatbot to sprinkle in keywords from the job description. Others direct chatbots to compose their cover letters whole cloth. (Ethical or not, it may be a shrewd move: Research suggests that A.I. résumé screeners <a href="https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5417394&amp;__cf_chl_tk=4f8nl8vBOwhVi3_BOPJmMnL4yHHAM56rXUD0PuZRV00-1791475155-1.0.1.1-3XS3dnsM.pVBaghNVXbD.u3DU8dw5wRJcTVtI6jHBrU">rate applications written by A.I. more highly</a> than ones written by humans.)</p>
<p>And then there are the interviews.</p>
<p>Job-seekers described occasionally sitting down for <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2025/07/07/technology/ai-job-interviews.html">video interviews with A.I. avatars</a> complete with blank stares and too-shiny foreheads. They said they were especially sick of <a href="https://www.indeed.com/career-advice/interviewing/one-way-video-interview">one-way video interviews</a> in which applicants tape themselves answering questions for a hiring manager (or an A.I. system) to assess on their own time.</p>
<p>Jasmine DeGuzman, a 25-year-old in Orlando, Fla., finds these self-tape interviews a little bit depressing. An onscreen timer counts down while the applicant answers. “If you’re talking with an actual person, you can get a feel for, ‘Do they like me? Do they like what I’m saying?’” DeGuzman said. Instead, “you’re just talking to a camera in your bedroom.”</p>
<p>Growing up, DeGuzman, who uses they and them pronouns, had been told over and over that studying computer science was a path to a secure career. But after two years of searching, they have not been able to secure a job in a field that has been <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2025/08/10/technology/coding-ai-jobs-students.html">upended by A.I. coding tools</a>.</p>
<p>Eventually they enrolled in a Ph.D. program, hoping to boost their credentials. The job-application process had begun to feel like a referendum on their life choices, they said.</p>
<p>“You just feel like the entire system has somehow flipped in the time that you’ve been in school,” DeGuzman said, “so everything has felt like a lie.”</p>
<p>“It feels like a game that’s rigged against you.”</p>
<p>“It just feels impossible to know what I’m supposed to actually do.”</p>
<p>“It doesn’t necessarily feel like there’s a safe path, or a safe industry.”</p>
<p>“A college degree doesn’t mean anything anymore.”</p>
<p>“It is a little embarrassing to be working a retail job as a college graduate.”</p>
<p>“It’s just hard to want to go out, party, celebrate.”</p>
<p>“I’ve had to reset my expectations.”</p>
<p>“I put the work in, but it’s just not enough anymore.”</p>
<p>“Could someone please just give me a chance?”</p>
<h2>‘A Lot of Nihilism’</h2>
<p>An unsatisfying experience on the job market appears to be deepening Gen Z’s sense of alienation from the political establishment. With the midterm elections approaching, these young voters are feeling ignored, destabilized and financially squeezed — a recipe for disaffection.</p>
<p>There is a disconnect between how politicians talk about the economy and how young people are actually experiencing it, said Daniel Erby, 23, who lives in Austin, Texas.</p>
<p>“The president will say something like, ‘The stock market and the American economy has never been more active, never been healthier,’” he said, “but everybody I know is broke and unemployed.”</p>
<p>After graduating with a degree in English from Texas State, Erby applied to hundreds of roles as a desk clerk or office assistant, he said. When none of them worked out, he got fed up and applied to every Starbucks in the city. (Only one got back to him, and he was hired as a barista.) But Austin has been too expensive for him and his girlfriend, and they are planning to move back in with family in Houston.</p>
<p>With the midterms approaching, Erby, who said he was not registered with either political party, sees his peers either gravitating toward the extremes of the political spectrum or choosing to sit the election out entirely. “It breeds a lot of nihilism in people, it breeds this vicious idea that nothing matters and so who cares, just burn it all down,” he said.</p>
<p>Rachel Janfaza, the founder of <a href="https://www.theupandup.us/">the Up and Up</a>, a research and media firm focused on Gen Z, consistently hears in her focus groups with young people that they do not see the federal government providing solutions to their economic challenges.</p>
<p>That translates to a sense of frustration with the party in power, which, she said, could show up on Election Day. “Instead, it’s pretty much anti-authority, anti-establishment, anti-anyone who is currently in charge,” she said.</p>
<p>James Hernández, 23, who lives in Minneapolis, spent months looking for a job in public health — a task made more challenging by the president’s <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/04/22/science/trump-nih-funding-research.html">cuts to funding for the National Institutes of Health</a>, he said. While Republicans insisted the <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/03/business/trump-economy-inflation-midterms.html">economy was in good shape</a>, Hernández was being asked about his hobbies by an A.I. recruiter.</p>
<p>This summer, after months of radio silence, he finally got what seemed like a golden opportunity: a video interview with two real people for a role at a utility company. But shortly after Hernández joined the call and began making his pitch, the recruiter onscreen closed his eyes and leaned back in his chair.</p>
<p>He seemed to have fallen asleep mid-interview.</p>
<p>“At that point, just give me the A.I.,” said Hernández, who did not end up getting the offer.</p>
