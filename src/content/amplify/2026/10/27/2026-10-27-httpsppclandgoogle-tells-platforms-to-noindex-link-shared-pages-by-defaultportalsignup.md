title: Google tells platforms to noindex link-shared pages by default

<p><em>Google today revised its </em><a href="https://developers.google.com/search/docs/monitor-debug/prevent-abuse?ref=ppc.land"><em>Search Central guide on preventing user-generated spam</em></a><em>, widening it beyond comment forms and hosting services to "tools that let users publish shared pages, generated content, or web apps." The page gains a new section, "Only index content meant for Search," which says pages created for direct sharing, collaboration or utility should carry a noindex rule or be blocked from crawling by default, and it adds Search Console alerts and platform-level metrics to its monitoring checklist. The change is logged in an entry posted today on Google's </em><a href="https://developers.google.com/search/updates?ref=ppc.land#updated-guidance-on-preventing-user-generated-spam"><em>documentation changelog</em></a><em>, and the page carries a "Last updated 2026-10-26 UTC" stamp.</em></p>
<h2>In Short</h2>
<p>Google has a help page for websites that let the public post things, and today it said that tools where people make pages, apps or automatically generated content to share by link can be abused by spammers too. Those pages can end up in Google Search even when they were only meant for the few people holding the link, which lets spammers fill results with junk hosted on someone else's trusted website. Google now says pages like that should be hidden from <a data-google-interstitial="false" data-google-vignette="false" href="https://ppc.land/google-tells-platforms-to-noindex-link-shared-pages-by-default/#"> search</a> by default, and that the companies running these tools should watch Search Console and their own numbers for sudden jumps in new public pages.</p>
<h2>What changed on the page</h2>
<p>The page is titled "Prevent user-generated spam on your site and platform." It sits in the "Preventing and monitoring abuse" part of Search Central's monitoring and debugging documentation, alongside guides on malware, phishing and deceptive sites, and the Google Safe Browsing Repeat Offenders Policy. Three passages are new or rewritten. Every other paragraph, from the reputation system for new accounts to an example about overnight traffic from Italian IP addresses on a site aimed at Japan, is unchanged word for word.</p>
<h3>Tools join hosting platforms</h3>
<p>The introduction previously warned that spammers exploit open comment forms, and that "hosting platforms may be similarly open to abuse," with spammers creating large numbers of sites that break Google's spam policies. The revised sentence reads: "Hosting platforms and tools that let users publish shared pages, generated content, or web apps may be similarly open to abuse; spammers may create a large number of pages or sites that violate our spam policies and add little or no value to the web."</p>
<p>Two edits sit inside that sentence. The first adds a category of service defined by what its users can publish - shared pages, generated content and web apps - rather than by whether it sells hosting. The second changes the unit of abuse from "sites" to "pages or sites," which brings individual URLs, not just whole sites, within the scope of the warning. Google names no products, companies or technologies. The words "artificial intelligence" appear nowhere on the page.</p>
<h3>A new section on what belongs in the index</h3>
<p>The new section, "Only index content meant for Search," is placed after the advice on identifying spammy accounts and before the passage on manual approval. It runs to two short paragraphs. The first asks services whose users create "shareable pages, generated outputs, or interactive tools" to consider whether that content is meant to be found through <a data-google-interstitial="false" data-google-vignette="false" href="https://ppc.land/google-tells-platforms-to-noindex-link-shared-pages-by-default/#"> search engines</a> or "only accessed directly by people with a link."</p>
<p>The second paragraph sets the default:</p>
<p>"When pages are created for direct sharing, collaboration, or utility rather than public search discovery, apply the noindex rule (using a robots meta tag or X-Robots-Tag HTTP response header) or block crawling in robots.txt by default."</p>
<p>Everything turns on the final two words. The guide does not tell platforms to keep shared content out of <a data-google-interstitial="false" data-google-vignette="false" href="https://ppc.land/google-tells-platforms-to-noindex-link-shared-pages-by-default/#"> Search</a> permanently, nor does it describe what should happen when a user wants a shared page to be found. Exclusion is the starting position. How, or whether, a page later moves into the index is left unaddressed.</p>
<h3>Search Console and platform metrics</h3>
<p>The "Monitor your service for abuse" list gained two items, both placed at the top. The first asks operators to verify their site or subdomain in <a href="https://ppc.land/search-console/">Search Console</a> so that Google can email them if it issues a <a href="https://ppc.land/manual-actions/">manual action</a> against the site or detects security issues. It also names two Search Console reports, Page Indexing and Search performance, to be checked regularly for "unexpected spikes in indexed pages, impressions, or queries" that do not match what the platform is for. The reference to subdomains is specific. Many hosting and app-building services give each user project its own subdomain, and a subdomain can be verified as a separate Search Console property.</p>
<p>The second item reaches beyond Google's own tools: "Track platform-level creation and indexing metrics, and set up alerts for anomalies such as spikes in newly published public pages or a small number of users generating a large share of indexable URLs."</p>
<p>Neither item comes with a threshold. Google does not say what size of spike warrants attention, or what share of indexable URLs counts as "large." In the previous version, the monitoring list opened with spam signals such as redirects, large numbers of ad sections, spammy keywords and large blocks of encoded JavaScript, and Search Console was not mentioned anywhere on the page. Those signals remain, now from third place down.</p>
<h2>Google's stated reason</h2>
<p>The changelog entry is brief. Under "What," Google wrote that it "Updated the user-generated spam prevention guide with guidance on blocking indexing of link-shared content and monitoring abuse in Search Console." The "Why" line says the change was made "To help platforms keep user-created pages that aren't meant for Search out of the index and detect spam earlier."</p>
<p>The phrase "link-shared content" appears in the changelog but not on the guide itself, which speaks of "shareable pages" and "direct sharing." The entry offers no figures on the scale of the abuse, no examples and no date for any related enforcement. It is filed as an update to a guide, not as a change to Google's spam policies, which live in a separate document. The page remains a set of recommendations addressed to site and platform operators.</p>
<h2>Noindex or robots.txt: two routes that behave differently</h2>
<p>The new section gives platforms two mechanisms, and they are not interchangeable.</p>
<p>The noindex rule can be delivered in two ways: as a robots meta tag in a page's HTML, or as an X-Robots-Tag header in the HTTP response. The header form is set at server level and also covers files without an HTML head, such as PDFs or images, where a meta tag cannot be placed. Either way, Google has to crawl the page to see the rule.</p>
<p>A <a href="https://ppc.land/robots-txt/">robots.txt</a> disallow rule works at a different stage. It stops a crawler from fetching a URL but does not, on its own, keep that URL out of the index. Google's John Mueller explained in September 2024 that <a href="https://ppc.land/google-may-index-pages-blocked-by-robots-txt/">pages blocked by robots.txt can still be indexed</a> when other pages link to them, and that a crawler shut out by robots.txt never sees a noindex tag on the blocked page. The two methods therefore do not stack, and combining them can defeat the noindex rule.</p>
<p>That distinction carries particular weight for link-shared content. A share link exists to be pasted elsewhere - into social posts, forums, messaging apps and emails - which is precisely the kind of external linking that can lead Google to index a blocked URL without its content. The new section does not discuss the interaction. It also differs in emphasis from another Search Central page revised five days earlier: on October 21, Google's "Control what you share with Google <a data-google-interstitial="false" data-google-vignette="false" href="https://ppc.land/google-tells-platforms-to-noindex-link-shared-pages-by-default/#"> Search</a>" page <a href="https://ppc.land/google-lists-7-ways-to-keep-content-out-of-search-ai-opt-out-included/">listed robots.txt as a blocking method only for images and videos</a>, not as a way to keep web pages out of results.</p>
<h2>What stayed the same</h2>
<p>The remainder of the guide predates today's revision. It still suggests applying a noindex robots meta tag to posts from new users who have no reputation on a platform, lifting it once they earn one, and adding a "nofollow or ugc rel attribute" to links in untrusted content. The concern is the use of a platform's domain to publish spam rather than ranking damage to the host: in January, Mueller said that <a href="https://ppc.land/google-confirms-comment-spam-cant-hurt-your-rankings/">spam links in blog comments have no effect on a host site's rankings</a>.</p>
<p>Other recommendations are untouched: a published abuse policy communicated at sign-up; records of signup patterns such as form completion time, request volumes from one IP address range, user agents and chosen user names; manual approval of suspicious interactions; IP blocklists and firewall deny lists, with Akismet named as an option for WordPress; and reCAPTCHA or similar verification at sign-up to keep automated scripts from generating large numbers of sites. The monitoring section still cites the site: <a data-google-interstitial="false" data-google-vignette="false" href="https://ppc.land/google-tells-platforms-to-noindex-link-shared-pages-by-default/#"> search</a> operator, Google Alerts, web server logs, the Google Safe Browsing API and language detection tools, including the Google Translate API v2.</p>
<h2>Shared chats, app builders and games</h2>
<p>Google does not say which services prompted the change. PPC Land's coverage over the past year has documented several types of product that match the guide's new language.</p>
<p>Chat transcripts are one. When DuckDuckGo launched share links for its Duck.ai assistant on October 7, 2026, it said that <a href="https://ppc.land/duck-ai-share-links-expire-after-48-hours-by-default/">shared conversations from ChatGPT and Claude had appeared in search results</a> over the previous year, sometimes with names and personal details attached. Duck.ai's share pages instruct <a data-google-interstitial="false" data-google-vignette="false" href="https://ppc.land/google-tells-platforms-to-noindex-link-shared-pages-by-default/#"> search engines</a> not to index them in both the page markup and the server response, the two noindex methods that Google's new section lists, and they expire after 48 hours by default. Security firm Claroc has cited 2025 cases in which more than 370,000 Grok conversations were indexed, figures it gave when <a href="https://ppc.land/meta-halts-muse-share-links-after-6-800-turn-up-in-public-dns-records/">Meta paused sharing in its Muse AI agent on October 20</a> after more than 6,800 share hostnames surfaced in passive DNS records. Claroc said that exposure did not involve search engines, and that Muse's robots.txt asked crawlers to stay off its share paths.</p>
<p>App builders are another. Research by security firm RedAccess, reported in May 2026, found more than 5,000 apps built with <a href="https://ppc.land/vibe-coding/">vibe coding</a> tools such as Lovable, Replit, Base44 and Netlify that had essentially no authentication, along with <a href="https://ppc.land/vibe-coded-apps-are-exposing-corporate-and-personal-data-to-the-open-web/">phishing sites impersonating Bank of America, Costco, FedEx, Trader Joe's and McDonald's on Lovable's domain</a>. RedAccess located the apps by searching Google and Bing for the platforms' default domains.</p>
<p>Google's own products fall within the categories as well. Opal, its no-code app builder, <a href="https://ppc.land/google-expands-no-code-ai-app-builder-opal-to-160-countries/">expanded from 15 to more than 160 countries in November 2025</a>, drawing criticism from independent publisher advocate Nate Hake, who argued that promoting mass-generated blog and marketing content conflicted with Google's own Search guidance. Playground, the game builder Google opened to US adults on October 7, 2026, lets creators <a href="https://ppc.land/google-ties-playground-game-creation-to-ai-plans-for-us-adults-18/">share games by link or publish them to a public gallery</a>. Neither product's coverage establishes whether its shared pages are indexed by search engines.</p>
<h2>Reaction from search consultants</h2>
<p><a href="https://www.seroundtable.com/google-user-generated-spam-guidance-tools-42378.html?ref=ppc.land">Search Engine Roundtable</a>, which reported the change today, described the new section as a significant addition that tells platforms to distinguish between content intended for public discovery and content intended only for direct access. According to Search Engine Roundtable, consultant Marie Haynes <a href="https://x.com/Marie_Haynes/status/2114794894124326914?ref=ppc.land">asked on X</a> whether the update means new manual actions are coming for AI tools and app-building platforms.</p>
<p>SEO consultant Lily Ray <a href="https://x.com/lilyraynyc/status/2114801927938810076?ref=ppc.land">wrote on X</a>: "My first thought was Claude artifacts, ChatGPT pages/sites, FB posts, and other social/AI tools that make it easy to churn out indexable UGC at scale. Another clue from Google about new types of spam they are dealing with (likely at a big enough scale to warrant changing their documentation) and ... another clue about what they'll be targeting in the future?"</p>
<p>Both readings remain speculation. Google has not said whether enforcement will follow, and the guide describes no consequence for a platform that leaves shared pages indexable. What the page does say is that spammers "may create a large number of pages or sites that violate our spam policies." Those policies include <a href="https://ppc.land/scaled-content-abuse/">scaled content abuse</a>, which covers mass-produced pages that add little value for users, and Google stated in May 2026 that its <a href="https://ppc.land/google-spam-policies-now-officially-cover-ai-overviews-and-ai-mode-in-search/">spam policies apply to AI Overviews and AI Mode</a> as well as to traditional results.</p>
<p>The question of manual actions has been live for weeks. On October 7, Haynes reported a sharp rise in requests for help with manual actions, from essentially none to several a week, as <a href="https://ppc.land/googles-september-spam-update-ends-after-13-days-as-manual-actions-rise/">Google's September 2026 spam update ran for 13 days and 16 hours</a> before finishing on October 8. The evidence was anecdotal, and Google did not comment. That update was the fourth spam update of the year, following <a href="https://ppc.land/googles-third-spam-update-of-2026-rolls-out-to-every-language/">rollouts on March 24, June 24 and August 18</a>.</p>
<h2>A crowded month on the changelog</h2>
<p>Today's entry is the sixth that Google has logged on its documentation changelog in October. On October 1 it updated its generative AI content guidance, telling sites it is <a href="https://ppc.land/google-tells-sites-to-manually-factcheck-all-ai-content-before-publishing/">critical to manually fact-check AI output before publishing</a>. On October 8 it added documentation for the <a href="https://ppc.land/platforms-face-6-8-week-wait-for-google-ugc-fresh-data-program-answers/">UGC Fresh Data Program</a>. On October 16 it <a href="https://ppc.land/google-drops-the-quota-form-from-its-200-a-day-indexing-api-page/">dropped the quota request form from its Indexing API page</a>. The October 21 revision of the content controls page followed, then an update to guidance on crawlable URL structures on October 23.</p>
<p>The UGC Fresh Data Program offers a contrast with today's change. It gives approved forum and social platforms a dedicated pipeline to send new public posts and engagement signals to Google, on condition that they run active moderation and a user reporting mechanism, and that every post is tied to a creator with a public profile. Within one month, then, Google has documented a faster route into its systems for moderated public discussion, and a default route out of its index for user-created pages that were never meant to be searched.</p>
<h2>Why it matters for marketers and publishers</h2>
<p>Marketing teams use many of the kinds of services the guide now describes. Campaign microsites, prototype landing pages, hosted forms, interactive calculators, AI-generated reports and shared chat outputs are often built on third-party platforms and passed around by link. If platforms adopt noindex as the default for such pages, content created there will not enter Google's index unless the platform provides, and the user selects, a way to make it public. Google's guidance does not describe what that opt-in should look like.</p>
<p>For publishers and SEO teams, the change concerns the supply side of <a data-google-interstitial="false" data-google-vignette="false" href="https://ppc.land/google-tells-platforms-to-noindex-link-shared-pages-by-default/#"> search</a> spam. Pages published on trusted platform domains can compete in results with original sites, and since May those same spam policies have applied to the AI answers that sit above the links. Platforms that tighten indexing defaults reduce one source of that competition. The guide's warning applies to every such service, whatever its current defaults.</p>
<p>For platform operators, the monitoring additions carry a cost. Verifying subdomains, watching Page Indexing reports for unexplained growth, and building internal alerts for concentrated URL creation by a handful of accounts all require engineering and moderation time. The guide acknowledges this kind of trade-off elsewhere, noting that moderation "adds overhead" to daily workflows. It does not quantify what Google expects of smaller services. And it leaves a commercial tension unexamined: many platforms have grown in part through user pages that rank in Google, and the line between a page "meant for <a data-google-interstitial="false" data-google-vignette="false" href="https://ppc.land/google-tells-platforms-to-noindex-link-shared-pages-by-default/#"> Search</a>" and one made "for direct sharing" is drawn by the platform, not by Google.</p>

<h2>Summary</h2>
<p><strong>Who:</strong> Google, through its Search Central documentation, addressing operators of hosting platforms and tools that let users publish shared pages, generated content or web apps. The change also concerns publishers, SEO teams and marketers who build on those platforms or compete with pages hosted on them.</p>
<p><strong>What:</strong> Google revised its guide to preventing user-generated spam. It now names tools for shared pages, generated content and web apps as open to abuse, adds an "Only index content meant for Search" section recommending noindex (via robots meta tag or X-Robots-Tag) or a robots.txt block by default for pages made for direct sharing, collaboration or utility, and adds Search Console verification, report checks and platform-level creation and indexing alerts to its monitoring advice.</p>
<p><strong>When:</strong> Today, October 26, 2026, according to Google's documentation changelog and the page's "Last updated" stamp.</p>
<p><strong>Where:</strong> On the "Prevent user-generated spam on your site and platform" page in the Search Central documentation, under monitoring and debugging, with the monitoring tools located in Search Console.</p>
<p><strong>Why:</strong> According to Google, "To help platforms keep user-created pages that aren't meant for Search out of the index and detect spam earlier." Google has not said which services prompted the change or whether enforcement will follow.</p>
