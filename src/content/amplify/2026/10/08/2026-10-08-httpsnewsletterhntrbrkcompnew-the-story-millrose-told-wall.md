<p><em>This is the latest investigation in Hunterbrook’s housing beat, which began with our debut story in 2024: an exposé on<a href="https://hntrbrk.com/investigations/uwm"> United Wholesale Mortgage</a>, which has since succumbed to <a href="https://hntrbrk.com/breaking-news/uwm3"> rescue financing</a>. We’ve investigated the private jets, yacht, and mounting debt of<a href="https://hntrbrk.com/investigations/rh"> Restoration Hardware</a>; the aggressive sales tactics<a href="https://hntrbrk.com/investigations/lgih"> LGI Homes</a> used to<a href="https://hntrbrk.com/investigations/lgih-2"> lure</a> renters into homes they can’t afford; and construction defects that turned dream<a href="https://hntrbrk.com/investigations/homebuilders"> homes</a> into nightmares. We’ve also investigated houses on wheels, exposing how<a href="https://hntrbrk.com/investigations/winnebago"> Winnebago</a> covered up failing RV frames, now the focus of a federal <a href="https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/69408644/nowak-v-grand-design-rv-llc/">lawsuit</a>. Today’s story follows up on last week’s <a href="https://hntrbrk.com/breaking-news/lennar-millrose">investigation</a> into Lennar and Millrose, which may be among the wildest yet.</em></p>

<p>When Hunterbrook Media discovered that Lennar was selling houses to the company it had spun off, reporters visited communities in Florida and Texas to see these homes firsthand.</p>
<p>On September 29, a reporter visited 36 houses owned by Millrose Properties in Florida’s Marion County. The next day, another reporter visited 39 houses Millrose had purchased from Lennar in Princeton, Texas. They found unfurnished rooms and notices hanging from doors. At one Texas house, large spiderwebs stretched across the entrance.</p>
<p>Not one of the 75 homes appeared occupied.</p>
<p>That same week, Millrose executives were describing a business to Goldman Sachs in which tenants would already be lined up before the company bought the houses, according to a research note sent to clients reviewed by Hunterbrook.</p>
<p>“Millrose buys the home with a signed lease already in place,” the bank wrote in its September 30 recap of the meeting.</p>
<p>The pitch was a path to homeownership for people who couldn’t qualify for a mortgage: Millrose would buy the house they wanted, rent it to them, and allow part of the monthly payments to accumulate toward an eventual purchase. Having a renter identified beforehand, Goldman’s note explained, could mitigate the risk of buying a house and then needing to find someone to fill it.</p>
<p>But Hunterbrook’s reporting suggests that is not the business Millrose is actually operating.</p>
<p>Millrose was created to hold land for future construction, giving Lennar access to building sites without having to own them outright. Its largest voting shareholder is Lennar’s own CEO, Stuart Miller. Last week, Hunterbrook <a href="https://hntrbrk.com/breaking-news/lennar-millrose">revealed</a> that the land bank had become a major buyer of Lennar’s finished houses, estimating roughly $200 million in purchases in about a month.</p>
<p>Though Millrose accounted for just a fraction of Lennar’s home deliveries last fiscal <a href="https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/920760/000162828026064557/len-20260831.htm">quarter</a>, its purchases pushed Lennar’s third quarter deliveries past guidance, the only homebuilding target it hit that quarter.</p>
<p>Lennar shares fell 6.7% on the first full trading day after Hunterbrook’s report, their “sharpest one-day decline since March 2024,” according to <a href="https://www.barrons.com/articles/lennar-stock-millrose-hunterbrook-1d050484">Barron’s</a>. Millrose shares fell 8.5% the same day. Neither company has publicly addressed Hunterbrook’s findings.</p>
<p>In the days since, Hunterbrook has expanded its search and identified a total of 1,096 transaction records across 16 states, bringing the estimated aggregate transaction value of Millrose purchases of Lennar homes to roughly $290 million in about a month.<a data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" href="#footnote-1" id="footnote-anchor-1"><sup> [1] </sup></a></p>
<p>And while Millrose may describe this opportunity as a small pilot, it’s a totally different business model from what the land bank had pitched investors prior to being listed last year. It also doesn’t appear to make rational economic sense: With interest rates soaring, institutional landlords have <a href="https://www.resiclubanalytics.com/p/housing-market-net-selling-institutional-sfr-jumps-408-percent-spring-2026">become</a> net sellers, not buyers, of homes.</p>
<p>So we wondered: How was Millrose justifying this?</p>
<p>The Goldman note provided <em>an </em>answer, but new reporting reveals that the explanation Millrose gave Wall Street for those purchases may be inaccurate.</p>
<p>Hunterbrook found that, roughly a month after purchase, over 95% of homes Millrose bought from Lennar are advertised as available to the public for rent on the website of Evergreen, a rental management company Millrose has hired, with a move-in date on most listings ranging from “now” to mid-October.<a data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" href="#footnote-2" id="footnote-anchor-2"><sup> [2] </sup></a></p>
<p>That is not the business Millrose described to Goldman, where the lease is signed before the purchase closes — and therefore, presumably, there would be no need to list the homes for rent.</p>
<p>When a Hunterbrook reporter contacted Millrose’s designated property manager, Evergreen Residential Holdings, about Millrose-owned homes this week, multiple call center representatives said Evergreen did not offer rent-to-own. Evergreen offered ordinary leases instead.</p>
<p>When asked whether Millrose had really made these claims, a Goldman analyst said: “I can’t talk to that,” and ended the conversation.</p>
<p>Neither Millrose nor Lennar have responded to Hunterbrook’s repeated requests for comment.</p>
<p>What’s clear is that Millrose undoubtedly has a reason to keep Lennar happy.</p>
<p>In addition to being Millrose’s largest customer, responsible for 72% of revenue last quarter, Lennar also has the leverage of being able <a href="https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2017206/000119312525022336/d760441dex102.htm">pause</a> scheduled land purchases and halve the associated payments for up to a year, giving it leverage in any broader negotiation with Millrose over the home purchases.</p>
<p>As Hunterbrook previously <a href="https://hntrbrk.com/breaking-news/lennar-accounting">reported</a>, Angelo Gordon, another Lennar land bank, has publicly described steep penalties for missed purchases, including forfeiting deposits and selling lots to competitors. So, unlike with Millrose, it appears Lennar cannot similarly halt payments to their other land banking partners, without jeopardizing their long-term right to the land itself.</p>
<p>Which is to say: If Lennar is looking to save money during a tough stretch, it may turn to Millrose.</p>
<h2><strong>The Pitch: A Tenant Before the Purchase</strong></h2>
<p>Goldman’s recap described a “rent-to-own hybrid product.”</p>
<p>The idea was to target customers who visit sales offices but “fail to qualify for a mortgage,” according to Goldman’s recap. Millrose would buy the homes. Tenants would rent them. A portion of the rent would count toward an eventual purchase.</p>
<p>Millrose’s investor pitch was about more than helping people become homeowners. Buying a house with a tenant already committed could avoid a basic problem landlords face: paying for an empty property while searching for someone to rent it.</p>
<p>Goldman identified that as a key advantage: “a renter is often identified before Millrose buys the home, eliminating lease-up risk.”</p>
<p>Millrose management also expected discounts from builders and eventual home sales to help produce returns comparable to or better than its main business of holding land for homebuilders.</p>
<p>Even if the renter ultimately fails to buy, the eventual sale of the house is a “meaningful component of returns,” Goldman’s recap says.</p>
<p>Some features were still being developed. Millrose management reportedly told Goldman that it was working out the payment structure and considering leases of at least two years. The recap does not say Millrose promised every house it owned already had a signed lease.</p>
<p>But identifying renters before buying was central to the explanation of why the program made financial sense.</p>

<p>Dozens of Millrose’s homes on Evergreen that Hunterbrook checked ranged from “available now” to a move-in date of October 21. Four out of five homes currently listed for rent had been purchased by Millrose over a month ago — in contrast to Millrose’s description of the program as being tailored towards pre-sourced tenants.</p>
<p>Hunterbrook also called the number on the Evergreen listings to test what prospective tenants were being offered.</p>
<p>A reporter spoke with two Evergreen representatives about one Millrose-owned home currently advertised on its <a href="https://evergreenlive.com/find-your-home/listing/01M1N8CMX5NZ2XNA8FJEX1Q908/?marketId=01GDK3MAAA4PME8BE85KKSYRC0">website</a>. The representatives offered leases of 12 or 18 months and denied that there was any rent-to-own program available for the property. The house would not be available until October 16 at the earliest, they said, and that date could slip because construction or renovation work was still underway.</p>
<p>Hunterbrook reporters also called the sales offices at dozens of Lennar communities where Millrose purchased a home. None of those who answered said there was any rent-to-own program for buyers unable to qualify for a loan. One sales rep said they were just told that they might have such a program in mid-2027 but had no details to share.</p>
<p>A listing or empty-looking house doesn’t necessarily mean no lease has been signed, and calls about one property don’t rule out a pilot program elsewhere. But if the rent-to-own homes described to Goldman exist, Hunterbrook could not find them.</p>
<h2><strong>Lennar has its buyer. Millrose gets a new business with questionable economics</strong></h2>
<p>Millrose’s move into rentals was authorized through an August 27 <a href="https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2017206/000119312526378466/d83131dex101.htm">amendment</a> to the Founders’ Rights Agreement, subsequently filed with the SEC. It defined the new assets broadly as completed houses intended to operate as rentals.</p>
<p>The benefits to Lennar are already visible. The 224 homes that have a listing history on <a href="http://realtor.com/">realtor.com</a> sat for a median of 86 days before Millrose’s deed was recorded. Millrose bought them at a slight discount from the last listing price — a median discount of about 4% — but still likely more than individual buyers who also got incentives averaging 13% of the purchase price.</p>
<p>The benefits to Millrose — besides doing a solid for a homebuilder responsible for <a href="https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2017206/000119312526332732/ck0002017206-20260630.htm">72%</a> of its second-quarter revenue — are less clear. And Hunterbrook’s analysis finds the company might actually be losing money on some of these purchases.</p>
<p>Even assuming every house finds a tenant at the full advertised rental price, Hunterbrook’s estimated yield on the rentals based on specific state-level tax and average insurance rates — two of the most important expenses in a rental business — suggests Millrose will not make enough on most of their homes to clear its borrowing costs.</p>
<p>Across the nearly 1,000 Millrose homes put up for rent, the gross rent comes to 8.44% of the price a year. But after factoring in taxes and insurance, proceeds to Millrose are more like 5.92%, below the 6.5% to 6.75% Millrose agreed to pay on its latest borrowing — the $1 billion of senior notes it sold on October 6, as disclosed to the SEC in an <a href="https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2017206/000119312526415565/d151159d8k.htm">8-K</a>.</p>
<p>In states where Millrose invested most heavily — Florida and Texas — the economics are worse. In Florida, where insurance on a $300,000 house reportedly <a href="https://www.insurance.com/home-and-renters-insurance/home-insurance-basics/average-homeowners-insurance-rates-by-state">averages</a> $8,471 a year, the yield is 4.9% before financing costs. South Carolina and Alabama, which have the lowest property taxes of any state where Millrose bought, according to Hunterbrook’s estimate, are the only ones that seem to exceed Millrose’s financing costs.</p>

<p>Millrose also told investors that the rentals’ return will come partly from the eventual sale of the homes. “If the renter does not convert: Millrose sells the home on the open market. There is no put-back to the builder,” Goldman’s note says.</p>
<p>But many of the homes sit in markets where prices are already falling and are projected to fall for at least another year. Typical home values in the zip codes where Millrose bought <em>dropped</em> 9% over the past year while the national index <em>rose</em> 1.3%, according to <a href="https://files.zillowstatic.com/research/public_csvs/zhvi/Zip_zhvi_uc_sfr_tier_0.33_0.67_sm_sa_month.csv">Zillow analytics</a>. The decline is steepest where Millrose spent the most: Texas, which accounts for about 30% of the money, is down 4.2%.</p>
<p>Whether those homes can later be sold for more than Millrose paid in these markets is an open question.</p>
