<p><strong>Math rock is a subgenre based on complex guitar riffs, unusual harmonies, abrupt changes in style and tempo, and odd time signatures. While many math rock bands feature vocals, the subgenre is mostly instrumental. It combines influences from indie rock, post-hardcore, prog rock, and post-rock.</strong></p>
<p>Created in the late ’80s and popularized in the two following decades, “math rock” was named as such due to its quasi-mathematical rhythmic complexity. While math rock bands sound very different from one another, they’re all based on the same core principles: to explore new song structures and techniques in rock, no matter how strange or complicated. Because most math rock songs feature complex arrangements, the subgenre is also known for its wall-of-sound sonority.</p>
<p>A type of music made for die-hard rock fans, math rock evolved a lot since it first appeared in the late ’80s, drawing in a series of influences from musical spectrums as disparate as metal, jazz, and electronic music. Below, you can find the 10 best math-rock bands of all time.</p>
<h2><strong>1. Shellac</strong></h2>
<figure><div class="rw-embed-wrapper"><embed src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/mkrwrXs2snY?feature=oembed" type="video/mp4"/></div></figure>
<p>The guys at Shellac earned their place among the bad boys of math rock and the noisiest rock acts around. Led by the charismatic Steve Albini, the Chicago, Illinois band became a familiar name at alternative music festivals, including Barcelona’s Primavera Sound. Albini died in May 2024; their sixth studio album, “To All Trains,” was released later that month.</p>
<p>Even though they reject the math-rock tag and view themselves as a “minimalistic rock trio,” it’s undeniable that Shellac is one of the main bands responsible for shaping the math-rock sound. They’re hardcore and violent but they’re also extremely imaginative and cohesive, often subverting rock clichès.</p>
<p>To this day, Shellac’s 1994 debut album “At Action Park” remains their most influential work. While their records capture that intensity, Shellac’s natural habitat was the stage. To get a sense of their live power now, seek out concert recordings alongside the studio albums.</p>
<h2><strong>2. Battles</strong></h2>
<figure><div class="rw-embed-wrapper"><embed src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/5KExDqN1qRw?feature=oembed" type="video/mp4"/></div></figure>
<p>When everybody thought that math rock wasn’t going to live past the ’90s, a then-new band from New York City came up with the answer to the question “How does the math rock of the future should sound like?” When Battles dropped their hit song “Atlas” in 2007, it was clear to everybody that they weren’t just another math-rock band.</p>
<p>Battles’ 2007 debut album “Mirrored” is a great place to start with their sound. The <a href="https://battles.bandcamp.com/album/mirrored">official album page</a> provides a route into their music, and the record shows how their rhythmic ideas can work in memorable songs as well as knotty instrumental passages.</p>
<p>Battles are different from most math rock acts around because they present a much cleaner sound and draw influences from electronic music (not only in terms of timbres and textures but also song structure). All of their studio albums are extremely convincing, but “Mirrored” and “La Di Da Di” are probably the best.</p>
<h2><strong>3. Drive Like Jehu</strong></h2>
<figure><div class="rw-embed-wrapper"><embed src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/jLQ0dXcrAaQ?feature=oembed" type="video/mp4"/></div></figure>
<p>There’s one reason why Drive Like Jehu went down in history as a post-hardcore band: because the math rock tag wasn’t as well-known back in the early ’90s. However, it’s undeniable that this short-lived group from San Diego, California is one of the most influential bands in the history of the subgenre.</p>
<p>While indie-influenced math-rock acts thrive on complex guitar riffs, the hard-hitting Drive Like Jehu set themselves apart because of their unusually intricate approach to the fundamentals of punk hardcore. They’re passionate and sweaty, but they do it with hypnotizing complexity and mathematical precision.</p>
<p>Drive Like Jehu was such a unique band that it still populates the imagination of thousands of math-rock fans today, more than two decades after its disbandment. They existed between 1990 and 1995 (and briefly between 2014 and 2016) and released only two studio albums, both deemed masterpieces: the self-titled “Drive Like Jehu” and “Yank Crime.”</p>
<h2><strong>4. Slint</strong></h2>
<figure><div class="rw-embed-wrapper"><embed src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/_PUqHByh8pY?feature=oembed" type="video/mp4"/></div></figure>
<p>When rock fans think of the year 1991, “Nevermind” is the record that usually comes to mind. However, some may prefer Slint’s seminal “Spiderland” to Nirvana’s magnum opus. Why? Because it <a href="https://www.theguardian.com/music/2014/may/01/spiderland-slint-album-reinvented-rock">marked a generation of bands to come</a>. Slint’s music was cerebral and smart but also deeply existentialistic, depressive, and longing, much like emo.</p>
<p>With their 1991 masterpiece “Spiderland,” Slint combined taut, angular rock with eerie spaces and sudden bursts of intensity. Their use of quiet passages and sharp changes in volume gives the record an unsettling atmosphere, and makes it a great starting point for this side of post-rock. The result was a timeless record that was somehow devoided of feelings and abundantly emotional at the same time.</p>
<p>Formed in Louisville, Kentucky, Slint was also able to introduce a bluesy, Southern flavor to the math-rock subgenre (mostly popular in New York and California). The band separated for the first time in 1990, before the release of “Spiderland,” but made numerous comebacks, the latest of which was in 2014.</p>
<h2><strong>5. American Football</strong></h2>
<figure><div class="rw-embed-wrapper"><embed src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/uRXpvXpc45c?feature=oembed" type="video/mp4"/></div></figure>
<p>American Football is one of the most special math-rock bands around. They released three albums between 1999 and 2019, all titled “American Football,” and added a <a href="https://www.polyvinylrecords.com/products/american-football-american-football-lp4">fourth self-titled album, usually called LP4</a>, on May 1, 2026. Somehow, they are both a great ’90s math-rock group and a phenomenal present-day math-rock band. They’re as historic as they are contemporary, making for arguably the best emo/math rock band ever.</p>
<p>Initially active between 1997 and 2000, American Football made a comeback in 2014 and went on to release further self-titled albums, much to the excitement of their fans. Surely a cult phenomenon, the guys at American Football know how to combine great emo songs with complex math rock arrangements that often go full post-rock and “orchestral.”</p>
<p>If you love the ideas behind math rock but some of the bands are just too rough and heavy for your taste, you may want to check out American Football’s music.</p>
<h2><strong>6. Don Caballero</strong></h2>
<figure><div class="rw-embed-wrapper"><embed src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/88SjmIUuBg8?feature=oembed" type="video/mp4"/></div></figure>
<p>Welcome to the early ’90s: at a time when most alternative rock groups were exploring depressive themes, a band from Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania came up with the idea of gluing math rock and the happy guitar riffs of ’80s indie rock together. That band was Don Caballero and, if their characteristic riffs sound familiar to you, that’s because they were made by the guitarist Ian Williams, who would later create the band Battles.</p>
<p>Like most math-rock acts from the ’90s, Don Caballero has separated and reunited more times than your favorite on-and-off couple. Their output, however, is quite extraordinary: their catalog includes studio records such as “American Don” and 2008’s “Punkgasm,” alongside archival live releases and the critically acclaimed “What Burns Never Returns.” The Meshuggah drummer Tomas Haake named it one of his favorite albums of all time.</p>
<h2><strong>7. Tera Melos</strong></h2>
<figure><div class="rw-embed-wrapper"><embed src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/rYaloTRzVtc?feature=oembed" type="video/mp4"/></div></figure>
<p>Unlike the early-’90s “gods” of the math rock subgenre, ’00s math rock bands tend to have a cleaner sound, with crisp production and instrumentation but the same energy and passion that’s always characterized the style. Tera Melos is a great starting point for the more playful side of math rock.</p>
<p>Formed in 2004 in Sacramento, California, they built a cult following with records such as “Patagonian Rats,” “X’ed Out,” and “Trash Generator,” as well as EPs and split releases. They’re math-rock through and through, but they also draw influences from punk, noise rock, and even free improvisation.</p>
<p>The <a href="https://sargenthouse.com/tera-melos">Sargent House discography</a> offers several ways into Tera Melos’ mix of jagged riffs, playful sounds, and intricate arrangements. “X’ed Out” and “Trash Generator” are useful places to start if you want to hear how that restless approach works across a full album.</p>
<h2><strong>8. CHON</strong></h2>
<figure><div class="rw-embed-wrapper"><embed src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/8EcjZdSjlUk?feature=oembed" type="video/mp4"/></div></figure>
<p>CHON is a math rock band from Oceanside, California that’s been around since 2008. Their debut album, “Grow,” was only released in 2015 but was met with widespread enthusiasm. If technical ability and smart composition are what make you love math rock, then CHON is the right band for you.</p>
<p>Their use of complex harmony and elaborated time signatures is remarkable, especially if you consider how pleasant and accessible most of their songs are. Their jazzy feel derives from musicians such as <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z7j7bdEPSd0">Tigran Hamasyan</a> and <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EFeouD2IWSA">Hiromi Uehara</a>, listed as two of their main influences. While they explore elements of post-hardcore (as most math rock bands do), their sonority can be described as gentle and mellow.</p>
<p>Mostly instrumental, CHON’s self-titled 2019 album is especially jazzy, drawing inspiration from contemporary jazz fusion acts. From a technical standpoint, they’re arguably the most competent math rock band of the hour.</p>
<h2><strong>9. tricot</strong></h2>
<figure><div class="rw-embed-wrapper"><embed src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/Q77gbjfsVO8?feature=oembed" type="video/mp4"/></div></figure>
<p>Don’t get fooled by their name: unlike the art of tricot, tricot (the band) is perfect for getting a party started. There’s an interesting math rock scene in Japan, with tricot being the standout act. They’re among the most renowned math rock bands in the world right now.</p>
<p>Pretty much like CHON, tricot tries to create accessible songs without compromising on the core values of math rock. Their music is complex but accessible at the same time; it helps that tricot’s music focuses extensively on vocals, with the very talented Ikkyu Nakajima serving as the band’s lead singer.</p>
<p>Up until now, tricot’s debut album “T H E” remains their most acclaimed work. However, this band from Kyoto, Japan has a broad catalog to explore, including 2021’s “Jodeki” and its 2022 follow-up “Fudeki” (不出来).</p>
<h2><strong>10. TTNG</strong></h2>
<figure><div class="rw-embed-wrapper"><embed src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/iYpydJm3L4A?feature=oembed" type="video/mp4"/></div></figure>
<p>A list of the best math rock bands of all time couldn’t be complete without This Town Needs Guns, best known as TTNG. They’re probably the most beloved math rock band coming from the United Kingdom, and their music is a finely-balanced combination of math rock and emo music.</p>
<p>Formed in 2004, TTNG are emo in their core but work like a breath of fresh air in the genre due to their incorporation of math rock riffs and rhythms. In other words, they’re an emo band capable of pleasing both music experts and angsty teenagers.</p>
<p>Led by the bassist/singer Henry Tremain, TTNG surprised their fans in 2018, with the release of “Animals Acoustic,” an acoustic album inspired by their seminal 2008 record “Animals.” That’s how good TTNG’s songs are: they can stand tall even without all the flourishing that characterizes most math rock music.</p>
<h2><strong>Summary</strong></h2>
<p>Math rock is a subgenre that’s existed since the late ’80s but only matured in the mid-‘2000s. Ever since pioneers such as Drive Like Jehu and Slint decided to incorporate sophisticated musical notions into their in-your-face post-hardcore music, the subgenre has evolved in multiple directions, dragging influences from all territories of music.</p>
<p>Any list of the 10 <a href="https://musicianwave.com/best-bands-of-all-time/">best bands</a> of math rock of all time is certainly subjective, but this one covers the evolution of the style from its very beginning to the present day. Hopefully, the future will continue to present us with terrific math rock bands that can bring something new and innovative to the table.</p>
<p>Finally, I believe it’s important to add some honorable mentions to the list, as many top-tier math rock acts were sadly left out of the top 10.</p>
