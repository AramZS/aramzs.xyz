<p>Residents living in Air Force Academy family housing are concerned about lead contamination in their water after their plumbing fixtures tested positive for lead and medical tests showed lead in their children’s blood.</p>
<p>Families have shared their worries on social media and a handful of families spoke with The Gazette after at-home swabs showed lead on their plumbing fixtures, and they sought testing for their children. They have all switched to drinking bottled or filtered water after seeing positive test results.</p>

<p>The families expressed frustration with the company that manages homes on base, Hunt Military Communities, which also manages housing across 59 military installations and 60,600 homes.</p>
<p>“There’s been no advising, no testing, no follow-up whatsoever,” said the mom of a young infant who tested positive for lead.</p>
<p>Parents said they were also unhappy when 10th Medical Group staff members declined to test their children for lead. Most spoke on the condition of anonymity because of the fear of retribution from the military.</p>
<p>The Air Force Academy said in a statement 10th Medical Group did not provide blood testing when residents asked for their children to be tested.</p>
<p>“While the 10th Medical Group follows CDC testing guidance, installation leadership is reviewing the circumstances surrounding these requests and the processes used to address resident concerns,” the statement said.</p>
<p>The academy also said no lead contamination on the base had been identified. However, most of the residents The Gazette spoke with did not have anyone come to their home to do testing.</p>
<p>Hunt Military Communities said in an emailed statement to The Gazette it has contracted for water testing in a sampling of homes across all neighborhoods and will communicate the results and any recommended actions. About 2,700 active-duty service members live on the base in addition to the cadet population.</p>
<p>The company also said that in a water sampling of 31 homes on base, no tests had lead levels above the level that would require action.</p>
<p>When Kevin Leong took his children for lead testing through the 10th Medical Group on base, the nurse said the doctor refused to put a lab order in for lead testing because Hunt Housing had told the medical staff that the lead at-home tests of his water fixtures were false positives.</p>
<p>A representative of Hunt Military Communities visited his house, took pictures of his sink and other plumbing fixtures and told him someone with the company would do laser testing. Leong later found out from a neighbor that Hunt would not provide additional testing.</p>
<p>“We should not have to be environmental investigators simply to determine whether the home provided to our family is safe,” Leong said.</p>
<p>He later took his toddler and 20-month-old to Children’s Hospital Colorado for tests that showed lead in their blood but at levels that do not trigger treatment.</p>
<p>No level of lead in blood is safe, according to the <a data-id="73205" data-type="post_tag" href="https://gazette.com/tag/centers-for-disease-control/">Centers for Disease Control and Prevention</a>. Blood tests are also the only way to know if a person been exposed because lead typically causes no visible symptoms, according to a statement from the state Health Department.</p>
<p>“Lead builds up in the body over time, so ongoing exposure, even at low levels, may eventually cause health effects,” the statement said. Exposure can hurt children’s physical or mental development and cause lower birth weight in babies and lead to kidney problems.</p>
<p>Early testing for children can help identify exposure and prevent serious long-term health effects, it said.</p>
<p>Leong said the medical providers at Children’s Hospital advised him to try to limit his children’s exposure. He said he doesn’t want any contamination in his home, particularly since his wife is pregnant with their third child.</p>
<p>This week, at least one home at the Air Force Academy had its fixtures replaced following the discovery of lead. But such an option has not yet been presented to Leong.</p>
<p>His home also flooded with sewage three times last year, and he is generally frustrated with base housing.</p>
<p>“We trusted that the housing provided to a military family would be maintained to a reasonable health and safety standard,” he said.</p>
<p>“Instead we have had to deal with repeated sewage flooding, concerns about contaminated building materials, potential lead contamination in multiple areas of the home and difficulty obtaining definite testing for our children.”</p>
<p>Leong and his family have lived on base for two years. One year in the home that flooded with sewage and one year in the home where he found lead contamination.</p>
<p>Two other families who had children test positive for lead spoke with The Gazette as well.</p>
<p>The mom of the infant took her child in for testing after her husband overheard someone else talking about the problem in the housing office on base. The family was also denied a test for lead by base providers.</p>
<p>After visiting Children’s Hospital, the infant tested positive for a low level of lead that doesn’t require treatment, the mom said. She was particularly concerned because the baby had been vomiting profusely.</p>
<p>In another case, a mom discovered her child had lead in his blood after a routine medical exam and started testing the plumbing fixtures in her home to find the source of the environmental contamination.</p>
<p>All of her kitchen fixtures tested positive for lead, she said.</p>
<p>She would like to see Hunt representatives be responsive to the community’s concerns.</p>
<p>“We just want them to come in and test the actual fixtures,” she said.</p>
<p>In its statement, the academy said it was aware of one resident reporting an elevated blood level test and a later test showed the lead blood level was not elevated. It was not immediately clear what the academy meant by an elevated lead blood level.</p>
<p>At that one home, academy personnel collected a water sample for independent laboratory analysis.</p>
<p>The results are expected in one to two weeks.</p>
<p>The water fixture in the one residence was replaced proactively, the statement said.</p>
<p>“We remain committed to addressing resident concerns, communicating findings as they become available and maintaining a safe and healthy environment for our Academy community,” the academy statement said.</p>
<p>Hunt Military Communities and the 10th Air Base Wing have scheduled a town hall on Oct. 14.</p>
<p>The company also encouraged residents with health concerns to contact their medical provider for advice.</p>
<p>Hunt has faced serious complaints about its housing before. In 2021, five families sued the company and associated business entities for poor housing conditions at Randolph Air Force Base, The Military Times reported. The families claimed at the time the homes had problems with mold, rodents, insects and seeping sewage, among other problems.</p>
<p>Lead can enter drinking water when pipes and plumbing fixtures corrode over time, the state health department said.</p>
<p>When water sits in plumbing for extended periods, it can pick up trace amounts of lead from older plumbing, Colorado Springs Utilities said, in response to questions.</p>
<p><a data-id="4354" data-type="post_tag" href="https://gazette.com/tag/colorado-springs-utilities/">Colorado Springs Utilities</a> does not have any lead water mains — large lines that run under streets or service lines that run to homes, the statement said.</p>
<p>If anyone suspects they have lead contamination in their home, they can run water through their faucets until the water turns cold, Utilities advises. Residents can also use tabletop filters that are manufactured for lead removal.</p>
<p>Utilities encourages residents to inspect their home for older fixtures that may contain lead and replace them.</p>
<p>Home swabs can indicate the presence of lead, but the state Health Department recommends following up with a certified lab.</p>
