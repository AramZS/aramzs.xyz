<p>The <a href="https://www.thecut.com/article/what-we-know-about-the-cornell-seven-rape-case.html">“Cornell Seven” lawsuit</a>, in which a woman known as Jane Doe alleged that seven Chi Pi fraternity brothers gang-raped her two years ago and that <a href="https://www.thecut.com/tags/cornell-university/">Cornell University</a>, where they were all undergraduates, failed to protect her afterward, has led to a national reckoning over the ubiquitousness of unchecked sexual violence <a href="https://nymag.com/intelligencer/article/cornell-seven-me-too-generation-campus-life.html">on college campuses</a>.</p>
<p>The school’s record has raised eyebrows since news of Doe’s lawsuit made headlines. Between 2018 and 2024, Cornell officials filed disciplinary charges against a student or staff in just 162 cases out of more than 1,200 incidents of sexual violence, intimate-partner violence, and stalking reported to the school’s Title IX office, according to <a href="https://www.usatoday.com/story/news/nation/2026/09/29/cornell-sexual-assault-rape-expelled-data-exclusive/92007157007/">a recent data analysis by <em>USA Today.</em></a> Out of those, only seven cases ended with the accused being expelled or dismissed. (A Cornell spokesperson told <em>USA Today</em> that not all reports lead to formal complaints for a variety of reasons, including jurisdiction issues and when a victim declines to participate in the process.)</p>
<p>The <a href="https://www.thecut.com/article/cornell-daily-sun-students-interview-cornell-seven-lawsuit.html">case</a>, and the others that have been revisited since, has put a spotlight on how the Trump administration’s Title IX rules — which survivor advocates have long said place onerous burdens on victims and offer many more protections to those accused — may have impacted the outcome <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/01/nyregion/cornell-university-case-jane-doe-investigation.html">of Cornell’s investigation</a> into the Jane Doe allegations.</p>
<p>To understand why this is, and why colleges still fail to address this crisis, I called up Nicole Bedera, a sociologist and author of <a href="https://www.ucpress.edu/books/on-the-wrong-side/hardcover"><em>On the Wrong Side: How Universities Protect Perpetrators and Betray Survivors of Sexual Violence</em></a>. As part of her research, she spent a year as an ethnographer embedded in a school’s Title IX system, interviewing victims, accused students, and school administrators. “The Trump’s administration’s Title IX regulations are already so hostile to survivors,” she says. “And yet schools have really gone above and beyond in saying ‘no’ to survivors and denying them their rights.”</p>
<p><strong>Let’s start with what people misunderstand about sexual violence on college campuses.</strong><br/>
One of the biggest myths is that men rape by mistake. Like, “Oh, they need consent training.” But research is very clear: Sexual violence is not a mistake. It is an intentionally made decision by people who know they don’t have consent. They just don’t prioritize consent over things like status, power, domination, and sexual gratification.</p>
<p>For my research, I got a certificate of confidentiality from the National Institutes of Health, so my notes couldn’t be subpoenaed. One of the reasons I call them perpetrators in my work is because every single person I interviewed confessed in that context. The first question I would ask them was, “When you received that email from Title IX that you were under investigation, did you know what it was about?” Every perpetrator I interviewed said “yes.” If sexual assault were a miscommunication, you would be wondering, <em>What have I possibly done wrong? </em>That is not the way that any of them reacted because they knew.</p>
<p>Another misunderstanding is that sexual violence can be ended through education. There is not a single evidence-based program for perpetrators that has reduced sexual violence. What schools call an educational intervention is making perpetrators sit down to read the sexual-misconduct policy and asking if they have any questions. It’s having them write an essay. None of these things work.</p>
<p><strong>Can you talk about Title IX and how it has historically addressed the issue of sexual violence?</strong><br/>
Title IX essentially says that no education institution that accepts federal funds is permitted to discriminate on the basis of sex. For 50 years, we’ve been fighting how to interpret that. A lot of those decisions were made in the courts, where students sued their universities or filed a complaint with the federal government. One of the earliest was <a href="https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/FSupp/459/1/1392608/"><em>Alexander </em>v. <em>Yale</em></a>, which declared that sexual violence is a form of sex discrimination because it impacts women’s abilities to access education and its benefits.</p>
<p><a href="https://www.ed.gov/sites/ed/files/about/offices/list/ocr/letters/colleague-201104.pdf">In 2011</a>, President Obama let schools know that they needed to use a civil standard for adjudicating Title IX cases called “preponderance of evidence” instead of the criminal standard of “beyond a reasonable doubt” — that is what the courts ruled because Title IX is civil enforcement. Universities panicked and said, “Whoa, we’ve never followed any of these guidances.” Most schools did not even have a designated Title IX coordinator despite past court rulings. Schools feared the Obama administration might take this seriously because concurrently it said that it was going to start investigating schools if they were out of compliance. The administration convened a White House task force to put together a guidance, which is the basis of a lot of the Title IX processes that you see today. It was still really contentious because universities did not like it.</p>
<p>Then, Trump won.</p>
<p><strong>How did Title IX change under Trump’s first administration?</strong><br/>
Former Education secretary Betsy DeVos <a href="https://www.vox.com/first-person/2017/7/19/15990454/betsy-devos-title-ix-campus-rape-mens-rights">specifically asked men’s-rights activist groups</a> to offer some components, which <a href="https://www.thenation.com/article/politics/betsy-devos-title-ix-mens-rights/">were implemented</a> literally unedited. She also refused to meet with survivors in any substantial way. The administration cherry-picked a more restrictive standard for defining sexual harassment. It also installed things like a live hearing process with cross-examination, which is a burden on survivors that’s even more onerous than the ones in our criminal-justice system. You are not required to take the stand for cross-examination in a criminal rape case.</p>
<p>The administration published its draft of that rule in 2017. It received a historic number of public comments. But the administration did not take a single recommendation from anyone other than men’s-rights activist groups. All concerns about how the changes would impact survivors were systematically cast aside. Lawyers wrote in saying, “This section would violate our state law,” but the Trump administration moved on with it anyway.</p>
<p>It wasn’t implemented until the summer of 2020. Students were not on campus. People like victim advocates who would have been really involved in that process had been reallocated in their positions to do pandemic-response work. And, because it was an election year, there was a sense that if Trump lost, you could wait for the Biden administration to come up with something new.</p>
<p>Biden instead really dragged his feet. He didn’t put out a draft on new rules until quite late into his administration, and it too was only implemented right before the 2024 election. When Trump got into office again, one of the first things he did was once again was tell his Department of Education to stop accepting complaints about Title IX violations, especially if they were about sexual violence. He <a href="https://www.cnn.com/2025/08/27/us/title-ix-college-sexual-assault-policy-change">reinstated his first term’s rules</a>. That essentially gave universities carte blanche to handle this however they wanted, and they have been taking advantage of the lack of oversight.</p>
<p>They can make decisions based on what they think is best for the school’s reputation and which individual students they want to protect. They no longer have to think about gender discrimination as one of the essential roles of the Title IX office. Additionally, in the Obama years, the entire process was supposed to be done within 60 days. Under the Trump rules, there are now no requirements on how long schools can take, so they have gone back to doing what they did before: essentially pressing “Pause” on every case until the perpetrator graduates.</p>
<p>Back in the 2010s, you would have a survivor going to the dean of students to try to report because the school didn’t have a Title IX process. You would be told you should have made better decisions and to go on about your day. That was the end of it. Now survivors have to go through learning how to report an assault, filing a complaint, figuring out the right way to explain what happened to them that matches their school’s sexual-misconduct-policy language. A lot of schools don’t have a provision for rape, so using the incorrect terms can really mess up the reporting process. Now, there’s a paper trail. There’s an investigation. And their perpetrator is notified and can respond with retaliation. Before, institutional betrayal happened in one sitting. Victims now are getting that same response over the course of months or years before a final decision is made. And the outcome is often the same — nothing really happens.</p>
<p><strong>What kind of roadblocks do survivors face now under the Trump 1.0 rules?</strong><br/>
First, a report and a complaint are separate things — and students often don’t know the difference. The reporting requirement is one of the biggest roadblocks because universities are not required to take any action after a report is made. They are welcome to if they’d like to, but they almost never do. So survivors have to figure out a way to then file a complaint. I can’t emphasize how difficult it is to get to this step. At the school where I did my research, the link on the university website that said “Submit a Complaint” sent you to the report form. That meant there were victims who thought they’d filed a complaint and they instead had filed another report. The only surefire way to file a complaint is if they spoke to school staff with independent knowledge of the process, like someone in the Title IX office.</p>
<p>Once they finally submit a complaint, they’ll write their first statement about what happened to them. Many times they have to try to categorize it based on the school’s policy: <em>Would you say that this is sexual assault? Sexual misconduct? Intimate partner violence? </em>Survivors are not legal experts, and even if they were, how schools define these terms can vary from state law. Often how their complaint is evaluated is, “Did they check the right box?” If not, the school dismisses the case. Another issue is that survivors aren’t sure how much information to include in their description — and a lot of them didn’t realize that whatever they include is going to be used as evidence against them.</p>
<p>Then they have to decide, <em>Do you want the school to handle a formal investigation or through an informal resolution?</em> Informal resolution is now defined clearly by the Trump administration: It must be voluntary for all involved parties, including the perpetrator, and it cannot be punitive. Schools use really different definitions of punitive. The one that I was at would say that something like an agreement that the victim will only go to the library in the morning and the perpetrator will only go to the library in the afternoon would be considered punitive. It also did not allow for apologies or reflection essays because they found those to be too punitive for a perpetrator.</p>
<p>The other option is formal investigation. If they initiate that, survivors might be invited to write <em>another</em> statement. They will almost certainly be asked to come to an interview. Some schools — like Cornell — will record them, but many won’t. In the school that I studied, Title IX investigators were forbidden from recording anything. That led to huge discrepancies between what the victims would say and what administrators would document. Then the survivor would be the one accused of being inconsistent. Like, “You wrote this in your statement, but you said this in an interview.”</p>
<p>At some point in this process, the victim will be asked if they have evidence, including witnesses who could be interviewed or text messages, emails, voice-mails, security footage, and anything like that. Under the Trump regulation, they are supposed to notify the perpetrator right away that he’s been accused and present evidence. I can’t really emphasize the degree to which that allows perpetrators to build up evidence that they never would have come up with otherwise. It can also lead the perpetrator to start a harassment campaign and sort of get ahead of the story. There’s a case in my book where the perpetrator got to see the victim’s statement before he wrote his own. In hers, she alleged that he had choked her and she had almost lost consciousness. His entire statement read as if it was debunking hers line by line. He was like, “I didn’t strangle her. I put my hands on her shoulders. Like yes, I like to be a little kinky.” The administrator treated that as if it was proof that he was being so honest. Like, “Wow, he brought it up on his own accord!” But of course he did. He had read her statement first.</p>
<p>The Trump rules also say there must also be a hearing. These are often compliance theater. I found in my research that a lot of the decisions are predetermined before the hearing even begins. Like, general counsel have already decided what the outcome should be before they ever look at evidence, and they will tell everybody beneath them to act accordingly. The hearing requirement also means the victim and perpetrator have to either be in the same room or attend on Zoom. Victims were really discouraged from doing the latter and were told it would make them look less credible. Funnily enough, perpetrators attended on Zoom almost all the time.</p>
<p>At the hearing, there is supposed to be a cross-examination before a board. Afterward, usually somebody from the university’s Title IX office will write up a preliminary report with their findings and their recommended sanctions. Then an administrator will write the final report with the final decision. In my research, I found myself wondering if those recommendations were ever taken seriously. I was there for all four cases that were heard by the university that year. In every case, they recommended expulsion, but only one perpetrator was expelled.</p>
<p><strong>Can you talk about the appeals piece of this? I am thinking of </strong><a href="https://www.wafb.com/2026/09/24/exclusive-lsu-student-says-university-failed-her-after-title-ix-finding-alleged-sexual-assault/"><strong>the Louisiana State University case</strong></a><strong> that has gone viral recently, too, where a Title IX investigation found a football player responsible for “forcible rape” and yet the school </strong><a href="https://www.espn.com/college-football/story/_/id/50077551/lsu-football-damien-shanklin-rape-investigation-police"><strong>lifted his suspension</strong></a><strong> after he appealed. (He denies the allegations.)</strong><br/>
Perpetrators appeal all the time. If you go all the way up the appeal chain, at most universities it ends with the president, who makes whatever decision they want using whatever standards they want. That’s the difference between the Obama and Trump rules: The former told schools that they could only consider suspension or expulsion for cases involving rape. Trump rescinded that, and now everything’s back on the table. This is why there is an essay in the Cornell case.</p>
<p>Victims, on the other hand, are very unlikely to appeal because this process is so traumatizing. Overall, the process is really disruptive in their education. Hearings will take place during finals weeks, and victims would say, “Can I ask for it to be rescheduled?” And the university would say “no.” The school that I studied only gave survivors five business days to appeal. If they asked for an extension, they would be told, “No. If this matters to you, you’ll prioritize it.” I did not find the same impatience from Title IX administrators when perpetrators asked for extensions. In every case I evaluated, perpetrators got every extension they asked for. There was a real asymmetry.</p>
<p>Afterward, a lot of survivors don’t trust the administration to give them a fair chance.</p>
<p><strong>In light of the Cornell allegations, I’ve seen people ask, “Should schools even be involved in adjudicating these cases? Shouldn’t the police handle them instead?”</strong><br/>
During the Obama years, I’d have said police cannot provide what students need under Title IX.</p>
<p>If you’re waiting for the way to move perpetrators off campus or to be incarcerated, you might be waiting forever. There many other reasons that survivors don’t want to report to the police, including that they often experience sexual harassment from police themselves and that officers hugely overestimate the amount of false allegations. Additionally, the police cannot do something like remove your perpetrator from your dorm if you live on the same floor. They can’t do things like help if a victim fails a class or provide accommodations. That’s at the discretion of the university.</p>
<p>That answer is still true now. But what I’d add is that there’s no difference on most campuses between the school and the police because universities have their own police departments. If the violence happened on campus, you are going to have a very hard time with your city’s police department due to jurisdiction and vice versa. This happened with <a href="https://www.cnn.com/2018/10/24/us/lauren-mccluskey-university-of-utah-fatal-shooting">Lauren McCluskey</a>, a University of Utah student who was murdered by her ex-boyfriend in 2018. Before her death, she reported her killer for stalking and other abuse both to campus police and the Salt Lake City Police Department. But because some of it occurred on campus and some off campus, because she lived on campus but he didn’t, neither police jurisdiction would take her case. Both told her, “We can’t help you.”</p>
<p>The Cornell community has floated abolishing the Cornell Police Department. We should take it seriously, especially if you think that survivors should have the option of going to the police instead of their schools. I feel strongly about this too, because something people don’t understand is that universities at this point are essentially <a href="https://sk.sagepub.com/ency/edvol/socialtheory/chpt/total-institutions#_">total institutions</a>. For a lot of students, their schools are not just schools. They’re their landlords, their employers, their source of health care, their gym, their coffee shop, the arena where they attend concerts. It leaves survivors with almost no real options. If you can’t trust your school and your school systematically discriminates against sexual-assault survivors, where is left for you to go?</p>
<p>In Judith Herman’s <a href="https://www.barnesandnoble.com/w/truth-and-repair-judith-lewis-herman-md/1141725154"><em>Truth and Repair</em></a>, she asked survivors what they wanted. Most — if not all — survivors just wanted their perpetrator gone. About half were interested in incarceration, many of them just because they thought it was the only way to remove a perpetrator from their lives. A lot of them still had misgivings about the carceral system, but they tried to go to other places like their school or their employer, and it just didn’t work.</p>
<p>I wish schools and other institutions would take that more seriously. That’s why student survivors have been so clear that they wanted their perpetrators expelled so that they didn’t have to tiptoe around campus anymore or make their education smaller to avoid them. It’s not to punish them. It’s because, if you do not remove them, the survivor doesn’t get the college experience that they’re supposed to get.</p>
<p><strong>One could argue this is the reason Jane Doe may have dropped out of Cornell. The school didn’t expel most of the men she accused, so the only way to avoid them was to remove herself from campus.</strong><br/>
One of the questions that I still have about the Cornell case that we have not had answered is, Were any of these men <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/03/nyregion/cornell-men-university-discipline.html"><em>really</em> expelled or suspended</a> the way you and I would think about it?</p>
<p>The common understanding of expulsion is that a student will not get a degree from that institution. But in Title IX, a lot of the time the decision came down after the perpetrator had graduated. Expulsion means they are not allowed to reapply to campus for an additional degree. So in the Cornell case, do the two accused students who were expelled still have degrees with the university’s name?</p>
<p>I had a similar question about suspensions. The average person would think if a student is suspended, they can’t take any courses for however long their suspension is. Eventually, they can resume their education and get a degree. But suspension in Title IX very often means that the student has graduated and that that is a period of time that they have to wait until they can apply to graduate school, for example. There was a case in my field study where the perpetrator was suspended. The university then shrugged its shoulders and said, “Whoops, he already graduated. We would’ve suspended him, so now he can’t come back to campus for two years. Afterward, he’s welcome to come back for a graduate degree.” But it turned out that the perpetrator had not fulfilled the requirements for his degree, so he had not graduated. The university postponed his suspension by a semester so that he’d be able to. The survivor was irate. She said something like, “That’s so fucking stupid. You confer the degree. You make the rules. You could change that, but you don’t want to.”</p>
<p>Universities have a lot of these outcomes, which look good on paper but mean nothing in practice. They wanted to be able to say they suspended a student for sexual assault, but they didn’t wanna<em> actually </em>suspend a student for sexual assault in a way that would have any impact on his life.</p>
<p>Meanwhile, you have Jane Doe asking for her tuition back and Cornell allegedly denying her. That strikes me as a big difference, too, between the 2010s and now. Back then, there was a real focus on offsetting the harms of sexual violence so survivors could have an equal education. Schools give students their money back in all sorts of situations. It was a no-brainer that a survivor who asks for their tuition back would get it.</p>
<p>This is one of those situations where university officials across the board may try to blame a decision like that on the Trump regulations, but the rules explicitly state that universities are still required to offer supportive measures to students whether or not they report. This is something Jane Doe should have been able to get.</p>
<p>There are other ways in which the Trump regulation has shaped things. When victims file a report now, a lot of them say that they get lectures about due process and that it’s really important that the process is fair to their perpetrator’s rights. This is not explicitly in the rules, but the Trump administration was so focused on “due process” that now survivors are made to feel guilty about reporting. The way I talk about it in my book is that the Trump administration gave universities cover for what they were already doing behind closed doors. I started my fieldwork almost two years before his regulations went into effect. I asked administrators, “What are you gonna have to do to change your policies and procedures based on this?” The Title IX coordinator at that school told me, “Oh, we’re barely gonna need to change anything. This is already stuff we’ve been doing.”</p>
<p><strong>How do we fix these issues? Is that even possible?</strong><br/>
We cannot make the same mistake of the Obama and Biden administrations, handing the power back to schools. We need to make Title IX as independent as possible. We cannot trust schools to put the survivors’ interests ahead of their own. So much of what I found was that universities were violating their own policies constantly, doing a completely different process than what they told students they would do or what federal regulation required.</p>
<p>A recommendation is to move as much of the Title IX process as possible out of universities and into the states. That has been tried in California, where victim advocates are now hired and fired by the state government. This structures them as if they’re an independent state agency, but they still have offices on campus and they’re still easily available to the university community. That means that, if they are too good at advocating for survivors, they won’t get fired or pushed out of the institution — which is something that I’ve seen.</p>
<p>During my research, I was really particularly shocked by how normal this all felt to administrators. They didn’t try to hide what they were doing. They felt completely morally justified. It’s wild because during the first Trump administration, there were university presidents and other officials who came out very overtly against his Title IX regulations. They said they were misogynistic and that they went too far. Privately, however, the sentiment was that they really preferred them. Generally, they really were eager to get back to a time when there was no oversight on Title IX and they could discriminate as much or as little as they wanted against survivors.</p>
