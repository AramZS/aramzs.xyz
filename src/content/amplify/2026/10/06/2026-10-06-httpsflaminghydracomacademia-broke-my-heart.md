<p>Some people with doctorates like to show the world what they’ve done. They write their names with “Ph.D.” as a suffix, or refer to themselves as “Dr. So-and-so.” I do not. Instead I call myself a recovering academic, because getting my Ph.D. was a form of intellectual trauma that I will carry with me for the rest of my life.</p>
<p>I entered graduate school at the age of twenty, which means I was getting fucked up on Marx and Foucault when most of my peers were slamming shots. Both were natural responses to devoting ourselves to a life in the humanities when the university system was crumbling around us. Things are worse now, but even in the late twentieth century, getting a tenure-track job was less likely than scoring a record deal with a major label. Unfortunately, I was so young and inexperienced that I had no way of understanding this.</p>
<p>My parents were both English teachers—my mother in high school and my father in community college—and they wanted something better for me. To them, “better” meant getting a Ph.D. and working as a professor at an elite research institution. They were what you might call backstage academic parents. They punished me brutally if I earned any grade lower than an A, and constantly schemed about how I would get to that top tier university as quickly as possible. At their urging, I dropped out of high school after 11th grade, mashed my face into a double course load at my local community college, and matriculated at UC Berkeley as a junior at the age of eighteen. My two years as an undergraduate were a blur: German, French, Latin, Middle English (Chaucerian and the Welsh-inflected version written by the Gawain Poet), Modern English, and a lot of weed for good measure. I was at Berkeley, so the latter was practically a requirement.</p>
