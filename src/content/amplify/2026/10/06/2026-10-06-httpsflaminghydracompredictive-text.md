<p>My wife says that when I talk about artificial intelligence and the possible end of humanity, I sound excited. I tell her this is absurd. We have two children. I would prefer humanity to survive. She says she is not accusing me of wanting catastrophe. She is telling me what she sees on my face.</p>
<p>When I was six, we lived in Orunia, then a poor district of Gdańsk. My grandmother was dying of cancer. One day I was in a dark garage full of grease with some boys. I told them about the cancer, the bedsores and my grandmother’s suffering. I spoke for a long time. They listened. I could see they felt sorry for her. I was glad they understood. I was also excited that the words had worked.</p>
<p>As a child, like generations of Polish children, I knew a poem by Julian Tuwim about another Grześ, a diminutive of my own name, Grzegorz.</p>
<p>His aunt asks whether he posted a letter for her. He says he did. She asks him to give his word. He gives it. Then he remembers everything: the red mailbox, the envelope, the address in Łódź, the stamp, a tall officer, a taxi, a carriage, a cow, a bus, three girls passing by. His aunt listens. Then she tells him she never gave him a letter.</p>
