<p>In a way, I grew up in Gilead. Although maybe it turns out, in a way, that we all did. But perhaps I did in a more real way than many.</p>
<p>I was an Orthodox Jewish girl. In the world I grew up in, photographs are doctored to paint longer sleeves and longer skirts on to representations of the past, to pretend that whatever we’re doing now is what we’ve always done – the essence of fundamentalism. In that world, women sat behind screens during prayers and I was taught as a sure and certain fact that if a man looked at me with desire, that was a cause for sin for him, and that sin was my fault. I was told – by my mother, in fact, the same mother who encouraged me to study philosophy at Oxford, a tangle I will never be able to fully untie – that women’s brains just aren’t equipped to learn Talmud.</p>
<p>And certainly, being raised in a very religious community as I was, learning and reading the Bible as the immutable word of God, I was taught about the system in Genesis in which Jacob’s wife, Rebecca, can’t have a baby and so she gives him her handmaid, and they conceive a child together, and that child is considered Rebecca’s child. I’d been taught that idea, and taught that it was beautiful. A moving expression of women’s generosity and mutual support.</p>
<p>Which it can be, of course. One woman freely offering to carry a child for another who can’t – there’s no more beautiful expression of love and generosity on Earth.</p>

<p>But “freely” is a load-bearing word there. Margaret Atwood is remarkable for going to the heart of things. She really thought about what this story meant, more than I ever had as a teenager when I first encountered The Handmaid’s Tale. When I was taught about Jacob and Rebecca, we sort of skated over the “gives him her handmaid” part. Over the consent part. Over the sexual assault part. We sort of skated over the part where Genesis is a book from the bronze age and – like the works of Homer – accurately depicts the morality of bronze age Mediterranean societies, a time when women were a kind of possession and could be given as a gift, won or lost in battle or even a sporting contest.</p>
<p>Nothing wrong with a bronze ege book accurately depicting the morality of the bronze age. Nothing wrong with studying it closely and appreciating our similarities and differences with that ancient world, as we do when we read Homer. Some extremely wrong things about taking those ways of life as the revealed and unalterable word of God and trying to enact them today.</p>
<p>I think if I was perhaps – only perhaps – unusual in being raised around people who did believe in biblical literalism 50 years ago, it’s not unusual now to have had your life affected by those beliefs.</p>
<p>In all the ways that fiction can be real, the Gilead of Atwood’s novels The Handmaid’s Tale and The Testaments is real. It’s been real all along. It is the name of a force trying to push itself back into the world. It is those ideas that a person can be property, that might is right and the victor can do whatever he pleases. It is the bronze age coming for us again and again.</p>

<p>There are women today around the world, indeed in the United States, who are being forced to carry to term babies who were conceived by rape. Since the publication of The Handmaid’s Tale, the supreme court of the US has overturned the Roe v Wade ruling. Many states have banned abortion outright, including the morning-after pill. Many would like to see a ban on contraception. In 2025, Reuters reported that the US defence secretary had reposted a video of a conservative pastor advocating for the repeal of the 19th amendment. That’s the amendment that gives women the right to vote. When they tell you who they are, believe them. It’s happening now, inch by inch.</p>
<p>In all of her far-sightedness, the one thing Atwood did not foresee is that Gilead would be born again online. There are forces with enormous amounts of money, power and influence that would like this to happen in countries other than the US too. Afghanistan under the Taliban is already Gilead; the women there forced to give up the lives and freedoms they had only a handful of years ago. But Afghanistan certainly isn’t enough for the people who would like to see global Gilead. They turn their attention, like the all-seeing eye under which the residents of Gilead live, to countries around the world where they can bankroll political movements that were only an extreme fringe a few years ago.</p>
<p>They flood our information systems with fake accounts and fake images to convince us we’re under threat. They teach young men the old stories about being owed sex, about women’s bodies being designed to tempt men, about male sexual frustration being women’s fault. They talk about the past as a golden age in which men and women knew their places. They find cracks in the solidarity between women, wedge issues where women can be convinced to go to war against each other instead of keeping our attention on the great threats that confront us all. They stick the chisel in. They drive it home.</p>

<p>How does Gilead happen? Millimetre by millimetre. While you’re looking the other way. While you’re telling yourself it couldn’t happen here.</p>
<p>Gilead is the dark shadow self of the US. But Gilead can happen anywhere. In every city in the world, in certain streets and certain homes, some women are already living in Gilead.</p>
<p>Gilead has always been real to me. Perhaps women’s rights and women’s freedoms have seemed solid and immutable to others but they’ve never felt that way to me. We are living in – for the history of the world – an extraordinarily new way, with our ideas of freedom, self-sovereignty and self-determination. The old ways are still there, buried just under the surface. It doesn’t take much for them to climb back out into the light.</p>
<p>Sometimes I feel despair about it.</p>
<p>There are social factors. Aunt Lydia, perhaps the most complex and interesting character in Atwood’s pair of novels, is a prime example. Solidarity between women is what will save us, but solidarity between women is hard to create and preserve. The world around us likes to set women against each other, offering every woman a little reward if she is willing to say that other kinds of women are doing it wrong. There is no right way available to be a woman. However a woman lives, she is always wrong. It is wrong to be pretty: frivolous, silly and probably “asking for it”. But it is also wrong to be unattractive: “letting yourself go’. It is wrong for a woman to be old: dried up, fertility gone. But it is also wrong for a woman to be young: whatever young women are interested in is characterised as stupid and trivial. It is wrong for a woman not to be a mother, but if she is a mother then she is probably a bad one; any problems her children have are her fault, while their successes probably mean she is “trying too hard”. It is always easy for a woman to gain some power by condemning other women; there are always grounds to do it. Aunt Lydia in The Handmaid’s Tale takes that road, and becomes powerful with it. How are we to possibly organise ourselves if the incentives are always to take this easy road?</p>

<p>There are demographic factors. Fundamentalist religious people – societies that are willing to treat women purely as units for the manufacture of babies – by definition end up having more babies than societies that are not. Women who are free to use birth control have fewer children than those who are not. In a democracy – one person, one vote – slowly but inexorably, the parts of society that have more children will gain an advantage at the ballot box. It takes a while. But they’ve got a while. A sense of divine mission gives you that multi-generation perspective. One person, one vote. If you have more people, you get more power. If you start Gilead tomorrow with a few dozen families and make it the centre of their beliefs that they have to try to have 20 children each, and wait a few hundred years… the logic of combining democracy with birth control is that Gilead is always trying to be born.</p>
<p>Why not give up now, then? If the very nature of democracy is to reward schemes and systems that strongarm women into doing nothing but bearing children, why bother fighting for any of it? Won’t this always be a losing battle?</p>
<p>Atwood’s two novels contain the most powerful answers to this question.</p>
<p>The first answer is in The Handmaid’s Tale: it is that we are right, and they are wrong. I can’t go back and tell the heroes of the bronze age epics that the women they buy and sell, rape and kill as sexual slaves are full humans with independent thought who deserve to be in charge of their own lives. But I know that it is true of every woman alive today. This is the story of Offred, the narrator of The Handmaid’s Tale. Her story, her telling of her own story, is the first refutation. Gilead wants her to be silent, but she refuses. It wants her to forget that she ever learned to read and write, but she will not. Gilead tries to erase all of her that is individual, turning her into simply a vessel for children. But she records her story. Gilead is simply wrong. It is wrong about what women are, what people are; its stories about biology as destiny are factually incorrect. When people said that women’s minds don’t work well enough to study philosophy or mathematics, when they said we would get prolapse from exercise or that we were simply too mentally weak to make decisions about our own lives – and people do still say these things – they are simply spreading falsehoods. They cannot make it so by saying it. The truth, in the end, comes out.</p>

<p>The second answer is the answer in The Testaments, which is that people naturally, instinctively and overwhelmingly want what is good for their children. It’s true of all people, not necessarily more true of women than of men. But it is true that a woman will fight through any obstacle the world can throw at her to give her children a better life. You can put a woman in Gilead, you can teach women that Gilead is the only way to live or, the best way, you can get them to a stage where they will teach their daughters the same things that pulled them down. But most women still want – somehow – to hurl their daughters a little further out of the mire than they were able to clamber themselves. We can’t do it all with any single generation, but we keep going. My mother might have told me my brain wasn’t suitable for studying Talmud, but she bloody wanted me to go to Oxford.</p>
<p>If it is untrue that women are less mentally capable than men (and it is untrue), then women know this. And however much you try to teach us different, the truth will keep spilling out. However much we accepted for ourselves, the idea of passing it on to our daughters will keep appalling us.</p>
<p>As a pair, Atwood’s two brilliant novels – full of invention, of humour, of excitement, of insight – give us the answers. Gilead will keep on coming; it will never truly be over. But we will never stop fighting.</p>
