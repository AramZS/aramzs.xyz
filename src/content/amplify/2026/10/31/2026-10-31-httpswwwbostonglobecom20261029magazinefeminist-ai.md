<p>In <a href="https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/08/11/opinion/musk-altman-tech-right-utopianism/?p1=BGSearch_Overlay_Results&amp;p1=Article_Inline_Text_Link">Sam Altman</a>’s favorite movie, <i>Her,</i> <a href="https://www.bostonglobe.com/2025/09/01/nation/drones-blasting-acdc-scarlett-johansson-are-helping-biologists-protect-cattle-wolves/?p1=BGSearch_Advanced_Results&amp;p1=Article_Inline_Text_Link">Scarlett Johansson</a> voices a warm, empathetic, and flirtatious AI assistant who becomes romantically involved with her human user. The OpenAI CEO called the film “prophetic.” But in 2024, he was famously accused by Johansson of <a href="https://www.bostonglobe.com/2024/05/20/business/scarlett-johansson-chatgpt-voice/?p1=BGSearch_Advanced_Results&amp;p1=Article_Inline_Text_Link">stealing her voice</a> for the company’s Sky voice assistant.</p>
<p>Altman had wanted Johansson to become the voice of ChatGPT. She declined, and said she was “shocked, angered, and in disbelief” when OpenAI released its assistant with a voice that sounded so much like her that “my closest friends and news outlets could not tell the difference.” The voice was dropped, but the incident underscored a sense of entitlement in Silicon Valley over women’s voices, bodies, and livelihoods — not to mention outright degradation and dehumanization — that has worsened ever since.</p>
<p>It’s not just OpenAI. Meta’s AI-powered Ray-Ban smart glasses were dubbed <a href="https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cwp80l0my1x2o">“pervert glasses”</a> over concerns about privacy and harassment. Across the country, police officers have been accused of misusing <a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/2026/08/04/how-one-tip-exposed-hidden-pattern-police-surveillance-misuse/">Flock cameras</a> to stalk their ex-partners. And Elon Musk’s SpaceXAI has faced global backlash after the chatbot Grok generated a <a href="https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/03/16/business/teens-allege-musks-grok-chatbot-made-sexual-images-them-minors/?p1=BGSearch_Overlay_Results&amp;p1=Article_Inline_Text_Link">flood of sexualized images</a> of women and children without consent.</p>

<p>The list goes on, and the lesson remains constant: Corporate AI models and the people who create them cannot be trusted to respect women. It also invites a radical question: <i>What would AI look like if it were feminist? </i></p>
<p>As researchers, we have each spent over two decades examining and designing feminist technology and believe a version of AI that respects people and the planet is possible, but achieving it will require a very different approach than the one being forced upon us.</p>

<p>For our 2020 book, <a href="https://data-feminism.mitpress.mit.edu/"><i>Data Feminism,</i></a> we wrote about transforming the tools and methods of data science into levers that emphasize connection, community, and the joy and richness of life — all components of feminist ethics. Consider this: Instead of stealing our voices and abusing our bodies, AI could work for gender equality and disrupt patterns of violence. Instead of propping up the techbro billionaire class that is hellbent on hoarding wealth and power, it could support fairer and more democratic processes.</p>
<p>An example: Since 2019, the Data Against Feminicide project — co-led by Catherine, one of the authors of this article — has collaborated with human rights activists in Latin America to design tools to support their work to end <a href="https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/10/15/world/an-increase-killings-women-cameroon-spurs-protests-demands-better-laws/?p1=BGSearch_Overlay_Results&amp;p1=Article_Inline_Text_Link">fatal, gender-related violence</a>. The system uses AI to scan tens of thousands of news articles a day and surface cases of feminicide to be included in databases and statistics. This information is used to create family-support resources and advocate for change.</p>
<p>The activists who co-designed the tools had a say in which aspects of their work were appropriate for automation. Users of the system retained all ownership of their data. Such elements are crucial in a feminist approach to AI because, by allowing for greater control over data and how it is used, they buffer against corporations extracting our information to profit in reprehensible ways.</p>
<p>In her role as director of the Atlanta Interdisciplinary AI Network, Lauren, co-author of this piece, has long used AI (or “machine learning,” as it used to be called) to research the contributions of women — particularly Black women — to social justice movements, including efforts to end slavery in the United States. This research embraces state-of-the-art modeling approaches that — unlike the Claudes and ChatGPTs of the world — are open source, fully documented, and do not rely upon copyrighted material.</p>
<p>These women’s stories are placed back into historical context by a research team that understands how they were shaped by their particular time and place. This is also key to the feminist approach: Only when AI is understood as one tool among many — and guided by human expertise — can it contribute to our collective knowledge.</p>
<p>Creating such an AI ecosystem — one that benefits all people — would require re-engineering the system itself by replacing Big Tech’s digital infrastructure with something that belongs to us, the public. Instead of shareholder governance, we need a system built on accountability and transparency, one that supports our civic institutions rather than undermining them. Only then can we build digital infrastructure that advances our collective knowledge and equally distributes risks and rewards.</p>
<p>Abolishing Big Tech may seem audacious, but the grass-roots movements against extractive <a href="https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/10/22/business/data-centers-pushback-tech-giants/?p1=BGSearch_Overlay_Results&amp;p1=Article_Inline_Text_Link">data centers</a> and Flock cameras show that people can organize to fight for a better digital future.</p>
<p>We can take inspiration from acts of protest of all sizes, including when citizens in Quilicura, Chile, decided to “<a href="https://apnews.com/article/ai-human-chatbot-chile-quiliai-data-centers-water-a798d7d1ae3b2fd7646ec5dbfc8a18dd">become AI for a day,</a>” answering over 25,000 prompts in their own words to call attention to the impact of the region’s AI infrastructure on their local waterways. Close to home, Catherine and fellow MIT faculty lead weekly walks to <i>“bedazzle”</i> <a href="https://www.cambridgeday.com/2026/10/07/mit-professors-bedazzle-cameras-protest/">surveillance cameras</a> on their campus with rhinestones and googly eyes to catalyze conversations about their AI capabilities.</p>
<p>The bottom line is that we deserve more than what’s being imposed on us. As political activist Angela Davis once said, “You have to act as if it were possible to radically transform the world. And you have to do it all the time.” This is the promise of feminist AI.</p>
