<p>I caught the first wave of Covid in New York City in April 2020. By May of that year my hands had swelled up and turned bright red, and my shoulders ached so much that I couldn’t sleep through the night without waking up crying in pain. I wasn’t sure what was happening and could only hope it was temporary: The guidance at the time was to avoid medical offices and hospitals unless you were dying. All I could do was wait for things to get back to “normal,” both for the world and for my own body.</p>

<p>Of course, waiting to see if you just get better is a first line of treatment for many even in normal times. It’s cautiously optimistic and technically free. I didn’t have good insurance at the time, having signed up for the cheapest health plan available to me via Oscar, a for-profit health insurance company founded with venture capital that advertised heavily on the subway. I settled for a makeshift heat pack made from a tube sock filled with rice, heated in a microwave, and paired with an intensive Advil regimen.</p>
<p>My treatment plan didn’t help at all. Still—too scared of the costs and of facing the disease—it took me about a year to seek help, finally winding up at Mount Sinai, where I was diagnosed with rheumatoid arthritis, an autoimmune condition that attacks your own joints.</p>

<p>What I’ve learned over the five years since beginning treatment is that being chronically ill is tedious and expensive. Trying to get my insurance to stop denying my medications and treatments, I’ve had to go from my cheap Oscar plan to more and more expensive options. In 2021, I was paying less than $200 a month. Now my premium for the Gold tier is just over $1,500. I’ve been failed upward through all the tiers of drugs, from the oldest and worst (cheapest for insurance) to newer, much more expensive specialty drugs. It took me four years to find a treatment program that gives me something like a normal life.</p>

<p>The perks of this regimen are few—mostly snacks and drinks during hospital visits, including water bottles poetically branded “Pure Life”—and the costs are many. I’ve gotten hundreds of vials of blood drawn; had endless X-rays, MRIs, ultrasounds, and intravenous medication treatments; and attended countless physical therapy appointments. I’ve been hit with surprise bills, denials of treatment, and copays costing thousands of dollars. I have taken unpaid time off to visit the doctor. I have had to quit jobs I enjoyed in order to take mind-numbing work that could actually cover my expenses. I’ve had to rethink my whole life—not just to get better, but to afford getting better.</p>

<p>Through all of this, my medical team has haggled with the insurance company on my behalf. Insurance has never felt like a partner, but instead like an obstacle to treatment. Every plan I’ve been on has its own unique shortcomings to navigate, whether it’s being forced to continually swap to different testing companies or being denied my IV drug treatment after switching to my employer-sponsored health plan.</p>

<p>I have an old sharps bin full of used Humira doses I’ve held on to for years as a reminder of my journey (and also because I’m not totally sure where to throw the medical waste). The bin has become a totem for my complicated feelings around my chronic illness. One of my doctors once told me, “It’s the best time in human history to have rheumatoid arthritis.” I might agree with the basic sentiment but can’t help but wonder if this applies to America specifically.</p>
