<h2>Misleading anecdotes, flawed data, and oversimplified analyses are at the heart of a narrative of Jewish exclusion promoted by the free expression organization, the ADL, and others.</h2>
<p><strong>“</strong><a href="https://pen.org/a-silent-moratorium-interviews-with-israeli-and-jewish-writers/"><strong>A Silent Moratorium</strong></a><strong>”</strong>—an article released on July 9th by the literary free speech organization PEN America alleging the “rising isolation and exclusion” of Jewish and Israeli writers in American publishing—<a href="https://web.archive.org/web/20260710035835/https://pen.org/a-silent-moratorium-interviews-with-israeli-and-jewish-writers/">began</a> with a striking claim: “Jerusalem-based literary agent Deborah Harris represents dozens of leading voices of Israeli literature, from established titans to debut authors of ‘literary gems.’ Her agency typically sells five to 10 literary novels a year into the United States. Since Oct. 7, 2023, they have not sold a single one.”</p>
<p>The problem is, that claim was false. The Deborah Harris Agency has sold at least two literary novels by Jews since October 7th, 2023, one American and one Israeli: Benjamin Resnick’s <i>Next Stop</i> was sold to Avid Reader and reported in <i>Publishers Marketplace</i> on November 9th, 2023, and Tehila Hakimi’s <i>Hunting in America</i> was sold to Penguin and reported on January 29th, 2024. When we asked Harris about Hakimi’s book, she at first insisted it was “sold way before the war,” but then acknowledged, in response to follow-up questioning, that it “was sold afterwards and it was a huge surprise for us.” Resnick, a Conservative rabbi, confirmed that his offer was made a few weeks after October 7th.</p>
<p>A <a href="https://s3.us-east-1.amazonaws.com/jewish-currents/Harris_Agency_US_Literary_2000-2026.pdf">closer</a> <a href="https://s3.us-east-1.amazonaws.com/jewish-currents/Harris_Agency_US_Fiction_All_2000-2026.pdf">look</a> at the agency’s track record over the last two and half decades—gleaned from <i>Publishers Marketplace</i>, the agency’s own website, and our bibliographic research—suggests that the Deborah Harris Agency has not sold five to ten “literary novels” a year, as claimed, but an average of one to two annually since 2000. This figure has remained mostly consistent after October 7th, 2023, even amid a <a href="https://authorsguild.org/news/key-takeaways-from-2023-author-income-survey/">difficult</a> <a href="https://www.esquire.com/entertainment/books/a60924704/debut-fiction-challenges/">market</a> for literary fiction writ large. The agency, meanwhile, continues to do healthy business in other genres, including horror, mystery, romantasy, and children’s books. Harris, contacted for comment, explained that the “five to ten” number “includes adult titles, not only literary fiction.” (After receiving a list of our findings, PEN America issued a partial correction on their article on September 28th regarding the number of literary novels Deborah Harris sells annually and the month in which she last sold one.)</p>
<p>As researchers of publishing history, with expertise between us in contemporary Jewish literature and quantitative literary studies, we reviewed the claims of “A Silent Moratorium,” which asserts that Jewish and Israeli writers face “blatant hostility, discrimination, and hate” and attempts to chart the “impact on their freedom of expression.” Digging into its anecdotal reports of abandonment by agents and missing book deals, online harassment and review-bombing, we found that many of the claims were too vague to qualify as evidence, and that many accounts, like Harris’s, turn out to be imprecise at best and entirely false at worst. (When reached for comment, PEN America said: “We believe that sharing the experiences of writers who describe exclusion, self-censorship, or barriers to participation in literary life is fully consistent with our mission to protect all writers, no matter their background or viewpoint.” They wrote that in addition to the “dozens of writers and literary professionals” they interviewed for the original article, “we have since heard from other writers who shared that the piece also reflects their own experiences.”)</p>
<p>Digging into PEN America’s anecdotal reports, we found that many of the claims were too vague to qualify as evidence, and that many accounts turn out to be imprecise at best and entirely false at worst.</p>
<p>The PEN article affirms a narrative advanced <a href="https://www.jta.org/2026/07/16/ideas/i-gathered-the-data-on-jewish-fiction-publishing-the-trends-are-alarming">across</a> <a href="https://jewishinsider.com/2024/11/petition-jewish-writers-palestinian-festival-of-literature-israel/">Jewish</a> <a href="https://www.washingtonjewishweek.com/too-jewish-to-publish/">media</a> and <a href="https://www.wsj.com/arts-culture/books/antisemitism-books-literary-publishing-world-high-line-78267985">mainstream press</a> alike that—as the <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2024/05/27/opinion/publishing-literary-antisemitism.html">headline</a> of a piece by James Kirchick in <i>The New York Times </i>put it—“a chill has fallen over Jews in publishing” amid the war on Gaza. A week after the publication of the PEN article, the writer Meg Keene published a piece <a href="https://blogs.timesofisrael.com/i-gathered-the-data-on-jewish-fiction-publishing-the-trends-are-alarming/">in JTA</a>, purporting to back up the anecdotal nature of such reports with hard data. We also audited Keene’s data, which has been cited <a href="https://www.thefp.com/p/simon-schuster-susan-abulhawa-antisemitism-palestine-israel">alongside</a> the PEN piece in accounts of antisemitism in publishing. In examining both her methodology, which she shared with us, and her results, we found that the data she gathered was not just “imperfect,” as Keene <a href="https://www.jta.org/2026/07/16/ideas/i-gathered-the-data-on-jewish-fiction-publishing-the-trends-are-alarming">called it</a>, but a substantial undercount of the Jewish books actually sold in a given year.</p>
<p>Keene also fails to account for longer-term patterns: When we tracked publishing deals since 2000 using a slightly expanded methodology adapted from Keene’s, 2025—while decidedly less robust than 2023—turns out to be the <i>third-best year of the past quarter-century</i> (tied with 2016) for book deals that publicly signaled Jewish or Israeli content. Our analysis found that much of what accounts for the drop-off in publications in Keene’s data is the fact that 2023 happened to be a high-water mark for Jewish fiction in the US since 2000. From this perspective, the years since October 7th illustrate less a precipitous decline than simply a return to the mean. (Notes on our methodology and how it departs from Keene’s, alongside all of our data, can be found <a href="https://s3.us-east-1.amazonaws.com/jewish-currents/JC26_Meg_Keene_Data_Report.pdf">here</a>.) Reached for comment, Keene said, “I was very upfront about the limitations of my data.” She called the comparison between her data and ours “intellectually dishonest” because it is an “entirely different dataset.”</p>
<p>In <a href="https://x.com/MegKeene/status/2064442218958692375">social</a> <a href="https://www.instagram.com/p/DZYN7CFyGjs/?hl=en">media</a> posts this summer, Keene announced that she would be “partnering with @ADL [the Anti-Defamation League] to create a data driven report on antisemitism in publishing.” That report, published by the ADL today, October 1st, notably does not mention Keene’s data. It does, however, clarify the political uses of the narrative promoted by PEN America about an anti-Jewish publishing climate. The ADL’s report features quotes by a handful of right-wing activists (without acknowledging them as such), conflates Jews and Zionists, and opposes boycotts—all familiar strategies to suppress leftist Jewish and pro-Palestinian expression.</p>
<p>In addition to our review of claims and data by PEN America, Keene, and the ADL, we spoke to more than two dozen Jewish and Israeli literary insiders—translators, agents, editors, writers, and publishers—about the welter of factors currently influencing decision-making at publishing houses large and small. Many of the people we interviewed, across the ideological spectrum, described a challenging market, and acknowledged that PEN’s article dramatically oversimplified the post-October 7th navigation of Israel/Palestine in the publishing world. Noah Schwartzberg, an executive editor at the Random House imprint Portfolio Books, told us that the nature of literary gatekeeping and decision-making means authors rarely have a clear picture about why their work isn’t getting published. “Very few editors are going to say, ‘You’re not a very good writer and your ideas don’t deserve a public hearing,’” he said. “It is such an obscure and opaque business for people outside of it that I understand the desire to make up stories about why things are happening.”</p>
<p>There were some among the writers and publishing professionals we interviewed who shared the recent articles’ sense of ostracism and alarm; in fact, we spoke to many of the same sources in PEN America’s piece. In our interview, Harris spoke movingly about the relationships she has lost: Jewish editors and publishing professionals in the US who used to “hug me and kiss me in Frankfurt and London,” and “had dinner in my house on Friday night when they’re in Israel,” and now won’t return her calls. “It feels very personal,” she said. Many others shared frustrations, disappointments, and feelings of bewilderment.</p>
<p>But we also spoke to many Jewish and Israeli writers and publishing professionals who disputed the portrait offered by the PEN America article, and who expressed frustration that the article claims to speak on their behalf while mischaracterizing their experiences. Ben Moser, who recently published a new book on the history of Jewish anti-Zionism and who identifies as anti-Zionist himself, told us the piece made him feel that “Jews like me, we just don’t count as Jews.”</p>
<p>Ben Moser, who recently published a new book on the history of Jewish anti-Zionism, told us the piece made him feel that “Jews like me, we just don’t count as Jews.”</p>
<p>Moser agrees that publishing has changed over the last three years, but he believes the shift concerns attitudes toward Zionism, not Judaism or Jews. Zionism, which enjoyed “unanimity in a lot of spaces until very recently,” no longer does, he said. The cultural boycott of Israel <a href="https://www.nbcnews.com/pop-culture/pop-culture-news/hollywood-stars-thousands-pledge-not-work-israeli-film-institutions-rcna230463">has</a> <a href="https://www.wunc.org/2025-11-13/why-more-than-1-000-musicians-are-boycotting-israel-with-no-music-for-genocide">gained</a> <a href="https://www.haaretz.com/israel-news/2024-10-29/ty-article/.premium/1-000-literary-figures-boycott-israeli-publishers-complicit-in-palestinian-oppression/00000192-d832-d4a2-ab97-d93f8bd90000">traction</a> as many writers and publishing professionals in the US grapple with how to respond to what an overwhelming number of <a href="https://www.ohchr.org/en/press-releases/2025/09/israel-has-committed-genocide-gaza-strip-un-commission-finds">experts</a> <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2025/07/15/opinion/israel-gaza-holocaust-genocide-palestinians.html">have</a> <a href="https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/news/2024/12/amnesty-international-concludes-israel-is-committing-genocide-against-palestinians-in-gaza/">called</a> a <a href="https://genocidescholars.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/IAGS-Resolution-on-Gaza-FINAL.pdf">genocide</a> <a href="https://jewishcurrents.org/a-textbook-case-of-genocide">in</a> <a href="https://www.ohchr.org/sites/default/files/documents/hrbodies/hrcouncil/sessions-regular/session55/advance-versions/a-hrc-55-73-auv.pdf">Gaza</a>. These moral considerations may have a market dimension, as risk-averse publishers try to read the tea leaves. As a Hebrew-language article in <i>Israel Hayom</i> phrased it earlier this year, summarizing the perspective of Israeli writer Iddo Gefen, “The reluctance to feature Israeli writers is often not connected to anti-Israel sentiment at all, but simply concerns about controversial events” that similarly affect Palestinian writers.</p>
<p>PEN America’s “A Silent Moratorium” responds to these circumstances clumsily, applying a reductive explanation—antisemitism—to varied claims with myriad, overlapping variables. In doing so, it fuels a campaign that has otherwise been forthrightly conservative and partisan, as in today’s ADL report, which cites PEN’s “comparatively cautious article,” and its fallout, as evidence of antisemitism in US publishing.</p>
<p>In lending its imprimatur to the flawed report, PEN America is “trying to find a way to convince themselves that Zionism and their purported values can be reconciled,” said Palestinian American writer, translator, and journalist Sarah Aziza. More broadly, Aziza characterized these seemingly “good faith efforts” to defend free expression as just “another tortured contortion to maintain a posture of liberal high-mindedness while coming to the defense of a state that’s perpetrated genocide.”</p>

<p><strong>PEN America</strong> <strong>has been battling</strong> acute attacks on its reputation regarding its treatment of Israel/Palestine since soon after October 7th. Writers Against the War on Gaza (WAWOG) began protesting the organization in January 2024, <a href="https://lithub.com/palestinian-american-writer-randa-jarrar-was-dragged-out-of-a-pen-event/">disrupting an event</a> in Los Angeles and circulating an <a href="https://www.publishersweekly.com/pw/newsbrief/index.html?record=4506">open letter</a> demanding action in defense of Palestinians. The effort severely curtailed PEN America’s work over the last several years, resulting in the cancellation of its 2024 <a href="https://pen.org/press-release/pen-america-statement-regarding-the-2024-literary-awards-ceremony-and-conferral-of-annual-prizes/">awards ceremony</a> and <a href="https://apnews.com/article/pen-america-world-voices-canceled-israel-palestinians-4b2214eb8eb38086edd95fc81750d821">World Voices Festival</a>. The organization’s CEO, Suzanne Nossel, <a href="https://apnews.com/article/pen-america-ceo-suzanne-nossel-stepping-down-81e590c30ea7955a178ec76bf2599d63">stepped down</a> in late 2024 and was replaced by Summer Lopez and Clarisse Rosaz Shariyf. The boycott was <a href="https://writersagainstthewarongaza.com/statements/pen-america-boycott-lifted">lifted</a> in December 2025.</p>
<p>The release of “A Silent Moratorium” has upended the organization’s attempt to turn the page on a destabilizing period. The day after it was published, PEN America’s president, the novelist Dinaw Mengestu, resigned, <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/07/10/arts/pen-america-dinaw-mengestu-resigns.html">telling</a> <i>The New York</i> <i>Times</i>, “This report is not an isolated incident,” but rather it continues an “approach toward defending some rights while not defending others.” In an <a href="https://lithub.com/why-pen-america-will-not-defend-you-against-anti-bds-legislation/">article</a> in <i>Literary Hub</i> explaining his resignation, Mengestu pointed to PEN’s contradictory position on cultural boycotts: The organization <a href="https://pen.org/a-silent-moratorium-interviews-with-israeli-and-jewish-writers/">claims</a> to defend “the rights of writers to [boycott] without facing retaliation,” while also maintaining that boycotts “threaten the free expression rights” of those from boycotted countries. “For lawyers working to defend and expand anti-BDS legislation in court, PEN America’s new boycott position is clarifying,” wrote Mengestu, asserting that the organization’s conflation of Zionism and Jewish identity aligned with “a critical argument in Title VI civil rights claims used to argue that BDS is harassment.”</p>
<p>PEN America United (PAU), the union representing PEN staff, <a href="https://www.instagram.com/united_pen/p/Da6AqL6GD6e/">posted</a> in solidarity with Mengestu’s decision to resign. The statement noted that “several [PAU] members working in communications and in relevant programmatic areas . . . had no input into the research, drafting, editorial process, or publication of the piece.” It also said that employees raised concerns about the report with senior leadership upon its publication, which were “dismissed.” Liesl Gerntholtz, an advocate for Palestinian writers within PEN America and the lead author on a PEN America report on “the cultural destruction of Gaza,” <a href="https://jewishcurrents.org/pen-america-staffer-who-led-work-on-palestine-is-resigning">resigned</a> from the organization on September 11th.</p>
<p>Nonetheless, PEN America leadership has stood behind the article. Co-CEOs Lopez and Rosaz Shariyf released a<a href="https://pen.org/a-statement-to-our-community/"> statement</a> on July 15th, saying “We are proud to have published this piece.” They pointed out that PEN has acknowledged, in the article and elsewhere, that what is happening in Gaza is a genocide, and that it has protested the enforcement of anti-boycott laws, objected to the use of the IHRA definition of antisemitism, and called it “dangerous” to “conflate anti-Israel expression with anti-Jewish bias.” (WAWOG <a href="https://writersagainstthewarongaza.com/boycott-pen-america">claims</a> that at least some of this was done in response to activist pressure.) The novelist Phil Klay, a PEN board member, <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/07/15/arts/pen-america-israel-gaza-report.html">defended</a> the report in a remark to the <i>Times</i>, calling it “strong.” He was <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/17/arts/pen-america-president-phil-klay.html">recently appointed</a> the new PEN America president.</p>
<p>Still, “A Silent Moratorium” is an unusual document for PEN America. Sources at the organization <a href="https://archive.md/Y6D3x">told</a> Gal Beckerman at <i>The Atlantic</i> that the piece “took months to produce—an unusual length of time . . . in part because of the scrutiny that such reporting would surely face.” But PEN America’s leadership have shied away from characterizing the article as a “report.” The <i>Times</i> noted that PEN America’s reports feature “extensive research and analysis,” before quoting Lopez saying “that sort of scope was never the intent here.” The authors of “A Silent Moratorium” were not PEN America program staff, with expertise in relevant fields, but two communications staffers and an outside consultant. Representatives of PAU told us that PEN America leadership explained to its members “that this publication is a blog which does not claim to make definitive conclusions.” This distinction is lost on most readers, said literary agent Meredith Kaffel Simonoff: “Despite its anecdotal reporting, ‘A Silent Moratorium’ seems to have been received and cited by many as a standard PEN report. Which means the stakes are even higher—particularly for a piece publishing into an already fractured environment—for it to have been subject to rigorous methodology.”</p>
<p>The authors of “A Silent Moratorium” were not PEN America program staff, with expertise in relevant fields, but two communications staffers and an outside consultant.</p>
<p>The lack of rigor is apparent in the number of false or misleading claims in the piece. It devotes a section to “A Chilling Effect on Israeli Writers,” citing one unnamed translator “who has been involved with trying to bring 20 to 30 Hebrew-language projects to international markets” and “did not know of a single project that sold.” The article acknowledges that Israeli nonfiction continues to be published, but claims that “fiction is another story,” suggesting that unusually few works of Israeli fiction in translation have been published since October 7th.</p>
<p>This paints a deceptive and oversimplified picture of a complex, tiny literary ecosystem and the pressures currently affecting it. First, at least 13 works of fiction written in Hebrew by Jewish Israelis—including novels by well-known writers like Yishai Sarid, Maya Arad, and Etgar Keret, and debut or early-career writers like Tehila Hakimi, Maya Kessler, Iddo Gefen, Yishay Ishi Ron, and Masha Zur-Glozman—were translated into English and published in the US in 2024, 2025, and 2026. The article’s authors qualify that “the chilling effect on the market for fiction titles might not be immediately obvious because only a handful of Israeli authors are published in the US each year, and contracts are typically signed years in advance and confidential.” By this logic, we wouldn’t know whether there is a “moratorium” for another few years. But even still, a number of works of fiction by Israeli writers have been announced for future publication, including <i>Fate </i>by Zeruya Shalev from Other Press and <i>House Arrest</i> by Noa Yedlin from New Vessel, both forthcoming later this year, as well as <i>Bandit </i>by Itamar Orlev from Deep Vellum in 2027, and Maya Arad’s <i>Our Lady of Kazan</i> from New Vessel, which has yet to announce a publication date.</p>
<p>Looking at longer-term trends offers a more complete and relevant picture of the challenges facing Hebrew literature in translation. Drawing on Yuval Amit’s bibliography of Hebrew literature in translation, compiled as an appendix to his 2007 Tel Aviv University dissertation, as well as our own survey, we found that while Hebrew fiction published in translation in America has steadily increased since 1948—from an average of over three titles per year in the 1970s, to about six in the 1990s, and about eleven in the 2010s—there has been plenty of variation within decades. (The bibliography we built is <a href="https://s3.us-east-1.amazonaws.com/jewish-currents/Hebrew_fiction_in_English_translation_US_1948-2025.pdf">here</a>.) In 2016, there were seventeen titles—the highest number we found—but there were only four titles in 2012 and six in 2021. Statistically, this makes it difficult to claim that six books in 2024 and five in 2025 represent anything patently abnormal.</p>

<p>That’s not to say that all has been business-as-usual for Hebrew literature in translation. Literary agent Jessica Kasmer-Jacobs explained that about a decade ago, as AI translation improved, American publishers became less willing to pay for translations. “Because the market is so cutthroat,” she said, “publishers are just not going to lift a finger for translated books if they don’t have to.” The Israeli government had offered some funding for literary translations, but more recently that’s “no longer the cultural ministry’s priority . . . because of who runs that place,” she said, referring to the right-wing minister of culture, Miki Zohar.</p>
<p>Jessica Cohen—the preeminent translator of Hebrew fiction into English, who was not contacted by the authors of the PEN America article—explained another facet of this challenge: Even if Israeli authors <i>can</i> get government grants for translation, some of them will not apply. Cohen told us about two recent cases in which Israeli authors asked her to translate their books, and have interest from American publishers, but neither the authors, nor Cohen, nor the publishers are willing to accept funds from the current Israeli government. Considering that most of the publishers Cohen works with “are cash-strapped indie presses that rely on grants, which are almost nonexistent in the US,” that leaves her without a way to get paid for her work, which means the projects won’t happen.</p>
<p>While we can’t say exactly how common this dynamic is, we also heard from two Israeli authors who told us they would not accept funds from the Israeli government now, and they would not want their American translators or publishers to, either. The Israeli translator and children’s book author Shoham Smith, who received the Bialik Prize in 2018, told us that there are Israeli government grants she will not apply for, because she does “not want to find [herself] forced to shake hands with criminals.” Such individual choices could have more than a marginal effect on annual tallies in an environment where only a handful of Hebrew titles in translation are published each year.</p>
<p>Individual choices by Israeli writers and translators not to accept grants from the Israeli government could have more than a marginal effect in an environment where only a handful of Hebrew titles in translation are published each year.</p>
<p>The fates of individual editors and small publishing houses can also have an outsize effect. Almost a third (53 out of 172) of the books on our bibliography of Hebrew-to-English translations published in the US between 2004 and 2020 came from just four publishers: Dalkey Archive, HarperCollins, Toby, and the Jewish press Schocken. These presses published only four such books total from 2021 through 2025, for reasons unrelated to October 7th. For example, Dalkey’s founder and publisher <a href="https://www.publishersweekly.com/pw/by-topic/industry-news/Obituary/article/85000-obituary-john-o-brien-founder-of-dalkey-archive-press.html">died</a> in late 2020, and Toby “<a href="https://www.haaretz.com/2010-05-07/ty-article/toby-press-cutbacks-cast-pall-over-local-english-language-book-scene/0000017f-f8c9-d044-adff-fbf907930000">scal[ed] down</a> its publication of new titles” back in 2010. Other new publishers have emerged to pursue related goals, like New Vessel and Deep Vellum (the latter of which acquired Dalkey Archive), but they are contending with the same rapidly changing conditions affecting most publishers, including increasing <a href="https://lunch.publishersmarketplace.com/2025/08/profits-fall-at-penguin-random-house-due-to-rising-costs/">costs</a> of <a href="https://kathleenschmidt.substack.com/p/is-the-publishing-industry-in-crisis">production</a> and the dramatic <a href="https://www.niemanlab.org/2026/06/book-reviews-are-an-endangered-species-so-this-independent-bookstore-decided-to-start-publishing-its-own/">reduction</a> of <a href="https://derekkrissoff.substack.com/p/the-state-of-book-publicity-now">traditional</a> book review venues, in addition to the specific challenges related to Hebrew literature in translation that Kasmer-Jacobs and Cohen described.</p>
<p>At least one effort is attempting to bypass the issues presented by Israeli government funding. Israeli writers Moshe Sakal and Dory Manor recently established a Hebrew publishing house, Altneuland, in Berlin, where they can publish, as the <i>Times of Israel</i> put it, “<a href="https://www.timesofisrael.com/new-hebrew-press-in-berlin-seeks-to-build-literary-home-outside-of-israel/">free from Israeli state funding</a>.” Cohen, who worked with Altneuland to secure publication of English translations of their books, says that her experiences suggest that such efforts can make a difference with American publishers. “I reached out to 15 editors, and not a single one of them expressed any reluctance to work with Israelis,” she said. “In fact, a few [of the rejections] went out of their way to say their response had nothing to do with the boycott, it was just a business model that wouldn’t work for them.”</p>

<p><strong>It would be difficult to argue</strong> that American Jewish writers in the US are having trouble publishing their books. Notable works of fiction published over the past few years that deal with Jewish characters and themes include Taffy Brodesser-Akner’s <i>Long Island Compromise</i> (2024), Allison Epstein’s <i>Fagin the Thief </i>(2025), and Gary Shteyngart’s <i>Vera, or Faith </i>(2025). Forthcoming novels from the most prestigious presses, like Maia Ipp’s <i>Sugar Truck</i>, Gemma Seiff’s <i>Dear Charlie</i>, and Joshua Cohen’s <i>Dead Herzls</i>, should keep the lover of American Jewish literature very busy.</p>
<p>Perhaps for this reason, a separate set of claims animate the section of “A Silent Moratorium” that concerns Jewish writers in the US. Here, the focus is primarily on harassment and prejudicial treatment. The article cites “a hotline for reporting ‘antisemitic literary-related incidents’” created by the Jewish Book Council that received “about 350 self-reports by July 2026.” Though we do not doubt that some of these reports are real and distressing, the Jewish Book Council declined to share these unpublished claims or any analysis of them, so we are left with the anecdotes provided in the article, which are often partial or too vague to qualify as factual.</p>
<p>To bolster claims of online harassment, the article details instances of “review bombing,” a phenomenon in which a large number of negative reviews or ratings are posted to a review site by an organized group or at the suggestion of influencers. “A Silent Moratorium” claims that “Zibby Owens and other Jewish writers have reported being ‘review bombed’ on Goodreads,” linking to an <a href="https://www.tabletmag.com/sections/arts-letters/articles/review-bombing-for-gaza">article</a> in the conservative magazine <i>Tablet </i>that offers no evidence related to Owens. The only instance of negative reviewing related to Owens’s books seems to be one she described in<a href="https://www.zibbyowens.com/p/an-antisemitic-troll-tanked-my-goodreads"> a blog post</a> in January 2024, in which her Goodreads ratings were “tanked” by a single one-star rating calling her a “Zionist-racist.” (Owens—whose father Stephen A. Schwarzman, co-founder and CEO of the private equity firm Blackstone, is a major Republican donor and close associate of Donald Trump—has also <a href="https://www.zibbyowens.com/p/review-bombing-situation">claimed</a>, more plausibly, that there was coordinated review bombing directed at a documentary film, <i>October 8</i>, on which she was an associate producer, and which a <i>Slate </i>reviewer characterized as “<a href="https://slate.com/culture/2025/03/october-8-film-israel-palestine-antisemitism-mahmoud-khalil.html">nakedly propagandistic</a>.”)</p>
<p>PEN America repeats novelist Lisa Barr’s claim, made in <a href="https://www.tabletmag.com/sections/arts-letters/articles/review-bombing-for-gaza"><i>Tablet</i></a> and elsewhere, that “her novel <i>Woman on Fire</i> received 450 one-star reviews as a result of an organized review bombing.” But the distribution of review scores on the book’s <a href="http://amazon.com">Amazon</a> and <a href="https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/58275730/reviews?reviewFilters=eyJyYXRpbmdNaW4iOjEsInJhdGluZ01heCI6MSwiYWZ0ZXIiOiJOVE1zTVRZMk5USTRNemd3T1RBNU13In0%3D">Goodreads pages</a> do not look like well-known cases of review-bombing: Only 1.5% of <i>Woman on Fire</i>’s roughly 38,000 Goodreads ratings and 1% of its 5,500-plus Amazon ratings are one-star ratings, and these came in steadily across several years, as opposed to in sudden batches. Many of the ratings were left by established users. The one-star reviews, in which users offer commentary along with their rating, appear legitimate, and many of these were posted before October 7th.</p>
<p>The only book cited in “A Silent Moratorium” that seems to have genuinely been the target of review bombing is <i>Once More with Chutzpah</i>, Haley Neil’s young adult novel about a youth trip to Israel. We found <a href="https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/56268860-once-more-with-chutzpah?from_search=true&amp;from_srp=true&amp;qid=RtMPVvqBWu&amp;rank=1#CommunityReviews">reviews</a> on Goodreads that criticize the book as “crazy zionist BS,” for example, from accounts with only a single review to their credit. But this took place well before October 7th—Neil’s experiences were <a href="https://www.kveller.com/this-moving-tale-about-an-israel-youth-trip-sparked-outrage-before-it-even-came-out/">reported</a> in <i>Kveller</i> in February 2022—so it is misleading to suggest, as the PEN article does, that this represents a post-October 7th phenomenon.</p>
<p>“A Silent Moratorium” also refers to “event disinvitations and cancellations,” though it cites only one example (“one of the few if not the only fully specific and therefore checkable case in the entire report,” as <i>New York Times</i> columnist and former PEN America vice president M. Gessen quipped in a critical <a href="https://www.facebook.com/story.php?story_fbid=10163671331454398&amp;id=748854397&amp;mibextid=wwXIfr&amp;rdid=MgmcGlSnV4WHi3Da">Facebook post</a>).<a href="https://www.facebook.com/story.php?story_fbid=10163671331454398&amp;id=748854397&amp;mibextid=wwXIfr&amp;rdid=MgmcGlSnV4WHi3Da"> “Amid warnings of protests and threats,” the report states, Etgar Keret and Ira Glass </a>“in agreement with their hosts” decided to cancel a live event in Australia. Gessen noted in their post that around the same time as this incident, Palestinian and pro-Palestinian artists’ appearances were canceled by organizers of high-profile literary and cultural events in Australia, whereas Keret and Glass made the decision to <i>withdraw themselves</i> due to organizers’ own lack of confidence in their ability to provide security. (Gessen further pointed out that Keret and Glass have been performing to “large and enthusiastic audiences” in American cities.) Since October 7th, there have been many <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2024/08/21/nyregion/powerhouse-books-andy-bachman-event-cancelled-zionist.html">widely</a> <a href="https://www.algemeiner.com/2026/08/03/nashville-bookstore-cancels-book-launch-of-jewish-author-behind-white-blonde-jew-after-anti-zionist-social-media-campaign/">reported</a> <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2025/09/18/nyregion/palestinian-author-jenan-matari-event-canceled.html">examples</a><a href="https://www.jta.org/2024/09/20/culture/albany-book-festival-cancels-panel-with-jewish-moderator-citing-impasse-over-her-zionism">of</a> <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2023/10/21/arts/92ny-viet-thanh-nguyen-israel.html">event</a> <a href="https://stanforddaily.com/2025/04/14/palestinian-poet-cancels-lecture/">cancellations</a>, both of Zionist Jews and Palestinians and their allies, some of them citing security concerns. PEN America failed to explore the practical and political considerations affecting these varied event cancellations in its article.</p>
<p>Perhaps the most serious allegation in this section of the article—and the most difficult to verify—is that “several Jewish writers have reported being dropped by their agents,” presumably because they identify as Jewish or because their projects deal with Jewish subject matter. Again, the claim is largely anonymized. However, the single writer named in the PEN piece and in a linked <i>Forward </i>article, Susan Blumberg-Kason, gives a far from conclusive account of her agent saga in an extensive <a href="https://leekofman.com.au/the-writer-laid-bare/on-identity-and-gatekeeping-a-guest-post-by-susan-blumberg-kason/">blog post</a>, also linked by PEN. In it, she makes clear that an agent first dropped her in 2021, and then, sometime in 2023 or 2024, her next agent “left her job and [she] was passed on to another agent.” That third agent told Blumberg-Kason she did not want to continue to represent her in the summer of 2025. At that time, Blumberg-Kason explains, she was pitching a trade biography of Gertrude Stein; she does not mention Francesca Wade’s <i>Gertrude Stein: An Afterlife</i>, which had just been published in the UK, and was <a href="https://www.theguardian.com/books/2025/may/26/gertrude-stein-an-afterlife-by-francesca-wade-how-a-literary-legend-was-made">already</a> <a href="https://www.lrb.co.uk/the-paper/v47/n17/adam-thirlwell/devotion-to-the-cut">being</a> <a href="https://www.nybooks.com/articles/2026/02/12/epic-ambitions-gertrude-stein-an-afterlife/">celebrated</a> as a landmark in literary biography.</p>
<p>As further evidence of the phenomenon of Jewish writers being dropped by their agents, the PEN article cites Deborah Harris’s claim that she “gets requests every week from Jewish writers in the United States who are seeking new representation because of perceived bias from their US agents.” We asked Harris whether she has signed any of these writers as her clients. She said she has not, and that “if anything had come in that I thought was really good, I probably would have taken it on.”</p>

<p><strong>In the wake of “A Silent Moratorium,”</strong> a writer named Meg Keene published an op-ed in JTA touting “alarming” trends in publishing. She had her own story of alleged literary discrimination: Having successfully published two nonfiction books about wedding planning, she began querying agents in 2024 about a novel she had written the previous year. As she <a href="https://thecjn.ca/opinion/from-girlboss-to-ghosted-meg-keene-on-the-quiet-muzzling-of-jewish-fiction-writers/">told</a> the <i>Canadian Jewish News</i>, she wrote the novel in seven days and “had it edited” within six weeks. An agent reportedly told her, “to have a chance [at publication], we would need to strip everything Jewish out of it”—a claim which also appears in the PEN article. Keene refused and signed with a different agent. The book did not sell.</p>
<p>Despite her inclusion in the PEN America piece, Keene has been critical of it, writing in the <a href="https://www.wsj.com/opinion/pen-america-equivocates-on-boycotting-israel-c31e6fcb"><i>Wall Street Journal</i></a>that “A Silent Moratorium” was “in many ways, a failure”; she faults its authors for not unambiguously adopting “antisemitism as the central explanation for the pattern its reporting reveals.” Keene has endeavored not just to clarify the PEN piece’s meaning, but to ground its anecdotal evidence in numbers. “Critics have noted that conversation about Jews being excluded from publishing hasn’t had much data,” Keene <a href="https://google.com/url?q=https://www.instagram.com/p/Da23CzIFJmY/?img_index%3D3&amp;sa=D&amp;source=docs&amp;ust=1787879086767427&amp;usg=AOvVaw0mdt06a8AW1b3hyYOnl17x">posted</a> on Instagram, offering her research as a corrective.</p>
<p>Keene collected “fiction deals for books by Jewish authors that publicly signaled Jewish or Israeli content,” searching the terms “Jews,” “Judaism,” “Jewish,” “Israel,” and “Hebrew” in <i>Publishers Marketplace</i> announcements. She found a 76% decline from 2023 to 2024, and a 47% decline from 2023 to 2025. She concludes that “publicly announced fiction deals foregrounding Jewish themes dropped sharply after Oct. 7, and the decline appears to be worsening.”</p>
<p>We spoke with Keene to learn more about her methodology, in an effort to replicate her findings. We already knew that she counted only large presses, even though so many important Jewish and Israeli books come from small and midsize publishers. Notably, we also learned that she only included those writers who she determined are “publicly Jewish,” which raised questions for us about Keene’s approach to making such determinations.</p>
<p>Once we <a href="https://s3.us-east-1.amazonaws.com/jewish-currents/Jewish_Fiction_Deals_AllPresses.pdf">expanded Keene’s inquiry</a>—declining to determine who qualifies as “publicly Jewish”; including small and midsize presses; and adding “Israeli,” and not just “Israel,” as a search term—the decline was still substantial, but significantly lower than Keene’s figures: We found a 41% decrease in deals for Jewish novels from 2023 to 2024, and a 22% decrease from 2023 to 2025.</p>
<p>If we look back further than just three years, though—as Keene told us she did not do—these dips seem even less statistically significant. Using our methodology, 2025 turns out to be the third-best year of the 21st century for book deals that “publicly signaled Jewish or Israeli content.”</p>

<p>Both methodologies exclude important works of Jewish or Israeli fiction published during the last few years because they do not include any of the relevant search terms in their <i>Publishers Marketplace</i> entries or were not announced there—including Ayelet Tsabari’s <i>Songs for the Brokenhearted </i>(2024) and even Joshua Cohen’s Pulitzer Prize-winning <i>The Netanyahus</i> (2021)—underscoring the fallibility of the data. When we compared the <i>Publishers Marketplace </i>data that Keene relies on to <a href="https://s3.us-east-1.amazonaws.com/jewish-currents/Jewish_Book_Council_Fiction_2026-09-14.pdf">listings of fiction titles</a> on the Jewish Book Council’s website in the same period, we found that the former accounts for fewer than one-fifth of the titles on the latter.</p>
<p><strong>Even before </strong>the publication of the PEN article, Keene <a href="https://fieldnotesforthebrave.substack.com/p/im-collaborating-with-the-adl">announced</a> that she would be “collaborating with the ADL” to create a new report on Jews—“particularly ‘public Jews,’ Zionists, and those writing on Jewish topics”—who have been “silenced and sidelined” by antisemitism in the publishing industry. (The ADL itself has <a href="https://jewishjournal.com/commentary/opinion/363502/the-wrong-way-to-count-antisemitic-incidents/">come under scrutiny</a> in recent years for its method of counting antisemitic incidents, which often relies on <a href="https://jewishcurrents.org/the-adls-antisemitism-findings-explained">shoddy data</a> and a politicized <a href="https://jewishcurrents.org/examining-the-adls-antisemitism-audit">conflation</a> of anti-Zionism and antisemitism.) The organization published its report, titled “Missing Chapter,” today, on October 1st. Though Keene confirms that she did serve as a consultant on it, the ADL report does not reference Keene’s data. “The ADL made their own independent decisions about the shape of the report that they wanted,” Keene told us. “The data was not excluded because of its quality.”</p>
<p>The ADL document includes previously reported incidents that went unmentioned in “A Silent Moratorium,” such as the <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2024/08/21/nyregion/powerhouse-books-andy-bachman-event-cancelled-zionist.html">cancellation</a> of a book promotion event for Joshua Leifer’s <i>Tablets Shattered </i>in August 2024 because of the participation of a liberal Zionist rabbi, and some amount of genuine, pre-publication <a href="https://forward.com/culture/576532/this-novel-is-a-glowing-portrayal-of-israels-early-days-now-its-being-review-bombed-on-goodreads/">review bombing</a> of Talia Carner’s novel <i>The Boy with the Star Tattoo</i> in January 2024.</p>
<p>The ADL report also clarifies the political motivations behind the narrative—promoted by Keene and fueled this summer by PEN America—about antisemitism in publishing. The ADL’s report features testimonies by Liza Libes, <a href="https://www.instagram.com/reel/DWee2bZAKwy/">an anti-woke crusader</a> who has recently written about how she came to <a href="https://www.pensandpoison.org/p/how-columbia-university-made-me-a">support the Republican Party</a>, and Alyssa Rosenheck, who recently gave an <a href="https://www.c-span.org/program/campaign-2026/author-alyssa-rosenheck-and-influencer-emily-austin-at-republican-jewish-coalition-summit/684692">impassioned address</a> to the Republican Jewish Coalition. It includes an extensive quotation from literary agent Lisa Leshne, who complains that “several editors declined even to read” a book proposal she was shopping, based “on what they assumed the author’s views would be,” characterizing this stance as an “unwillingness to engage with Jewish and Israeli perspectives.” In a <a href="https://leshneagency.com/news/">September 10th post</a> on her agency’s website, Leshe explains that the book in question was written by former Columbia assistant professor Shai Davidai, who was <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/08/11/nyregion/columbia-university-lawsuit-palestinians.html">named</a> by Palestinian American students in a suit brought against the university as the ringleader in a campaign of doxxing and harassment, and whose <a href="https://jewishcurrents.org/jewish-organizing-at-columbias-encampment">vitriolic</a> pro-Israel conduct led the university to <a href="https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2024/10/16/columbia-university-suspends-outspoken-pro-israel-professor-for-harassment">temporarily suspend</a> him. “Every publisher has its own personality and presumably a book by this guy would naturally not be a fit for many places,” said Schwartzberg, the Portfolio Books editor. “It has nothing to do with that individual being Jewish, it has to do with who they are.”</p>

<p><strong>As often as Keene </strong><a href="https://thecjn.ca/opinion/from-girlboss-to-ghosted-meg-keene-on-the-quiet-muzzling-of-jewish-fiction-writers/"><strong>retells</strong></a> <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ljOVzjCO_cw"><strong>the</strong></a> <a href="https://jewishinsider.com/2026/08/books-jews-israel-publishing-companies-authors-editors/"><strong>anecdote</strong></a> about being asked to scrub Jewish markers from her book—and as often as publications like <i>Jewish Insider</i> have rushed to conclude that the manuscript didn’t sell “because it was ‘too Jewish’”—even she is quick to offer a caveat to her suggestion of antisemitic exclusion: “There are countless reasons a book may not be published,” she wrote in JTA. “Taste is subjective. Editors carefully build their lists. Nobody is owed a book deal.” (We’ll add that had a novel written in a week by a first-time novelist sold, that itself would have been extraordinary.) Keene told us she feels “like there are ways that the story is being reported that’s more salacious than it needs to be.” In a follow-up email, she wrote, “I’m not claiming, and have never claimed, that the book didn’t sell because of antisemitism.”</p>
<p>This speaks to the difficulty, in general, of assessing the reasons a book does not get published. “There have always been and always will be people who are not able to get their books published,” quipped Schwartzberg. The cultural critic Maris Kreizman said, “There are people claiming antisemitism when they didn’t get a book deal, but maybe they’re just bad writers.”</p>
<p>Author and literary agent Rebecca Podos told us that in her experience, the backlash to DEI under the second Trump administration has made it “harder to sell marginalized stories right now,” but that this hasn’t noticeably affected her Jewish clients: “I have not experienced some silent-but-collective understanding that we’re not publishing Jewish stories right now.” Kreizman, who writes a biweekly column for <i>Literary Hub</i> and hosted a literary podcast, said she has mostly heard “the opposite” of what was reported by PEN America from publishing people: “‘We’re talking about Judaism a lot lately, so why don’t we do some books about that?’”</p>
<p>Many of the highest profile, most successful recent nonfiction books on Jewish subjects take up histories of leftist or anti-Zionist Jewish activism that would have been difficult to publish a decade or two ago, like Molly Crapapple’s improbable <a href="https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/646320/here-where-we-live-is-our-country-by-molly-crabapple/">bestseller about the Bund</a> and Moser’s <a href="https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/770236/anti-zionism-by-benjamin-moser/">history of anti-Zionism</a>. “A Silent Moratorium” quoted one anonymous writer who observed that increasingly, in publishing, there is a vague sense that “the good Jews are anti-Zionist, and the bad Jews are Zionist.” Ben Hyman, editorial director of Schocken Books, a venerable Jewish imprint within Penguin Random House, told the <a href="https://www.wsj.com/arts-culture/books/antisemitism-books-literary-publishing-world-high-line-78267985"><i>Wall Street Journal</i>:</a> “I don’t really buy the idea that only anti-Zionist books are getting attention. I’ve seen successful books on both sides of that divide.” A recent example, Dara Horn’s latest book, <i>The Final Solution to the Jewish Question</i>, which critiques anti-Zionism and argues against using the term “genocide” to describe Israel’s actions in Gaza, debuted at number six on <i>The</i> <i>New York Times</i> Bestseller List for hardcover nonfiction.</p>
<p>But Schwartzberg, who edits high-profile nonfiction books, told us that the PEN article is picking up on something real, even if it fails to adequately diagnose it. He explained that attention to current events and political trends are a normal facet of editorial decision-making. “There are considerations born out of the genocide in Gaza and the Israeli political apparatus. And those considerations would also be true if I got a proposal in tomorrow from someone in North Korea or a book about how glorious [King] Leopold was,” Schwartzberg said. Literary agent Mark Gottlieb, who represented several books in our <i>Publishers Marketplace</i> datasets, and who believes that “antisemitism is a problem in US publishing today,” nonetheless noted a parallel between “Russian publishers and agencies” after Russia’s invasion of Ukraine and “Israeli publishers” after the war on Gaza.</p>

<p>Until very recently, some authors told us, those winds blew the other way. Even though he had won the Pulitzer Prize in 2020, Moser says that he knew “there were only three or four editors I could’ve taken the [Jewish anti-Zionist history] book to. People were scared of this topic and they thought it wasn’t the right time.” In his research, he observed that in previous decades, there were few if any anti-Zionist Jewish authors published by major commercial presses in the US: “They were all published by [left-wing presses like] Haymarket or Verso.” Aziza, the Palestinian American author, told us that when her first book went out on submission in the summer of 2023, her agent expected many offers, but about 30 publishers passed, with some editors sending her “private notes, saying, ‘This is really beautiful, we just don’t know how to sell a book about Palestine.’” (The book, <i>The Hollow Half</i>, was published by Catapult in 2025 to <a href="https://books.catapult.co/books/the-hollow-half/">wide acclaim</a>.)</p>
<p>Critics of “A Silent Moratorium” have noted that by suggesting that not publishing work that explicitly supports Israel is a matter of antisemitism, PEN America conflates Jewishness with Zionism. As the Jewish novelist Temim Fruchter put it, “These institutions are feeding the narrative that if people are angry that Israel is exacting mass collective punishment, they must hate Jews and Jewish literature.”</p>
<p>To their credit, the authors of “A Silent Moratorium” evince some understanding of this problem. They acknowledge that “it is difficult to assess how much of what the writers . . . are experiencing stems from cultural boycotts and broader efforts to protest the war; how much from anti-Israel, anti-Zionist, or antisemitic sentiment; and how much reflects matters of business or taste, which are also shaped by geopolitics.” But the piece gives no further attention to factors other than antisemitism and boycott, and declares that “the chill is especially profound for Jewish authors who write about their identity or about Israel.”</p>
<p>As evidence of this statement, the article offers the experiences of children’s book author K. Marcus and the writer Susan Shapiro. From an examination of her public Facebook page, Shapiro does seem to have stopped posting the incendiary, highly partisan material she favored in the 2010s, such as a post calling a Jimmy Carter op-ed advocating for Palestinians “<a href="https://www.facebook.com/susanshapironet/posts/pfbid02bj1ZjZccsJaapsZrsVGpWmLtsowfjS1RwiMyGRPak2UdvGVcYxeLZctsg2anWGD2l?__cft__%5B0%5D=AZhL5k4-yKj2FlOuFzwGPQgJJ9sV_Pr2HFTbY9o4_tsUtVdxWigXFitUs-pd1qoH1RleRjy9Oc-cqezb4wsZ7U5PK9ZGonFo_ZdSWOha9vl3k8nPohnhRhQrIi3T0NaBQiRMyTTUNpgfm0YWI90oYmYGI8bx&amp;__tn__=%2CO%2CP-R">stupid</a>,” and a link to her 2019 <a href="https://www.wsj.com/articles/anti-semitism-at-nyu-11555873457">op-ed</a> in the <i>Wall Street Journal</i> about purported antisemitism at NYU, which the president of NYU said at the time was <a href="https://www.wsj.com/articles/nyu-is-committed-to-its-jewish-community-11556563244">characterized</a> by “falsehoods and half-truths.” For her part, Marcus shared a screenshot with us of what the PEN America article referred to as “comments accusing Jews of supporting genocide”: Two comments left by a single account on her Facebook ad for a children’s book constitute the entirety of the harassment. In Marcus’s recollection, she was able to hide those comments from view (though Facebook reportedly told her that the comments did not violate their community standards). She told us this was the only harassing or antagonistic comment she has ever received, as far as she can recall. When asked whether the comments had changed what she feels comfortable writing or posting on social media, she replied, “I don’t think this happening to me changed or modified how I react.”</p>
<p>The experiences cited in “A Silent Moratorium” like that of Marcus or Zibby Owens, whose account of review bombing rests on a single unpleasant reaction to her work, contrast starkly with the online harassment some writers we interviewed receive from right-wing Jewish sources. Crabapple, for example, said that since her book on the Jewish Labor Bund appeared, her “social media has been near unusable because of thousands upon thousands of comments by Zionists telling me that I’m a kapo, I’m a traitor, I’m a fake Jew, comments saying I should be raped and murdered, or gloating about the deaths of Bund members in the Holocaust.” Aziza told us that she has come to expect “threatening emails, threatening comments or DMs” virtually every time she publishes anything or appears on a panel. “I’ve been told I don’t exist, I’ve been told my whole family should die, more of them should die.”</p>
<p>Two comments left by a single account on Marcus’s Facebook ad for a children’s book constitute the entirety of the harassment.</p>
<p>The contrast becomes extreme when one acknowledges the experiences of Palestinian writers—much of it <a href="https://pen.org/free-expression-amidst-the-war-between-israel-and-hamas/">documented</a> by PEN America itself—who have been <a href="https://pen.org/report/all-that-is-lost/">killed, detained, and assaulted</a> on the ground in Israel/Palestine. Fruchter reflected on the the ways the article, in equating minor unpleasant incidents with the most extreme forms of suppression, undermines PEN’s purported values: “I’m not sure what the purpose of an organization like PEN America is if not to fiercely protect and amplify the voices and stories of those most marginalized, and living under the most threat.”</p>
<p><strong>In response to the panic</strong> <strong>around exclusion,</strong> Jewish financiers are investing in new Jewish presses to insulate Zionist writers from trends in the general market. Before July was out, JTA <a href="https://www.jta.org/2026/07/28/culture/feeling-excluded-in-the-literary-world-jews-are-launching-their-own-publishing-houses">reported</a> that Keene will edit a new eponymous imprint at Wicked Son—itself an imprint of conservative publisher Post Hill Press—where she will publish “four to six novels over the next year.” (Notably, these titles, published by a midsize press, would not count toward her own tally of Jewish books.)</p>
<p>Her superior at Wicked Son, co-publisher David S. Bernstein, told JTA that he would institute an ideological litmus test for Keene’s authors that would exclude, by <a href="https://forward.com/news/antisemitism-decoded/804502/american-jews-zionist-zionism-survey/">some measures</a>, 63% of American Jews: “The one thing that we insist that our authors believe is that they’re Zionists.” For her part, Keene told us, “I don’t think political purity tests belong in literature, in either direction,” and insisted she’s “not interested in asking prospective authors to declare whether they’re Zionists or anti-Zionists.”</p>
<p>Keene’s imprint is launching alongside other new ventures like Canary Publishing, led by the editor and author Howard Lovy—who has <a href="https://www.algemeiner.com/2025/12/22/does-the-term-antisemitism-properly-convey-peoples-hatred-of-israel-and-jews/">endorsed</a> the characterization of anti-Zionism as a “hate movement”—and High Line Publishing Studio, founded by literary agent Neil Blair. Blair, most famous for representing J.K. Rowling, has helped to organize <a href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2015/oct/22/israel-needs-cultural-bridges-not-boycotts-letter-from-jk-rowling-simon-schama-and-others">anti-boycott efforts</a>, and has already <a href="https://www.publishersweekly.com/pw/by-topic/industry-news/publisher-news/article/101223-agent-neil-blair-launches-high-line-publishing-studio.html">announced a book deal</a> with the anti-anti-Zionist activist Adam Louis-Klein.</p>
<p>These efforts come as Jewish authors are protesting the ways that Zionism has already become a litmus test in Jewish literary institutions. This spring, a group of Jewish writers, many of them winners of or finalists for the National Jewish Book Award, sent an <a href="https://lithub.com/an-open-letter-to-the-jewish-book-council-from-a-concerned-group-of-jewish-writers/">open letter</a> to the Jewish Book Council, arguing that the venerable organization had “narrow[ed] its vision to a Zionist approach to Jewish culture,” alienating or excluding them. Fruchter, who helped to organize that effort, told us that “instead of being responsive” to the reality of the genocide—“taking accountability and creating real space for vocal dissent”—Jewish literary institutions are “turn[ing] inward, digging their heels in.”</p>
<p>PEN, meanwhile, is struggling with the reputational damage wrought by “A Silent Moratorium.” PAU representatives told us that its publication has negatively affected the work of the organization: “Several events have been canceled, partnerships have been severed or rescinded indefinitely, authors and readers have canceled their memberships with the organization, authors are retracting their awards and grants submissions.” PEN declined to comment on whether they would correct or retract their findings based on our reporting, but did point to the partial correction they made regarding Deborah Harris.</p>
<p>Some people we spoke with expressed frustration with PEN’s inability to meet the moment. “We need PEN,” the literary agent Kaffel Simonoff told us, “But it was dispiriting to see this lack of clear methodology [in ‘A Silent Moratorium’], especially given the ways the organization has recently worked to rebuild trust.” For her part, Aziza told us that the dustup over the article affirms that liberal institutions like PEN America are not yet “places where I would look for integrity or leadership. I would love to have my mind changed.”</p>
