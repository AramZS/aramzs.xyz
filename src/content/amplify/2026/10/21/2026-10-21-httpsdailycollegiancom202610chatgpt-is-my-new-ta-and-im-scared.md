<p>Artificial intelligence (AI) is my favorite conversation starter. It’s a great way to rile people up and get a peek into their most fundamental beliefs. I’m a journalism major; conversation, discourse and communication are my bread and butter.</p>
<p>Recently, I had a chance to talk to one of my professors, Rodrigo Zamith. He teaches the University of Massachusetts’ AI and Journalism class. We talked about why he thinks the class is necessary in the first place, given how polarized the conversation around AI is.</p>
<p>“The only way we can really defend our positions is to better understand how the technology works and better understand its social implications,” Zamith said.</p>
<p>I agree. As much as I personally dislike AI, I still want to talk about it. I want to dissect what it is, how it works and, most importantly, why it’s dangerous. I think there are a lot of places in the classroom where AI should be a part of the conversation.</p>
<p>But I’ve also come to believe that there are just as many places where AI has been integrated into the education system that are unnecessary and harmful.</p>
<p>The other day, I was talking with a classmate about my double major in public policy. She lit up as she brought up one of her favorite public policy classes, describing how interesting and unique the professor’s teaching style is. She excitedly reported that the professor in question has recently integrated AI into his teaching by having students debate their policy papers with AI chatbots. I could tell by her enthusiasm that the class itself must’ve been captivating, but I couldn’t help but die a little inside.</p>
<p>It seems every educator on the planet has their own special, unique way of integrating AI into their teaching. I understand that AI has become so widespread that many fields have to adapt to using it. It only makes sense that it includes education too.</p>
<p>But does it, really? Do professors truly have to work AI into their teaching?</p>
<p>As small as all these little acts of AI intervention are, they all have one major commonality–replacing the human. With education, any minor act of replacement is an insult to the students because education depends so much on interpersonal relationships.</p>
<p>I want to know that my professor is actually sitting with the essays I write. I want them to engage with it, to tear me apart in the comments. I want to know they read my email and cared enough to dignify me with a response, even if it’s half-hearted and sent from their iPhone. I want to be able to offer a barely perceptible nose-exhale to a meme on their slideshows, knowing they at least suffered searching for the perfect one.</p>
<p>These small acts of intimacy are what breed connection between teacher and student. They are the reasons why I choose to raise my hand in class. They remind me that a real human being is actively trying to put information into my brain. They make me willing to receive it.</p>
<p>But it doesn’t just end with teachers getting softer on themselves and their own AI use. They’re also getting softer on students and their AI use, to the point where they encourage it. In my high school AP Psychology class, my teacher had us use AI to look at different perspectives on dream analysis and compare them. In one of my political science classes, we had to generate political cartoons. I assumed all the AI-embellished assignments would stop when I got to college.</p>
<p>But they haven’t.</p>
<p>I have friends complaining about weird AI assignments they have to complete for their classes. It’s disheartening. It feels as though everyone in charge of our education has already accepted that, at the pace we’re moving, we’re all going to have to use AI at some point in our careers. By tasking us with AI assignments, professors do us a “favor” by removing our agency in school, rather than in our future workplace.</p>
<p>People will turn to me and present every new AI tool and activity as a new opportunity. They pitch it as optimization, as learning workplace efficiency.</p>
<p>I think Zamith put my fears into words the best. “In education, when it becomes so much easier to offload a lot of the hard work of learning, what does that mean for the ability to actually learn about that thing?” he said.</p>
<p>I know I’ll be surrounded by AI when I leave college too. I’m not going to dance around that. That doesn’t mean I ever intend to use it myself. So, you don’t have to teach me how to do it in advance.</p>
<p>In fact, I don’t want to optimize my learning. I’m fine drawing my own crummy cartoons and embarrassing myself in my own essays. I want to do it myself, so I know how to do it, and I’ll never need anyone else to do it for me.</p>
<p>Is that not the ultimate core of education? Have we all fallen so in love with Silicon Valley’s post-work utopia that even our educators have abandoned the principle of letting us struggle through it ourselves?</p>
<p>To a degree, I’m catastrophizing. Most teachers don’t like AI. Most professors aren’t writing it into their class activities. But a growing number are. As a student, it scares me. It feels like they’re not just pulling away, but abandoning me.</p>
<p>Let’s talk about AI. Let’s debate it in every classroom possible. Let’s experiment with it and question it. But let’s not lose sight of the humanity in school for the sake of learning easier<em>. </em></p>
