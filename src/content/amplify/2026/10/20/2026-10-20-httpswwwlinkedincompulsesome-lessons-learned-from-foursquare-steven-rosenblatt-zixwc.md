<h3> Steven Rosenblatt </h3>
<p>Last week it was announced that Infillion acquired Foursquare. I knew Infillion, which started out as Gimbal, a small competitor on the business side to Foursquare in the early days, and was disappointed but not surprised to see this acquisition.</p>
<p>Let’s rewind: after I left Apple in 2012, I was asked by Ron Conway to meet  <a data-test-link="" data-tracking-control-name="article-ssr-frontend-pulse_little-mention" data-tracking-will-navigate="" href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/dpstyles?trk=article-ssr-frontend-pulse_little-mention"> Dennis Crowley </a> , the co-founder and CEO of Foursquare, and now somebody I consider a long time friend. At the time, I had just started to lay the foundation of building a new venture capital firm after spending 13 years building tech companies and seeing how much NYC lacked early stage investors who had built companies. I walked into the Foursquare office, was impressed with the energy, and Dennis and  Evan Cohen (the COO at the time) asked if I would advise. I agreed to do so for 3 months while I worked on building a new venture firm. The first day I was pulled into the exec meeting, and was amazed at the level of transparency, something that was quite refreshing after spending the last 2 years at Apple, an extremely secretive company. What did shock me was that Foursquare had just done a secondary at an $800M+ valuation despite the fact that the company had yet to figure out a business model and was losing $35M a year with no revenue.</p>

<p>So back to the last chapter of Foursquare and its sale to Infillion. As I was leaving Foursquare, I convinced Jeff and Rory to hire a banker, which turned out to be Raine Group, to lead the next round of funding, while I left in 2018 to start and build Oceans. They asked me to stay on as an advisor, which I did throughout 2018, and then I decided to fully move on because I did not want the distraction of Foursquare and any possible drama around it. When I left, my mental state was that Foursquare’s options and RSUs would be worth zero, but I was hoping that all of our hard work would be rewarded. Over the years, the RSUs expired, the options became worthless, and sadly I watched the company that I had spent 6 years building slowly implode. Now that the final chapter has been written, I want to share some lessons that I have shared with founders, not so quietly, over the last 8 years at Oceans.</p>
<p>We live in a world where the numbers have gotten so big, where the tech press and “X” celebrate the fundraising rounds, where secondaries are happening all of the time, and founders are putting out fundraising videos. However, for every one of those types of companies, there are hundreds of others that are struggling. </p>
<p>So here are a few things:</p>
<p>Fundraising - Every dollar you raise increases the pressure on your company to see explosive growth on your metrics. If you raise a lot of capital and you don’t hit your metrics, you will slowly start to lose control and the equity value in your company. I have witnessed many founders who had hundreds of millions on paper only to wind up with $0 to little liquidity in the end. VCs are investing in power law; they invest in many companies knowing that only a small few wind up returning a disproportionate amount of capital. Later-stage investors have many tools to ensure they receive capital back before common shareholders do. As a founder, it might seem cool to celebrate the big numbers (large amount of capital raised or the headline amount), but you need to either know that you are swinging for the fence with the chance it becomes a zero, or you need to think long and hard about the amount of capital you raise.</p>
<p>Talent - What attracted so many amazing people to Foursquare was the people. It was the talent that made Foursquare come alive and thrive much longer than it probably should have, given the macro challenges in the business. The amazing talent is what convinced me to put a hold on starting a venture capital firm and to join Foursquare in 2012. These were the best of the best, across all functions (Eng, Prod, Sales &amp; BD, Marketing, Biz Ops, etc.). The Foursquare mafia has done incredibly well, going on to help build some exceptional companies, founding new ones, and standing at the forefront of AI. I can only imagine how many of these people would have created a mini PayPal mafia if Foursquare had a successful exit that created real liquidity. They were truly the best of the best in NYC and the Bay Area, which gave Foursquare a fighting chance, but probably also some false hope, given the overall challenges of the business.</p>
<p>Macro - Foursquare helped pioneer the beginning of the SoLoMo (Social/Local/Mobile) revolution and one of the core promises of mobile, which was location. Unfortunately, there were just too many macro headwinds: so many of Foursquare’s consumer competitors shut down or sold for pennies on the dollar, and most of the business-to-business competitors around location did the same. There were no public companies, acquisitions were minimal, and it just became a challenging category. Some of it had to do with privacy, some of it had to do with larger incumbents that had the distribution (Facebook buying Instagram killed Foursquare’s consumer business), and some of it had to do with anti-trust. I was involved in several M&amp;A discussions, but they all failed — either because of Foursquare’s liquidation stack and need for a higher valuation, or because of the concern that any acquisition of Foursquare by a Mag 7 company would have been scrutinized during years of rigid anti-trust enforcement. Some of it was macro headwinds, some was structural and self-inflicted.</p>
<p>In articulating the final obituary, I would say that Foursquare was such an important part of NYC tech history. It helped attract talent to NYC, brought West Coast VCs to NYC (some of which ultimately opened offices here), and created an amazing community and network of talent who have been influential in the next wave of tech, including AI. For me, as much as I was ready to start a VC firm in 2012, after 6 years in the board room with such amazing people and all of the learnings on the roller coaster ride that was Foursquare, it inspired me even further to want to start the VC firm I co-founded,  <a data-test-link="" data-tracking-control-name="article-ssr-frontend-pulse_little-mention" data-tracking-will-navigate="" href="https://www.linkedin.com/company/oceansventures?trk=article-ssr-frontend-pulse_little-mention">Oceans</a> , and made me a better partner to all founders. Although it is extremely disappointing to see that the final chapter in the Foursquare journey was not the chapter we had imagined early on, I think most of us take comfort in knowing how important the experience was to our careers. I will continue to share so many of the stories and experiences with founders with the hopes of ensuring that the blood, sweat, and tears that it takes to build a company get rewarded over time. The 2012-2018 period was pivotal to the growth of NYC tech, and I’m grateful to all the people I worked with and who worked for me, and now to the founders I get to work with every day, who inspire me. Finally, to the Foursquare alumni, congrats on creating such a bright future, and to those like  <a data-test-link="" data-tracking-control-name="article-ssr-frontend-pulse_little-mention" data-tracking-will-navigate="" href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/naveen?trk=article-ssr-frontend-pulse_little-mention"> Naveen Selvadurai </a>  (Co-Founder of Foursquare and now Co-Founder of Iconic) and  <a data-test-link="" data-tracking-control-name="article-ssr-frontend-pulse_little-mention" data-tracking-will-navigate="" href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/daniel-salinas-95a75a123?trk=article-ssr-frontend-pulse_little-mention"> Daniel Salinas </a>  (Co-Founder of Filament) who I have had the opportunity to invest in at Oceans; I’m glad our friendship and journey have continued into this next chapter, and I hope to fund more Foursquare alum in the future.</p>
