<p>A surgical team, a spacecraft crew, and your senior leadership team are each held together by something surprisingly intimate: a shared sense of who’s responsible for remembering what. Even close couples run on the same system. One partner can scale back their memory in a particular domain where their spouse has expertise. Based on this insight, the late social psychologist <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Wegner">Dan Wegner</a> originated the concept of “transactive memory systems.” Groups of people build a shared understanding—a system for who knows what—that works because each person can rely on the other’s expertise to coordinate daily life.</p>
<p>A great team works in the same way. People know and trust who has which skills or knowledge, and they know whom to go to for what. A Formula 1 pit crew exemplifies this: Each team member plays a specific role (e.g., front right tire carrier; back left tire changer), there’s no need to re-check each team member’s work, and the coordinated dance happens in under two seconds. Team specialization, trust, and coordination are compressed into the span of a few deep breaths.</p>
<p>So what happens when a <a href="https://hbr.org/2026/05/research-why-you-shouldnt-treat-ai-agents-like-employees">new kind of colleague</a> joins a workplace team—the kind of colleague who looks as competent as other colleagues, is always available, and rarely pushes back? As psychology researchers and communication scholars, we wanted to know what was happening to relationships when this new technological “coworker” joined the mix. We surveyed 1,033 U.S. full-time desk workers on intact project teams across over 20 industries to find out.</p>
<p>Our data tells a conflicted story about how AI adoption is changing teams. In our study we found that the more workers reported using AI, or perceived that their teams used AI, the more they felt their teammates had specialized and unique knowledge. Thus, encouragingly, one of the key dimensions of a successful team has improved with the adoption of AI. And yet, respondents in our survey revealed a clear cost: The more they turned to AI instead of a teammate, the more their trust in each other and their ability to coordinate seemed to erode. As one IT consultant in our study put succinctly, “AI has made my work easier, so I can focus more on working by myself. However, it means I talk and work less with my teammates, which sometimes makes teamwork feel disconnected and affects building trust and understanding among us.”</p>
<p>Strong team coordination and trust depend on shared mental models of what information coworkers have, and who has it. We are learning that AI is changing this dynamic on teams in subtle but significant ways. Although it seems to boost individuals’ capability, it also seems to be chipping away at the shared mental models that allow them to work well as a unit. All of this is happening because of a specific way that AI is being taught, used, and incentivized right now—and it can be avoided based on <em>how</em> teams integrate AI.</p>
<h2>AI, Teams, and “Relationslipping”</h2>
<p>As organizations have rushed to adopt generative AI, they’ve overwhelmingly concentrated on how it’s adopted and used at the individual level<strong>, </strong>rather than the team level. Adoption has often been driven by pressure rather than strategy, and success has been incentivized and measured by how many individuals are using AI well, not whether the team is working well together. This has had a surprising effect: We’ve developed AI-literate individuals with greater capacity, but their ability to work as a team is deteriorating.</p>
<p>Last year, we documented one unfortunate outcome of this individually-focused approach: <a href="https://hbr.org/2025/09/ai-generated-workslop-is-destroying-productivity">workslop</a>. Employees readily <a href="https://hbr.org/2026/01/why-people-create-ai-workslop-and-how-to-stop-it">admit to producing</a> low-effort, low-quality AI-generated content, seemingly to signal their compliance. We found this practice undermines team dynamics and makes colleagues think less of each other. But workslop is still fundamentally a story about colleagues communicating with each other, poorly, with AI sitting in between them. Our more-recent data highlights a different problem. AI is no longer only sitting in between collegial communication; in a growing number of cases, it’s outright replacing communications and stunting the growth of team relationships as a result.</p>
<p>Displacement—going to AI instead of a human colleague—allows the technology to absorb the time and attention that would otherwise go toward building relationships in the workplace. At its most extreme, a worker would rather receive a worse answer from AI than a better one from a teammate. Imagine a conversation with a colleague, or a check-in with a manager swapped out with a quick answer from a frontier model.</p>
<p>Repeated over time or across teams, displacement produces what we call “relationslipping”— the pernicious weakening of a team’s working relationships as its members substitute AI for each other. Displacement turns coordination into duplicated effort and dead ends, and trust into second-guessing and re-checking. Our data reveal that teams where displacement is the norm experience more than a 20% drop in team coordination (22.3%) and team trust (21.9%).</p>
<p>It’s tempting to want to <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/07/25/opinion/data-centers-environment-ai.html">blame AI for everything</a>, including the strain on team trust and coordination. But our data suggest otherwise: <a href="https://hbr.org/2025/03/the-strategic-genius-of-taylor-swift">we’re the problem, it’s us</a>. What drives the slippage in coordination and trust is the decision to displace human interactions with AI. When people come to see AI as a more competent, trustworthy, and preferable source of input than a colleague, they replace certain human interactions that otherwise would have built or maintained trust and, as a result, the coordinated dance of high-functioning teams falls apart.</p>
<h2>The Damage to Team Coordination and Trust</h2>
<p>Relationslipping is the sum of two costs of displacement. The first is a cognitive tax: Teams lose track of who knows what. The second cost of displacing your teammates is socioemotional: an erosion of trust.</p>
<p>Let’s start with the first. In our survey, responses to almost every question measuring perceived expertise on the team rose alongside AI use. The one question that bucked the trend asked respondents how well they knew “who knows what” on their team. Think about AI’s role here: Sometimes AI helps us learn, and the resulting work is attained knowledge being applied; other times, it’s a facade—a copy-and-paste job. This makes identifying the source of someone’s expertise much harder.</p>
<p>In the same way workslop decoupled effort and apparent quality, AI use on teams separates two other things that used to go hand in hand: apparent expertise and awareness of whose knowledge produced it. One respondent who works in retail lamented how some coworkers were letting colleagues’ knowledge go untapped and deferring to the AI’s outputs instead, saying “Even if they know they are missing something, they are content with the AI output, and there are missed opportunities for connection or new, creative thoughts from interacting with their colleagues.” Yet these are the connections that enable teams to work together seamlessly and innovatively.</p>
<p>People’s ability to maintain a map of who to go to for what is an essential part of team coordination, and it isn’t keeping up with how AI is being adopted. As can be seen in the chart below, people that prefer input from AI instead of teammates rate their team’s ability to work together smoothly and effectively lower. We believe that the cognitive map required for coordination degrades in two ways. First, when team members skip conversations in favor of a quick answer from AI, they aren’t developing or updating their mental map of who knows what. And second, when their colleagues offload their work to AI, those colleagues’ thinking becomes harder to see, so the map loses accuracy. One respondent described this feeling adeptly: “I have absolutely no idea what [my colleagues] are thinking or doing because the generative AI does most of their work.”</p>

<p>On the second cost, loss of trust, we found that the more likely a person is to go to AI instead of a teammate, the lower they rate their team’s ability to trust each other’s expertise and knowledge. On a day-to-day basis, a team with worse trust struggles to accept each other’s suggestions, stops relying on each other’s knowledge, and wastes time by double checking each other’s work. Beyond the consequences for the team, people experience the impact displacement has on a very human level too: They feel isolated. One respondent expressed how hurt she was that her colleague now gets writing feedback from AI instead of her. She wrote, “It was a good way to connect with a colleague and chat more. I miss having those connections and I feel lonelier now.”</p>
<p>AI’s negative effect on team dynamics isn’t unprecedented. It echoes what researchers found when an earlier connective technology entered daily life, decades before AI existed. In the late 1990s, a research team led by social psychologist Bob Kraut ran the HomeNet field <a href="https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~kiesler/publications/1998pdfs/1998_Internet-Paradox.pdf">study</a>, which followed 73 households through their first 1–2 years of having internet access. Kraut and his team expected the internet to expand people’s social networks and curb loneliness. Paradoxically, they found the opposite: Households that used the internet more heavily became less socially involved and lonelier. Like a portent of what is now happening with AI, the tool built to accelerate society through the information superhighway displaced stronger in-person interactions that had been doing that job before.</p>
<h2>Why We Displace Our Teammates</h2>
<p>One in five desk workers acknowledged a behavioral inclination to turn to AI over a teammate. Why do they decide to displace a teammate with AI? Our research suggests at least three reasons: beliefs about AI, team climate, and the sycophantic design of AI. In each case, people are making reasonable judgments about the conditions they’re working in. At scale, the result is a familiar case of a <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collective_action_problem">collective action problem</a>: Everyone is acting sensibly on their own and the group is worse off for it.</p>
<p>Let’s look at beliefs. According to our survey, people who are optimistic about AI are nearly three times more likely to displace a teammate with it. People who report high trust in AI are twice as likely to displace. If you believe AI is capable and dependable, going to AI can seem rational, productive, and sensible in the moment.</p>
<p>Displacing with AI can also seem rational when asking a colleague feels risky. Just as the conditions of an organization <a href="https://hbr.org/2026/01/why-people-create-ai-workslop-and-how-to-stop-it">predict who sends workslop</a>—for instance, mandates to use AI and low psychological safety—displacement is a choice made under conditions where AI usage feels safe and sensible. People with low psychological safety on their team are more than twice as likely to displace a teammate with AI.</p>
<p>What looks like a technological foible on the surface or a lapse in human judgment, appears to be a response to organizational conditions. Notably, this is not a demographic pattern: Displacement shows up equally as often across age, seniority, industry, team size, and project duration.</p>
<p>An organizational culture that rewards individual productivity over team-level efficiency can easily give rise to situations where displacement seems rational rather than careless and where asking a colleague feels like a personal cost rather than a relational bid. One respondent, a regulatory manager in pharmaceuticals, described how they felt that they’d be judged by team members for asking questions. “I use AI when I do not want to be thought of as an idiot or unintelligent. Rather than risk being judged by asking a question that they think I should already know the answer to, I use AI in these situations.” Others just don’t want to bother coworkers with what they fear to be annoying or trivial questions—a more pro-social concern. One respondent wrote, “[I]f I’m struggling and want to get an idea, I could just ask AI rather than bothering a teammate.”</p>
<p>Both instincts rest on the same miscalculation: <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34618536/">People consistently underestimate</a> how enjoyable a human conversation turns out to be. Whether the motivation is fear of judgment or a desire not to impose, avoiding a colleague is hardly a new workplace behavior. AI now provides an alluring alternative that forgoes the social and emotional costs of getting support.</p>
<p>All of this plays out alongside a third factor rooted in how the technology is built: AI is agreeable. It affirms and validates, which can make human interactions, with their pushback and friction, feel harder by comparison. A paper in <a href="https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aec8352?referrer=https%3A%2F%2Fwp.hbr.org%2F">Science</a> earlier this year found that the 11 leading models affirmed users’ actions 49% more often than humans did, even when users described deception or harmful behaviors. Then, in <a href="https://arxiv.org/html/2510.01395v1#S1">two experiments</a> with more than 1,600 participants, the same researchers found that even a single interaction with a sycophantic AI left people more convinced they were right and less willing to take responsibility or repair a conflict with another person. Participants also trusted the sycophantic AI more and said they were more likely to return to it. The validation that draws people back to AI also leaves them less willing to do the hard work that conversations with other people can require.</p>
<p>If frictionless AI can pull people away from friends and family, it’s not hard to see how it edges out a colleague. Part of what makes this a slippery slope is that using AI can be convenient, powerful, and effective. People are busy and unpredictable. Colleagues demand patience and care.</p>
<p>Both academic research as well as popular press have covered myriad dysfunctional, yet explainable, anti-social dynamics between people and AI programs. From <a href="https://www.apa.org/monitor/2025/10/technology-youth-friendships">AI friends</a> to <a href="https://www.newsweek.com/ai-girlfriends-are-already-making-the-dating-scene-harder-therapists-warn-12081342">AI lovers</a>, AI relationships have piqued interest around the world. That a nontrivial proportion of workers might also be susceptible to relationslipping in the workplace is not surprising. Displacement is an understandable outcome of creating frictionless and increasingly satisfying online experiences as an alternative to real-world relationships.</p>
<h2>The Psychological and Organizational Costs of Feeling Displaced</h2>
<p>Feeling displaced by AI can deal a heavy emotional blow to people. “I feel my skills are less needed by my teammates as AI helps them,” one manager described. Another told us that the decline of human contact at work left them with “feelings of isolation, reduced trust, and a sense of being replaceable.” Nearly half (49%) of the workers we surveyed reported that their teammates had a tendency to displace them with AI at least some of the time.</p>
<p>This experience has broad consequences. People who feel displaced are nearly half as likely to feel like they matter to their colleagues and are similarly less likely to feel like their unique skills and talents are valued by their teammates. It should be no surprise, then, that workers who feel displaced are also less likely to say they want to work with the team again in the future compared to those who do not feel displaced. This experience culminates in an increased likelihood of looking for a different job. Despite the individual productivity and knowledge gains that AI can surface, at the end of the day, people are more than machines, and seek out a sense of belonging, purpose, and connection at work.</p>
<p>Although we have yet to measure the true causal impact of displacement on employee turnover, we have identified a first look at the impact of displacement on organizations. Consider a large U.S. company with 10,000 employees rolling out AI. If <a href="https://www.gallup.com/workplace/712736/organizational-adoption-jumps-six-points.aspx">52% of an organization’s workforce uses AI at work</a>, and, as in our survey, just over three-quarters of them experience displacement, that means nearly 4,000 people would be affected. Displaced employees in our data reported higher job search activity, which <a href="https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/014920630002600305">research links to quitting</a>. Our analysis suggests roughly 6 percentage points of extra turnover, or 243 departures beyond the company’s baseline attrition. <a href="https://www.shrm.org/executive-network/insights/myth-replaceability-preparing-loss-key-employees">SHRM</a> estimates that replacing an employee costs somewhere between 50% to 200% of an employee’s salary. Assuming an average salary of $99,000 and a midpoint cost of labor replacement, this increase in turnover could cost the organization more than $25 million related to the deterioration of team cohesion that comes with relationslipping.</p>
<p>Organizations rolling out AI should consider the risk of displacement and subsequent turnover, as it could materially impact financial outcomes as well, potentially offsetting gains from AI enablement.</p>
<h2>AI Is Not Inherently Bad for Teams</h2>
<p>Given that AI can <a href="https://mitsloan.mit.edu/sites/default/files/2023-10/SSRN-id4573321.pdf">amplify individual productivity</a>, it’s reasonable to expect that people may take pride in their teams’ working relationships with AI. For example, it can free up resources, lead to <a href="https://hbr.org/2024/11/set-your-team-up-to-collaborate-with-ai-successfully">upskilling and reskilling</a>, and break constrained groupthink. A 38-year-old sales manager described their experience with their team’s AI use in positive terms: “My coworker went to AI instead of me when they needed a document right away and I was too busy. I was happy and relieved they did not have to rely on me.” In instances like these, team efficiency, productivity, and positive feelings might increase, and displacement can be liberating. These positive experiences encourage us to continue searching for the factors that determine whether displacement is experienced positively or negatively, and the long-term effects on teams.</p>
<p>How can teams continue on this path of technological exploration, while maintaining the strong connective tissue required for teamwork? This question may be even more important for <a href="https://hbr.org/2015/06/75-of-cross-functional-teams-are-dysfunctional">cross- functional teams</a> whose connections may be more vulnerable than single-function teams. Allowing slippage in those connections could lead to re-siloing within an enterprise. Here are a few recommendations based on our data.</p>
<h3>Increase asynchronous communication.</h3>
<p>Our data suggest that how often a team communicates with each other can buffer against the negative effect of displacement on team coordination. Even among those who displace, we see a 28% increase in coordination and 19% increase in team trust when employees are in daily contact with their teams. This is especially true for those that engage in regular asynchronous communication (e.g., Slack, email, Teams messages) which readily allows for staying in the loop with teammates.</p>
<p>Maintaining regular contact within a team may act as an antidote for relationslipping, where a person can turn to AI over a teammate and still maintain visibility with their teammates, build connection, and exchange information and expertise.</p>
<h3>Build psychological safety.</h3>
<p>This is the team condition that makes asking a colleague feel as safe as asking a model. Psychological safety has long been recognized as critical to <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2016/02/28/magazine/what-google-learned-from-its-quest-to-build-the-perfect-team.html">effective teamwork</a>. But we believe it matters even more now. Whereas previously the alternative to asking a colleague was staying stuck or looking uninformed in front of them, today the alternative is a model that doesn’t judge you, is always accessible, and increasingly looks as competent as a colleague. Psychological safety enables the precise question-asking, mistake-making, and experimentation that AI usage demands.</p>
<h3>Rethink teamwork with new team norms and aligned incentives.</h3>
<p>Teams forging ahead in this volatile and uncertain period of innovation will benefit from new expectations and norms about transparency, sharing how and when AI is being used by the team. Coordination and trust depend on strong mental models of what coworkers know, understand, and prefer. As such, leaders should design AI deployments that allow team members insight into each other’s practices with AI, <a href="https://hbr.org/2026/04/why-companies-that-choose-ai-augmentation-over-automation-may-win-in-the-long-run">augmenting</a> the work of employees as opposed to automating it. Although augmentation may show fewer short-term gains, we hypothesize that this approach will yield the strongest long-term benefits, as innovative AI practices diffuse across teams and the organization.</p>
<p>In addition, incentives currently encourage individual productivity with AI over team-level collective wins. Employees are tasked with getting their work done with AI, and the incentive structure gives them little reason to consider how their work impacts the team. Leaders of tomorrow need to challenge the status quo to re-architect incentives that encourage AI-enabled teams rather than AI-literate individuals.</p>
<h3>Use AI to prepare for conversations instead of replacing them.</h3>
<p>AI can be helpful in overcoming some of the anxieties people have toward the complicated relationships they have with other humans at work. By helping people practice and prepare for conversations they’re worried or anxious about, AI can center human relationships instead of casting them to the side. In earlier research, we have found that a simple five-minute AI role-play chat for a difficult conversation yields specific psychological benefits including anxiety reduction, confidence, and preparedness. These examples illustrate how the consequences of AI in the workplace depend highly on the individual and their environment.</p>
<h3>. . .</h3>
<p>One of our biggest practical insights from the workslop research is the importance of using AI with a relational lens: to think about who is on the other end to help keep your own judgment visible in the output and add the context AI can’t infer instead of shifting that burden onto the recipient. The same lesson reemerges here too, although in this case, a relational lens acts as a reminder to have those conversations with your colleagues, remembering not to displace activities entirely. The conversation might even include sharing how you’re using AI in your work, what’s worked well and what hasn’t. Of course, people need to feel safe to disclose their AI use in order to share said thought process with teammates. The more a team chooses conversation over displacement, the more it builds the trust and coordination that make the next disclosure feel safe too. This creates a virtuous cycle of psychological safety instead of the vicious cycle that results in relationslipping.</p>
<p>When Bob Kraut and his colleagues revisited their 1998 paradoxical findings about displaced relationships due to the internet <a href="https://spssi.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/1540-4560.00248">three years later</a>, the negative effects had faded. Heavy internet users were seeing net positive outcomes. The quality of relationships people used the technology to build, and the relationships around them, was more important than the technology per se.</p>
<p>It’s worth thinking through what changed in the years between the researchers’ studies. While technical advances have undoubtedly been made, the more important shift was in human behavior. People learned to use the same tool to strengthen the relationships they already had, rather than substitute them.</p>
<p>The same may be true in the workplace with AI. AI isn’t causing relationslipping, but it’s changing the way we relate to one another in the workplace.</p>
<p>Frontier AI models are improving their accuracy and speed at rapid rates. But more important is our adaptation—our decision as to whether we use AI to enhance and extend the relational systems holding a team together, and ultimately support the connective tissue of an organization, or something that breaks them down. A more capable model with a more accurate world view or relational intelligence won’t solve relationslipping. Human decisions to deepen relationships and connect our augmented capabilities with the people surrounding us everyday will.</p>
