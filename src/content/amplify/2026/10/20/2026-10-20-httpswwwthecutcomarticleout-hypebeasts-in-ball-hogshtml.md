<p>Menelik Demissie, better known as Beenslackin, is a 27-year-old downtown street-style <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2023/10/16/style/visual-profile-beenslackin.html">influencer</a> who is known for his porcupinelike facial piercings and outlandish designer outfits. But even he let out a weary sigh when I brought up “ball hog.” “I’ve been in the fashion world for years now, and this type of stuff definitely makes me feel like I’m a dinosaur,” he says.</p>
<p>“Ball hog” is essentially the deep-fried zoomer equivalent of the millennial hypebeast aesthetic. The hashtag has thousands of likes on TikTok, where posters share normal, casual outfits that feature only rare designer garments. At first glance, they look like typical students raised on Supreme and Playboi Carti. But then they’ll run down their credits, which might include Hedi Slimane–era Saint Laurent sweatshirts and Nicolas Ghesquière–era Balenciaga sneakers.</p>
<p>The look can be linked to the broader “archive” craze that is playing out on social media, with creators flexing their Grailed hauls of Japanese brands. All of it is going down in an online universe of attention-seeking content that seems almost entirely separate from the mainstream fashion conversation. While Paris is gushing about the latest Michael Rider Celine collection and raving about all the butts on the Tom Ford runway, some TikTokers are churning out hours of videos aura-farming in their archive grails.</p>
<p>According to Demissie, teens with bussin’ haircuts and access to their parents’ credit cards have moved away from mainstream luxury in recent years. “Since Demna left Balenciaga, nobody is making things that are relatable to the youth,” he says. “Because of that, people are just going to go backwards, and with that there’s just endless references and characters and archetypes that you can find. And the youth are heavily into <em>Oh, we’re niche</em>.”</p>
<p>A decade ago, when A$AP Rocky was name-checking Hedi Slimane and Ann Demeulemeester in his lyrics, fashion was much more exclusive. If you wanted to figure out the provenance of a Rick Owens jacket, you had to dig into obscure online forums. “You used to have to work to discover what these pieces were,” says Mark Boutilier, who, at 28, counts as an elder statesman of menswear TikTok. “You would start with Supreme and then work your way up. Whereas now you can be scrolling and discover everything in a matter of a few days. You can see archive Bradygoo fashion and then immediately get something on a <a href="https://www.wired.com/story/influencers-paid-promote-designer-knockoffs-from-china/">rep site</a> or on Grailed.” (Rep sites sell reproductions of designer goods.)</p>

<p>This is where I must explain Bradygoo, who is credited with popularizing the “ball hog” look on social media, where he shows off his fits on Instagram and critiques his followers’ looks on Twitch. He is one of the original members of the Ball Hog collective, a group of rappers, Twitch streamers, and content creators, and his name is Brady Goodwin. He’s a 23-year-old who lives in South Carolina.</p>
<p>“We never claimed to be the best dressed. People kind of put that label on us,” Goodwin tells me. The Ball Hog crew — which includes the streamer BruceDropEmOff and rapper Protect Ricky — came together on Discord a few years ago through a mutual interest in clothes and the basketball video-game series <em>NBA 2K</em> (hence the “ball hog” moniker). “When they knew what Undercover and Number (N)ine was, I was like, ‘We could be friends,’” he says.</p>

<p>Goodwin explains the aesthetic isn’t so deep to decipher. “I just like regular clothes, but they’re more expensive than what people might think. If I walk around, I want to look like a normal person. But to someone who understands it, they’ll be like <em>Damn, this outfit is like ten bands</em>.” By his count, there must be thousands of TikToks about Bradygoo fits, though he doesn’t quite know how the look spread. Or anyway, he can’t take full credit. “In April, there was one kid who made a video and he was like, ‘This is a Bradygoo fit.’ And then another kid made a video, because when someone gets views on TikTok, everyone wants to do it,” he says.</p>
<p>That doesn’t mean “ball hog” will take off in the real world as a naturally occurring trend. On fashion TikTok, style trends function like memes, where a buzzword hits the algorithm (remember “coastal grandma”?) and creators pile on in a never-ending quest for viral glory. Like any meme, “ball hog” has already become an inside joke you can share with the rest of the internet. Even creators who are serious about collecting archival fashion have leaned into the meme economy.</p>
<p>During New York Fashion Week, Boutilier assembled a crew to make a “ball hog vs archive” fit-battle <a href="https://www.instagram.com/reel/DdPa2QfPPaC/?hl=en">video</a>, sorting his friends into competing categories. “There’s been so much content in the last five years, where unless you’re making it a bit more outrageous it’s hard to cut through everything,” he said. When I ask how he decided who was ball hog and who was archive, he laughed. “I just picked them at random,” he said.</p>
