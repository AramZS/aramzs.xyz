title: A Haunting Masterwork From a Writer Who Is Reinventing Fantasy

<p><strong>THE ROUSE</strong>,<strong> </strong>by China Miéville</p><hr/><p>In the early 21st century, the English writer China Miéville began reinventing novels of the uncanny — and indeed, the fantasy genre itself — with his lauded New Crubozon trilogy and stand-alone books like “The City and the City” (2009). With his literalized abstractions, mirrored worlds and dramatization of impossible conceits, Miéville’s work often felt bold and experimental, a daring plunge into a shock of cold water.</p><p>Later books like “This Census-Taker” (2016) seemed sparse by contrast but also signaled that Miéville was moving toward something new. His first solo novel in 10 years, “The Rouse,” in which a secret British military unit attempts to weaponize the dead, is that something, a maturation of idea and form.</p><p>The opening section, almost a self-contained novella, chronicles the lives of a single mother named Maur and her son, Ewan (called Yew, after the trees he loves), who is obsessed with a strange house down the street. He joins the Royal Air Force in the 1990s, serves in the Balkans and perishes under mysterious circumstances. I can think of fewer more indelible portraits of a mother-son relationship in literature; it is a stunning display of interiority and brilliant writing. “She heard herself, that little sound she made,” Miéville writes. “Trapped in her body by the new emptiness of his.”</p><p>Before his death, Ewan became entangled in a mystery concerning a soldier named Steven Allardyce, the product of a hard childhood that he carries forward with violence of his own. After several false starts, Allardyce is recruited to R Unit, part of a clandestine Cold War-era agency seeking to raise and control the dead for use against enemies abroad. As Maur, at the turn of the millennium, embarks on a quest to understand Ewan’s death, Miéville weaves in Allardyce’s story, which spans several decades of British colonial history in Malaysia, Oman, Kenya and beyond. It is in Malaysia that Allardyce picks up a “watcher,” or shadow, one he may never quite shake.</p><p>His introduction to the spectral phenomena that will occupy much of his career occurs at the crash site of a Venom fighter jet — which was downed, he learns, for “no earthly reason.” To Allardyce’s surprise, an officer assigned to document the site includes a ghostly figure in his illustration of the wreckage, and debris collected from the plane will come to mean far more to them than just forensic evidence.</p><p>In the unit, run by a laconic woman named Croome, Allardyce trains on the uses of apports, objects that can help control or influence the wakened dead, or the Roused. Meanwhile, the Soviet Union and the United States have launched their own ghost-control operations, even as neither party nor their proxies have a clear view of what they are attempting to harness or what lies behind it.</p><p>Allardyce learns his haint craft at a secret base with a creepy windmill called the Black Beacon at its center, which houses texts both mundane and occult. Throughout the novel, dozens of dead literary souls peer out at the writer, with references to writers like M.R. James, William Hope Hodgson and Amos Tutuola. The physicality of text, its affect and effect on the world, becomes literal and deadly in ways that make the author’s prior forays into this area seem like child’s play.</p><p>If “The Rouse” sometimes reads like John le Carré’s “A Perfect Spy” by way of J.G. Ballard and Max Lury, Miéville transcends those comparisons with unparalleled lunges of thought about the spirit world that leave one breathless with their audacity. Literature itself becomes weaponized, part of the summoning of the dead; Croome notes that ghost stories make the Roused dazed and confused (mellow, high), while certain types of nonfiction draw their close attention. At one point, surrounded by arcane machines, she reads to the Roused, whose reactions manifest as patterns of light and dark. In another section, the process is likened to singing lullabies to an oscillating sine wave.</p><p>There is an emotional cadence, and even a rough joy, that enlivens what one might label exposition or theory — a searing incandescence illuminating the weightier material, like a pulsing line of red on metal being worked in a foundry. “Killing is ontologically simpler,” Croome says at one point. “You can’t kill something that’s not alive.” The academic language in the novel becomes urgent. Miéville is able to flex certain muscles — perilous for most fiction writers — in ways that support the dramatic tension rather than drain it. (The author also doesn’t neglect those small details that make a novel come alive: A swear jar for anyone in the unit who says “spirit” or “ghost” is a particularly nice touch.)</p><p>Curiously, though, when Allardyce’s story careens into Maur’s, the present feels less influenced by the past than it should. Allardyce, as an aging, discombobulated babbler stuck in Maur’s plotline, is not so much an essential source of urgency as he is a panicked conspiracy theorist who has had no one to talk to for a long time. He’s not substantiated by Maur but wisped by her, short-handed by dialogue that robs us of a fuller exterior view of the man.</p><p>One wonders why the old soldier suddenly uses so many words to say nothing. It is a disconcerting, watering-down effect but might be charitably interpreted as the confounding influence of the Roused. Thankfully, too, this jarring lurch and bobble fades before a renewed conjuring of Maur’s true voice.</p><p>While Miéville launches himself with enthusiasm into inhabiting Allardyce — a tour de force of sustained characterization — both reader and author understand him to be a profoundly flawed person. Shaped by his environment and by British colonialism, he becomes complicit in covert atrocity even as he tries at times to stand apart.</p><p>The merry camaraderie of his unit, the stiff upper lip of various military corps, whether they’re fixated on human uprisings or on the Roused, are touches of misdirection. Banter, innocent or dehumanizing, is just a guise. It reflects little of the substance of actions taken, has no bearing on what lies behind all the uncanny in this novel, which we eventually understand to be in part the weight of human history. As the band Giant Sand once put it, “They say this place here is haunted, yeah, but only by a ghost.”</p><figure><img alt='The book cover of "The Rouse," by China Miéville.' src="https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/09/16/books/16TBR-Mieville-cover/16TBR-Mieville-cover-articleLarge.jpg"/></figure><p>As Maur unwillingly becomes entangled in Allardyce’s desperate plans and dire warnings of disaster, Miéville manages to deepen our understanding of both characters. One of the most significant ways is through the depiction of the soldier’s relationship with his wife, Maisie. When she begins to write fiction and poems, Allardyce’s distrust at first suggests jealousy but actually stems from his professional preoccupation with the effects of text on the Roused. His suspicion, then, is in part with how the world apprehends narrative beyond a writer’s control. He has also been traumatized by his experiences, and this disconnects him from the ability to feel, or be in a healthy relationship. (Where this leads is inevitably tragic.)</p><p>As the novel then spirals toward demise, R Unit becomes compromised, Allardyce resigns and the characters find themselves trapped in something so sinister yet unknowable that the novel becomes hard to define, resistant to words like tragedy, serendipity, the occult. Miéville attempts to upend the foundations of thinking, devising a panopticon that spies back on itself. The choices Allardyce has made transform the narrative in gut-wrenching moments. Maur’s quest, too, for meaning over her son’s passing, attains a terrible and blinding light.</p><p>I’ve rarely read scenes so terrifying, achieved with a kind of stutter-step language, words that intensify and recede with such incantatory power. These passages seem unparalleled in literature and somehow bypass the brain to jolt the nervous system directly. Over its 1,200 pages, the novel becomes a haunting of a haunting, a terror echoed and heightened over time. This terror grows familiar but not stale; the reader is allowed to live within it almost calmly and thus catch glimpses of other forces in the darkness.</p><p>What Miéville has crafted in “The Rouse” is an intimate epic, a controlled sprawl of history and emotional resonance. I felt it in my bones, the undeniable power of a jagged masterpiece.</p><hr/><p><strong>THE ROUSE</strong> | By China Miéville | Del Rey | 1,246 pp. | $40</p>
