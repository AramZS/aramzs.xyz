title: The Latest Corporate Climate Ruse
---
<p>It’s no secret that trees are good for the climate. Not only do they store heat-trapping carbon in their roots, trunks and branches, but they also remove about a quarter of the carbon we pump into the atmosphere when we burn fossil fuels.</p><p>But proposed revisions to the <a href="https://ghgprotocol.org/">leading global standard</a> for measuring and managing planet-warming emissions would make cutting down trees look good for the climate, as strange as it sounds. The <a href="https://www.source-material.org/">changes</a> to the widely used Greenhouse Gas Protocol would create a warped reality where companies could claim credit for cutting emissions when they harvest trees for paper, burn trees for energy or convert trees into other wood products. And the more wood they use, the more emissions they could claim to reduce.</p><p>There’s a simple explanation for these perverse results: The process to rewrite the rules has been dominated by wood products companies that would benefit, including the forestry giant Weyerhaeuser, the furniture giant IKEA and other foreign and domestic conglomerates. One of the few independent experts overseeing the process <a href="https://heatmap.news/carbon-removal/greenhouse-gas-protocol-protest">resigned in June</a> to protest the excessive industry influence. Then last month the top land-use expert at the World Resources Institute, the environmental think tank whose management of the protocol has helped give it worldwide credibility, <a href="https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-13/dispute-over-counting-forest-carbon-roils-global-standard-setter">quit his job</a> after his repeated warnings about greenwashing were ignored.</p><p>A rulebook you’ve never heard of may not seem like an urgent climate priority, when record heat waves are broiling the earth, a glacier collapse most likely just killed thousands of people in Nepal and the Trump administration is avidly promoting fossil fuel energy. But the protocol was created to promote climate action through science-based accounting standards.</p><p>Almost all major corporations that count their emissions use the protocol to measure their carbon footprints, while countries like Australia and states such as California use it to regulate carbon emissions. Revising it to make deforestation look climate-friendly would encourage a lot more deforestation, make the companies responsible look like climate heroes and mislead consumers about the impact of forest products.</p><p>This is why corporate influence over its revisions is so dangerous; just as companies would report much less taxable income if they could define what the I.R.S. counts as taxable income, companies will do much less to reduce their emissions if they can define what the protocol counts as emissions. In fact, forestry and wood products companies already tested how the proposed changes would work, and what do you know? Eighteen out of 19 companies or “company units” reported in a pilot study that their current operations would be considered harmless to the climate. All but one of them concluded that according to the proposed changes, they were actually helping the climate by doing the things they’ve always done.</p><p>In reality, the status quo is a climate disaster. Deforestation <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/04/29/climate/wri-report-forest-loss.html">slowed down</a> in 2025, but it still generates 10 percent of global emissions, and as the United Nations <a href="https://www.carbonbrief.org/un-report-five-charts-showing-how-global-deforestation-is-declining">has documented</a>, the world is on track to deforest nearly an Argentina-worth of land by 2050.</p><p>Emissions from land use are more complex than emissions from fossil fuels, which is why the groups managing the protocol, the World Resources Institute and the World Business Council for Sustainable Development, are trying to modernize its land-use guidelines. But the forestry rules have gotten bogged down by a dispute over whether companies should get credit for carbon accumulating in forests whether or not the companies’ activities are responsible for that accumulation.</p><p>Wood product companies are pushing an accounting approach that would declare their products climate-friendly as long as they’re logged from regions where tree growth exceeds tree harvest, which currently includes enormous parts of North America, Europe and Asia. It’s a bit like declaring that as long as the stock market is going up, every individual stock in that market is automatically up, even the stocks that go down.</p><p>The industry-favored approach would also credit companies with emissions reductions for any ongoing tree growth on their land — and since all the excess carbon in our atmosphere is making trees grow faster, those companies would receive an ironic climate bonus for the effects of climate change.</p><p>In practice, this would produce wacky outcomes. Tree-cutting companies could cancel out their emissions under the protocol by simply buying or even leasing additional woodlands. Food companies could market hamburgers as “carbon-negative” if the ranchers had forests nearby, even though beef is by far the leading driver of deforestation. Burning wood for electricity would also pencil out as carbon-negative under the proposal, even though <a href="https://www.pfpi.net/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/UPDATE-800-signatures_Scientist-Letter-on-EU-Forest-Biomass.pdf">hundreds of scientists have warned</a> that burning wood is even worse for the climate than burning coal.</p><p>IKEA could brag that the more wooden furniture you buy, the better it is for forests and the climate. And recycled paper would look worse for the planet than virgin paper, because it doesn’t require additional trees.</p><p>In the <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=p93w7MpbZRw">words</a> of the psychopath Anton Chigurh from the movie “No Country for Old Men”: “If the rule you followed brought you to this, of what use was the rule?”</p><p>The problem, again, is that the key committees overseeing the rewriting of the land-use rules are stacked with corporate representatives, with few independent scientists or environmentalists to speak for the trees. Now experts are sounding alarms. First, the University of Pennsylvania researcher Danny Cullenward quit one of the protocol’s boards, denouncing its “treatment of scientific information — and scientists themselves.” Then Tim Searchinger, a technical director at the World Resources Institute, left his job after 15 years, complaining that his bosses consistently resisted his pleas to make sure science drove the rules.</p><p>Mr. Searchinger’s departure is a particularly ominous sign. My book “We Are Eating the Earth” chronicles his relentless crusades to reform climate accounting. He’s one of the world’s leading authorities on these arcane but far-reaching issues.</p><p>He likes to say that if you want to screw up the world a little, do a bad thing; if you want to screw up the world a lot, set up accounting rules that incentivize everyone to do that bad thing. He’s worried that if the protocol’s leaders approve rules that make logging trees for futons and paper and power look more climate-friendly than leaving trees alone, the rules will incentivize a lot more logging.</p><p>We’re living through a dispiriting moment of global climate fatigue, when companies worldwide are <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/07/17/climate/company-climate-change-commitments-renege.html">backtracking</a> on their climate commitments, but someday the pendulum will swing back toward climate action. The protocol will help define what counts, and it can’t be whatever actions companies are already taking. We’ll need to do a lot better than that.</p><p>Michael Grunwald, a contributing Opinion writer, is the author of “We Are Eating the Earth: The Race to Fix Our Food System and Save Our Climate.”</p><p><em>The Times is committed to publishing </em><a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2019/01/31/opinion/letters/letters-to-editor-new-york-times-women.html"><em>a diversity of letters</em></a><em> to the editor.
