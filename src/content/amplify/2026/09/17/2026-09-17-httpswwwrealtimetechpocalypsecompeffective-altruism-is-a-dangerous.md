<p>Since 2022, I have spoken with a large number of journalists about Effective Altruism and its Bay Area-sibling, Rationalism. For those new to the subject, these communities constitute the “EA” and “R” in “<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TESCREAL">TESCREAL</a>,” which denotes a constellation of overlapping ideologies that have become hugely influential within Silicon Valley. Dr. Timnit Gebru and I <a href="https://firstmonday.org/ojs/index.php/fm/article/view/13636">argued last year</a> that they are largely responsible for launching and sustaining the ongoing race to build superintelligent “God-like AI.”<a data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" href="https://www.realtimetechpocalypse.com/p/effective-altruism-is-a-dangerous/#footnote-1">1</a> In what follows, <strong>I will focus primarily on EA, though much of what I say applies just as well to Rationalism and other TESCREAL ideologies</strong>.</p>
<p>Over and over again, these journalists asked me: “<strong>Is EA a cult?</strong>” Prior to 2022, I would say that <em>aspects</em> of this movement are cult-like. However, by the end of 2022, my answer was an unambiguous: “<strong>Yes, EA is definitely a cult, with its own doctrines and dogmas, charismatic leaders, jargon, and eschatological narratives</strong>.” But I never got around to writing a comprehensive explanation of why my answer changed. This article, in more than 8,000 words, provides that explanation. My guess is that most people will agree with my view by the end of this piece.</p>
<h3>Being Effectively Altruistic</h3>
<p>My initial impression of EA was very positive. Unlike most EAs, I didn’t exactly <em>join</em> the movement. It was more like the movement found and adopted me. Why? Because I had been working on “<a href="https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/0020174X.2019.1658626">existential risks</a>” since about 2009, a couple of years before the EA movement emerged. Initially, EA’s focus was alleviating global poverty, but as the academic <a href="https://medialibrary.uantwerpen.be/files/8518/61565cb6-e056-4e35-bd2e-d14d58e35231.pdf">Mollie Glieberman shows</a>, <strong>leading EAs quickly discovered the idea of existential risks</strong> (events that would prevent us from creating a <a href="https://nickbostrom.com/utopia">techno-utopian paradise</a> among the stars) <strong>and realized that the best way to do the most good in the world is actually reducing such risks</strong>, rather than helping poor people or eliminating factory farming.</p>

<p>This is precisely why Nick Beckstead, a noted EA, <a href="https://80000hours.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/Beckstead-Nick-On-the-Overwhelming-Importance-of-Shaping-the-Far-Future-better-formatting.pdf">argued in 2013</a> that, given the “overwhelming” moral importance of the far future — “millions, billions, and trillions” of years from now — <strong>we should prioritize saving the lives of people in rich countries over those in poor countries, other things being equal</strong>. The reason is that people in rich countries are better positioned to influence the far future.</p>

<p>This idea was named “longtermism” by <a href="https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/qZyshHCNkjs3TvSem/longtermism">William MacAskill in 2017</a>. Before the word existed, the descriptor for those focused on the very far future was just “people interested in existential risk mitigation.” Hence, I was a longtermist before “longtermism” was part of our shared lexicon and the corresponding idea was incorporated into EA as one of its main “cause areas.” This is why I say that EA came to me, rather than me having discovered EA.</p>
<p>Yet, even in those early days, <strong>something seemed off about the community</strong>. It struck me as weirdly<strong> censorial, elitist, and hierarchical, even authoritarian in its structure</strong> — a fact that many later EAs picked up on, too.</p>
<p>I won’t go into a <em>ton</em> of details about my personal experiences in EA (of which there are many), though I will discuss some especially relevant incidents. Suffice it to say, for now, that I was repeatedly told not to write about certain topics; I was chastised for once publishing an article without the “approval” of an EA leader; I was told that if I continued to write about politics for outlets like <em>Salon</em>, I would lose opportunities for funding and collaboration; and after publishing a flurry of critiques beginning in the summer of 2022, once I had parted ways with EA, <strong>I was repeatedly harassed online and sent threats of physical violence</strong> (discussed more below). When I complained about this censorship, these intimidation tactics, and the EA community’s attempt to control its members, others began to speak out. For example, David Pearce, an EA, <a href="https://www.quora.com/What-does-David-Pearce-think-of-Longtermism-in-the-Effective-Altruist-movement/answer/David-Pearce-18">wrote</a>:</p>
<blockquote><p>Sadly, [Émile] Torres is correct to speak of EAs who have been <em>“intimidated, silenced, or ‘canceled.’”</em></p></blockquote>
<p>In 2019, I spent time at the Centre for the Study of Existential Risk (CSER) at Cambridge University. Over late-night drinks with CSER colleagues, I was surprised to hear many suggest that EA — our community — might be a “cult.” This stuck with me, and by the time I started to receive threats in late 2022 and 2023, it became obvious to me that they were right: <strong>EA really is a cult</strong>. What follows is an attempt to explain why I now think this.</p>
<h3>A Huge Red Flag</h3>
<p>EA <a href="https://x.com/RemmeltE/status/1623254338352349185">advertises itself as being open to criticism</a>. But this is a lie. <strong>EA likes the criticisms that it likes</strong> — those targeting specific components within its framework, requiring minor tweaks and adjustments. But <strong>if you criticize </strong><em><strong>key aspects</strong></em><strong> upon which the framework is based, you will be ostracized, attacked, and maybe even threatened with violence</strong>.</p>
<p>I discovered this first-hand, but I wasn’t the only one. In 2021, Carla Cremer and Luke Kemp coauthored an article titled, “<a href="https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3995225">Democratising Risk: In Search of a Methodology to Study Existential Risk</a>.” It targeted the “techno-utopian approach” at the heart of EA-longtermism, arguing for a more democratic and pluralistic approach to studying existential risks. However, <strong>the authors encountered significant resistance from the EA community when soliciting feedback on the article</strong>. In a post on the Effective Altruism forum, Cremer <a href="https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/gx7BEkoRbctjkyTme/democratising-risk-or-how-ea-deals-with-critics-1">recounted the difficulties</a> like this (bold in original):</p>
<blockquote><p>It could be a promising sign for epistemic health that the critiques of leading voices come from early career researchers within the community. Unfortunately, the creation of this paper has not signalled epistemic health. It has been the most emotionally draining paper we have ever written.</p><p>We lost sleep, time, friends, collaborators, and mentors because we disagreed on: whether this work should be published, whether potential EA funders would decide against funding us and the institutions we’re affiliated with, and whether the authors whose work we critique would be upset.</p><p>We believe that critique is vital to academic progress. Academics should never have to worry about future career prospects just because they might disagree with funders. We take the prominent authors whose work we discuss here to be adults interested in truth and positive impact. Those who believe that this paper is meant as an attack against those scholars have fundamentally misunderstood what this paper is about and what is at stake. The responsibility of finding the right approach to existential risk is overwhelming. <strong>This is not a game. Fucking it up could end really badly.</strong></p><p>What you see here is version 28. We have had approximately 20 + reviewers, around half of which we sought out as scholars who would be sceptical of our arguments. …</p><p>The EA community prides itself on being able to invite and process criticism. However, warm welcome of criticism was certainly not our experience in writing this paper. …</p><p>We were told by some that our critique is invalid because the community is already very cognitively diverse and in fact welcomes criticism. They also told us that there is no TUA [techno-utopian approach], and if the approach does exist then it certainly isn’t dominant. It was <strong>these same people that then tried to prevent this paper from being published.</strong> They did so largely out of fear that publishing might offend key funders who are aligned with the TUA.</p><p>These individuals — often senior scholars within the field — told us in private that they were concerned that any critique of central figures in EA would result in an inability to secure funding from EA sources, such as OpenPhilanthropy. We don’t know if these concerns are warranted. Nonetheless, any field that operates under such a chilling effect is neither free nor fair. <strong>Having a handful of wealthy donors and their advisors dictate the evolution of an entire field is bad epistemics at best and corruption at worst.</strong></p><p>The greatest predictor of how negatively a reviewer would react to the paper was their personal identification with EA.</p></blockquote>
<p>The fact that the EA community can’t handle trenchant critiques of central pillars underlying its worldview should be a huge red flag. Again, <strong>it likes the criticisms it likes, so long as they concern minor, tweakable aspects of the general EA framework</strong>. If you target the framework itself, you’d better watch out.</p>
<p>Targeting the framework is exactly what Cremer and Kemp did, and it’s what <a href="https://aeon.co/essays/why-longtermism-is-the-worlds-most-dangerous-secular-credo">my own critiques</a> have aimed to achieve as well, which is why the three of us, independently, had quite negative experiences with EA.</p>
<h3>Fear and Loathing Among the Altruists</h3>
<p>When Cremer published this EA Forum post in 2021, I still didn’t consider EA to <em>be</em> a cult — only that it was <em>cultish</em> in certain ways, such as trying to censor dissent within the ranks of EAs, or silence critics from outside the community.</p>
<p>As noted, my opinion changed in late 2022, after I published a flurry of critiques of EA-longtermism in response to MacAskill’s 2022 book <em>What We Owe the Future</em>. This was just before the <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bankruptcy_of_FTX">collapse of FTX</a>, run by arguably <strong>the most famous EA-longtermist in the world: Sam Bankman-Fried</strong>. MacAskill went on a media blitz, and received a <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QkB3Zq3zoR4">great deal of positive coverage</a>. Much of what he said and wrote during this time rubbed me the wrong way, as I saw it as dishonest and misleading. It was, in my opinion, nothing more than propaganda for an ideology that I considered to be profoundly dangerous, for reasons outlined in <a href="https://aeon.co/essays/why-longtermism-is-the-worlds-most-dangerous-secular-credo">my </a><em><a href="https://aeon.co/essays/why-longtermism-is-the-worlds-most-dangerous-secular-credo">Aeon</a></em><a href="https://aeon.co/essays/why-longtermism-is-the-worlds-most-dangerous-secular-credo"> article from 2021</a>. As one of the only former insiders who became publicly critical of EA, I felt a moral duty to speak out.</p>
<p>This is not to say that I was the only insider who was critical of EA. In fact, <strong>there are </strong><em><strong>a lot</strong></em><strong> of people in EA who think aspects of the community and ideology are rotten</strong>, but choose not to speak out. Instead, <strong>they engage in self-censorship</strong>. Why? Because they worry that speaking candidly about the movement’s problems will lead to them being ostracized, denied funding, and perhaps even harassed or threatened — exactly what Cremer highlights in her EA Forum post and I experienced in 2022 and 2023.</p>
<p>Consider another example: in early 2023, a group of around 10 EAs published <a href="https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/54vAiSFkYszTWWWv4/doing-ea-better-1">a detailed critique</a> of the anti-democratic and eugenic aspects of the community on the EA Forum. However, they posted this anonymously, precisely because they worried about retaliation. They <a href="https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/54vAiSFkYszTWWWv4/doing-ea-better-1">wrote</a>:</p>
<blockquote><p>Experience indicates that it is likely many EAs will agree with significant proportions of what we say, but <strong>have not said as much publicly due to the significant risk doing so would pose to their careers, access to EA spaces, and likelihood of ever getting funded again</strong>.</p><p>Naturally the above considerations also apply to us: <strong>we are anonymous for a reason</strong>.</p></blockquote>
<p>Someone on Twitter (I can’t recall who, or their exact words) responded to this: “I remember this stage of leaving a cult!” This person grew up in a Mormon community but eventually apostatized their faith, losing connections to their family in the process. <strong>In what kind of “intellectual” community do people feel the need to be anonymous when critiquing that community?</strong> That does not reflect well on the “epistemic health” of the movement, to <a href="https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/gx7BEkoRbctjkyTme/democratising-risk-or-how-ea-deals-with-critics-1">quote</a> Cremer.</p>
<p>To this day, I have no idea who those ~10 EAs were (maybe Cremer was one?), but someone whose name I won’t reveal wrote me just after it was posted on the EA Forum to say: “Please don’t promote this article on social media for another month or so. The authors are worried that if their article is linked to you in any way, the EA community will immediately dismiss it.” In other words, <strong>because I had become the leading critic of EA, any whiff of an association with me would be enough to render the entire critique “invalid.”</strong></p>
<p>Almost immediately after I started publishing my critiques in 2022, <strong>I started to received threats of physical violence from the EA community</strong>. A large number of anonymous accounts on social media popped up for the sole purpose of harassing me and my friends. Some were banned by Twitter (before Musk took over) for bad behavior. Two accounts <a href="https://web.archive.org/web/20230813150416/https://twitter.com/GmaPole/status/1580180543148527616">told me that</a> an “EA superhero” would “kneecap” me, one of which later <a href="https://web.archive.org/web/20230817151858/https://twitter.com/GmaPole/status/1584879339438764033">responded</a> to me: “I wish you remained unborn 😭😭🙏🙏.”</p>

<h3>The Prophets of Rationality</h3>
<p><strong>It was at this point that I became convinced that EA probably </strong><em><strong>is</strong></em><strong> a cult</strong>. Others began to notice as well. In late-2022, EA Forum user “nadavb” published an article titled “<a href="https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/7zg6pkrL6nQDLHPmk/ea-may-look-like-a-cult-and-it-s-not-just-optics">EA May Look Like a Cult (And It’s Not Just Optics</a>).” It begins with this description of the community:</p>
<blockquote><p>What if I told you about a group of people calling themselves Effective Altruists (EAs) who wish to spread their movement to every corner of the world. Members of this movement spend a lot of money and energy on converting more people into their ideology (they call it community building). They tend to target young people, especially university students (and increasingly high school students). They give away free books written by their gurus and run fellowship programs. And they will gladly pay all the expenses for newcomers to travel and attend their events so that they can expose them to their ideas.</p><p>EAs are encouraged to consult the doctrine and other movement members about most major life decisions, including what to do for a living (career consultation), how to spend their money (donation advice), and what to do with their spare time (volunteering). After joining the movement, EAs are encouraged to give away 10% or more of their income to support the movement and its projects. It’s not uncommon for EAs to socialize and live mostly with other EAs (some will get subsidized EA housing). Some EAs want even their romantic partners to be members of the community.</p><p>While they tend to dismiss what’s considered common sense by normal mainstream society, EAs will easily embrace very weird-sounding ideas once endorsed by the movement and its leaders.</p><p>Many EAs believe that the world as we know it may soon come to an end and that humanity is under existential threat. They believe that most normal people are totally blind to the danger. EAs, on the other hand, have a special role in preventing the apocalypse, and only through incredible efforts can the world be saved. Many of these EAs describe their aspirations in utopian terms, declaring that an ideal world free of aging and death is waiting for us if we take the right actions. To save the world and navigate the future of humanity, EAs often talk about the need to influence governments and public opinion to match their beliefs.</p></blockquote>
<p>All of this is true. For example, according to <a href="https://80000hours.org/career-reviews/">the EA career-advice organization 80,000 Hours</a>, <strong>among the top 10 “highest-impact career paths” is “helping build the effective altruism community”</strong> (!!). One way of doing this is by “<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earning_to_give">earning to give</a>,” whereby one pursues the most lucrative job possible to give more money to EA-approved charities and the EA community itself. This is precisely what MacAskill convinced Bankman-Fried to do in the early 2010s, which is why Bankman-Fried landed a job on Wall Street and then went into crypto. According to MacAskill, there is nothing morally wrong with working for what <a href="https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5506078de4b02d88372eee4e/t/5bc71d49c830252b777ce7aa/1539775830421/Replaceability%2C+Career+Choice%2C+and+Making+a+Difference.pdf">he calls</a> “<strong>immoral organizations</strong>,” such as Wall Street firms and petrochemical companies — so long as one then donates one’s extra money toward “<a href="https://qz.com/57254/to-save-the-world-dont-get-a-job-at-a-charity-go-work-on-wall-street">saving the world</a>” (another phrase EAs use), you’ll be doing what’s right — even, what’s obligatory — from a utilitarian-esque perspective.</p>

<p>This is especially important given that we live in the “<a href="https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/topics/time-of-perils">Time of Perils</a>,” a period of heightened existential risk. EA-longtermists argue that this period may last for only a century or so, and that if we manage to squeeze through it, what awaits is a veritable “utopia” of personal immortality, endless cosmic delights, and literally “astronomical” amounts of value. <strong>We can build God-like superintelligence, reengineer humanity to become a new posthuman species, resurrect the dead who’ve been cryogenically frozen, and colonize the universe to create a heaven in the literal heavens full of <a href="https://d1wqtxts1xzle7.cloudfront.net/83378274/Toby-Newberry_How-many-lives-does-the-future-hold-libre.pdf?1649336803=&amp;response-content-disposition=inline%3B+filename%3DHow_many_lives_does_the_future_hold.pdf&amp;Expires=1766086524&amp;Signature=RR53ht7mTpEPZ1hedWE3V48Oo0KmXvGRxr2PPCIXCag-DrlQ00YIN7AuPCF69q2u9-8uMlZpM6kQoRXSKcHNXkrLTFPBQataW4YzM4swVKg7Blssq7P3S~ii9kJyYfgIJ-wi6cwLgUtmSYPb~XMyrP0ie8NFGjsQvI-aTfBv4BAC9SMNpOaWUJWF4rGgzv5DTZugJpHGRPSKPr8U6PYjJDowaGnx1gWxOe8hUorNYtShuS9Hv~SsHkocz~tFCQXGJFCZH10Bnsr~qUyELNEx-n4sv~6fhhHxGaqtGW18aQXrgyYtVoUtgl9-MkGZw7ut6--7-WM9bjr7AHzabDHSSA__&amp;Key-Pair-Id=APKAJLOHF5GGSLRBV4ZA">10^45 “digital people.”</a></strong></p>
<p>This is the eschatology of the EA-longtermist cult, and it’s <a href="https://firstmonday.org/ojs/index.php/fm/article/view/13636">played an integral</a> role in launching the ongoing race to build AGI, because — the reasoning goes — <strong>if we build a controllable superintelligence, then we get everything</strong>. With superintelligence, we’ll have a super-engineer, and since paradise is just an engineering problem — Bostrom literally calls it “<a href="https://jetpress.org/v19/bostrom.pdf">paradise-engineering</a>” — a techno-utopian world among the stars will immediately follow.</p>
<p>This is part of the doctrine — the dogma — of longtermism, <strong>as delivered by Prophets of Rationality like Bostrom, Ord, and MacAskill</strong>. And while EAs are open to soft criticisms of particular, tweakable features of this doctrine (as noted), they are very much <em>not</em> open to criticisms of the doctrine itself. That is precisely why I endured an avalanche of harassment and threats in response to my publications, and why the “ConcernedEAs” were afraid to publish their critique using their names.</p>
<h3>Sexual Misconduct in EA</h3>
<p>Even more, 2022 and 2023 saw a number of investigative pieces that revealed other cultish aspects of EA. <em>Time</em> magazine, for example, found rampant sexual misconduct within some EA groups. The <a href="https://time.com/6252617/effective-altruism-sexual-harassment/">article opens</a> with the experiences of Keerthana Gopalakrishnan:</p>
<blockquote><p>When the world started opening up from the COVID-19 pandemic, she moved to San Francisco and went to EA meetups, made friends with other EAs, and volunteered at EA conferences where they talked about how to use evidence and reason to do the most good in the world.</p><p>But as Gopalakrishnan got further into the movement, she realized that “the advertised reality of EA is very different from the actual reality of EA,” she says. She noticed that <strong>EA members in the Bay Area seemed to work together, live together, and sleep together, often in polyamorous sexual relationships with complex professional dynamics</strong>. Three times in one year, she says, men at informal EA gatherings tried to convince her to join these so-called “polycules.” When Gopalakrishnan said she wasn’t interested, she recalls, <strong>they would “shame” her or try to pressure her, casting monogamy as a lifestyle governed by jealousy, and polyamory as a more enlightened and rational approach</strong>.</p><p>After a particularly troubling incident of sexual harassment, Gopalakrishnan wrote a post on an online forum for EAs in Nov. 2022. While she declined to publicly describe details of the incident, she argued that EA’s culture was hostile toward women. “It puts your safety at risk,” she wrote, adding that most of the access to funding and opportunities within the movement was controlled by men. Gopalakrishnan was alarmed at some of the responses. One commenter wrote that her post was “bigoted” against polyamorous people. Another said it would “<strong>pollute the epistemic environment</strong>,” and argued it was “net-negative for solving the problem.”</p><p>Gopalakrishnan is one of seven women connected to effective altruism who tell TIME <strong>they experienced misconduct ranging from harassment and coercion to sexual assault within the community. The women allege EA itself is partly to blame</strong>. <h3>A Checklist of Culty Characteristics</h3>
<p>I don’t know much about a website called “Cult Recovery 101” (seems legit), but it includes a <a href="https://cultrecovery101.com/cult-recovery-readings/checklist-of-cult-characteristics/#:~:text=The%20group%20is%20focused%20on,and%20rabbis%20of%20mainstream%20denominations).">useful list of characteristics of a cult</a>. Let’s take a look at these characteristics (quoted in bold as bullet points), commenting on each in turn:</p>
<ul><li><p><strong>The group is focused on a living leader to whom members seem to display excessively zealous, unquestioning commitment.</strong></p></li></ul>
<p>There is <em>a lot</em> of hero-worship within EA. This was a main point of <a href="https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/DxfpGi9hwvwLCf5iQ/objections-to-value-alignment-between-effective-altruists">yet another EA Forum</a> post by Cremer, bemoaning the worship of IQ.<a data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" href="https://www.realtimetechpocalypse.com/p/effective-altruism-is-a-dangerous/#footnote-3">3</a> People like MacAskill, Bostrom, and Yudkowsky are elevated to <strong>an almost messianic level of cosmic importance</strong>. They are the prophets of EA-longtermism, sent by the Rationality Gods to deliver fundamental truths about the apocalyptic hazards and utopian promises that lie ahead.</p>
<ul><li><p><strong>The group is preoccupied with bringing in new members.</strong></p></li></ul>
<p>EAs spend a great deal of resources trying to convert students on elite university campuses to the cause, because they think these students are most likely to become successful. They even now target high school kids. As noted, the 80,000 Hours website <a href="https://80000hours.org/career-reviews/">lists</a> “Helping build the effective altruism community” as one of its top 10 “highest-impact career paths our research has identified so far.”</p>
<ul><li><p><strong>The group is preoccupied with making money.</strong></p></li></ul>
<p>Absolutely! Earning to give is a good example of this, where the goal is to create home-grown millionaires or billionaires who can funnel their money back into EA for “community building.” But EA has also worked hard to develop relationships with rich and powerful people. MacAskill — unbeknownst to nearly everyone else in the EA community — <a href="https://www.businessinsider.com/sam-bankman-fried-guru-texted-elon-musk-helping-buy-twitter-2022-9">exchanged personal messages with Elon Musk</a> in an effort to get Musk to buy Twitter with Bankman-Fried (lolz). In fact, one journalist <a href="https://archive.ph/xy4MR">described</a> Bankman-Fried’s mission as “<strong>to get filthy rich, for charity’s sake</strong>.” Which “charities”? <strong>Those specifically approved by EA organizations, as well as Bankman-Fried’s own <a href="https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/topics/future-fund">FTX Future Fund</a>, run by Nick Beckstead (mentioned above), which funded longtermist research projects</strong> focused on things like “how to build a superintelligent God that brings about utopia rather than annihilating us.”</p>
<p>EAs are also explicit that <strong>their goal is to acquire a large fraction of the world’s wealth</strong>. Why? Because many of them truly believe in the “moral” mission of EA, and the more <strong>wealth and power</strong> they acquire, the better positioned they’ll be to shape the world today along with the future direction of civilizational development. As I noted in <a href="https://www.salon.com/2022/09/10/selling-longtermism-how-pr-and-marketing-drive-a-controversial-new-movement/">an article for </a><em><a href="https://www.salon.com/2022/09/10/selling-longtermism-how-pr-and-marketing-drive-a-controversial-new-movement/">Salon</a></em>:</p>
<blockquote><p>Yet <a href="https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/MnQ3N7sDFHa56hj2f/movement-building-and-investing-to-give-later">another article</a> [written by EAs] addresses the question of whether longtermists should use the money they currently have to convert people to the movement right now, or instead invest this money so they have more of it to spend later on.</p><p>It seems plausible, the author <a href="https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/MnQ3N7sDFHa56hj2f/movement-building-and-investing-to-give-later">writes</a>, that “<strong>maximizing the fraction of the world’s population that’s aligned with longtermist values is comparably important to maximizing the fraction of the world’s wealth controlled by longtermists</strong>,” and that “<strong>a substantial fraction of the world population can become susceptible to longtermism only via slow diffusion from other longtermists, and cannot be converted through money</strong>.” If both are true, then</p><p><em>we may want to invest only if we think our future money can be efficiently spent creating new longtermists. If we believe that spending can produce longtermists now, but won’t do so in the future, then we should instead be spending to produce more longtermists now instead.</em></p><p>Such talk of transferring the world’s wealth into the hands of longtermists, of making people more “susceptible” to longtermist ideology, sounds — I think most people would concur — somewhat <em>slimy</em>. But <strong>these are the conversations one finds on the EA Forum, between EAs</strong>.</p></blockquote>
<p>Money, wealth, power. A castle in Oxfordshire. Private jets and hundreds of millions of dollars in Bahamian real estate. $400 per plate restaurants to celebrate a book launch. Fostering relationships with deranged billionaires. EA looks a lot like Scientology.</p>
<ul><li><p><strong>Questioning, doubt, and dissent are discouraged or even punished.</strong></p></li></ul>

<p>EA has been hugely influenced by utilitarianism, which essentially says <strong>the ends justify the means</strong>. <p>As I explain in <a href="https://www.truthdig.com/articles/giving-altruism-a-bad-name/">this article for </a><em><a href="https://www.truthdig.com/articles/giving-altruism-a-bad-name/">Truthdig</a></em>, <strong>EA is actually </strong><em><strong>far worse</strong></em><strong> than traditional philanthropy for grassroots organizations, </strong>which has left many people in the Global South <a href="https://dear-humanity.org/effective-altruism-worse-for-poor/">feeling even more hopeless than before</a>. You might also check out the excellent book <em><a href="https://global.oup.com/academic/product/the-good-it-promises-the-harm-it-does-9780197655702">The Good It Promises, The Harm It Does</a></em> for additional accounts of how EA has destroyed philanthropic endeavors, essentially making things much worse for those who aren’t in the cult of EA but want to help others as much as possible.</p>
