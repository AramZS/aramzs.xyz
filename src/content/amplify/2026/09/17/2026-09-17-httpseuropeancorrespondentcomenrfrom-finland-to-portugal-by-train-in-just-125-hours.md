<p>Last week, Jon Worth, a railway activist who advocates for better cross-border train travel across Europe, completed the longest train journey in the EU – from Kemijärvi in northern Finland to Lagos in southern Portugal – in a speedy 125 hours and 31 minutes.</p>
<p>These two locations are about as far apart as you can travel by train within the EU. Worth covered 6,203 km with an Interrail pass, taking 23 trains run by 10 different railway companies. The journey was only possible thanks to the reopening of a railway line between Haparanda in Sweden and Tornio in Finland in August, which had been closed for 38 years.</p>
<p>Worth’s aim was not just to chase a record, but to show “all of the joys and all of the frustrations of long-distance cross-border rail travel with the record bid,” he <a href="https://jonworth.eu/press-release-record-breaking-longest-train-journey-in-the-eu-to-begin-in-kemijarvi/">noted</a> before setting off. “Trying to plan and book cross-border railway trips remains far too complicated for most people, and it has been no different for this trip.”</p>
<p>If everything had gone to plan, the journey would have taken 100 hours and nine minutes. Naturally, it didn’t, and somewhat surprisingly, Deutsche Bahn was not the main culprit. Worth lost 24 hours in Sweden because of a power outage, and another hour and 20 minutes in Portugal because of a delay that then caused a missed connection.</p>

<p>Still, things could have gone much worse, he told The European Correspondent at the end of the trip. “At one point I thought the trip would go wrong due to a WWII bomb found at a building site in Hamburg, but this was cleared before I passed. And I narrowly missed a (small) earthquake in Rhineland-Palatinate.”</p>
<p>His main takeaway? Europe’s rail infrastructure in many places is really fragile. Some routes depend on a single railway line or train, meaning that when something goes wrong, there is often no easy way around it. He also noticed a lack of coordination between some countries.</p>
<p>“But the vast majority of railway staff are amazing, and helped me a lot,” he says. The CEO of Swedish rail company Norrtåg even invited him into the train driver’s cab for an hour.</p>
<p>Any advice for anyone thinking of trying a trip like this? “Use Interrail. Simply that. With Interrail, you can get on regional trains almost anywhere, at any time.”</p>
<p>This flexibility is especially useful when things go wrong, he said. Even on high-speed trains that require reservations, Interrail makes things easier: you usually only pay a small fee, between €5-€20, and can cancel reservations you no longer need.</p>
<p>Going on a trip this complicated with regular tickets would have been “a nightmare”, he said. And it would also have been more expensive. His Interrail pass cost €387, and regular tickets for the same journey would have cost at least €563, he estimated.</p>
<p>The lack of data-sharing and a flexible, uniform ticketing system across national and private railway companies are some of the main obstacles to rail travel across Europe. There are plans to make things easier. In May, the European Commission proposed its “Passenger Package“ – three pieces of legislation designed to make cross-border train travel easier to plan and book.</p>
<p>“This cannot come a moment too soon,” Worth said. “Many more passengers would take trains if only it were easier to plan, and easier to know what's happening in the case of disruptions.”</p>
