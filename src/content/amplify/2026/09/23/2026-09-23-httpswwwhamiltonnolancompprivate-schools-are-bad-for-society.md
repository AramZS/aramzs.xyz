<p>Recent days have seen an outpouring of responses to Nikole Hannah-Jones’ <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/20/magazine/ny-public-schools-black-students-segregation.html?searchResultPosition=1">story</a> about the anguish and uncertainty she experienced over her decision to send her daughter to public neighborhood schools in Brooklyn, in an effort to align her own actions with her values about the pernicious inequality and racism surrounding school choice in America. Hannah-Jones writes with admirable honesty about the price that her daughter and all of the other kids in poorer public schools can pay. Some readers empathized with her ideological struggle; some decried her for not putting on blinders to maximize her own daughter’s educational environment. But the focus of the discussion has been on the question of what individual parents are obligated to do in the context of a school system where quality varies wildly, and competition for top schools is intense and expensive.</p>
<p>While that discussion may have immediate value for current parents, it’s unfortunate that more people haven’t taken this story as a reminder of an obvious conclusion that is always worth repeating: Private schools are bad for society. If we did not allow the existence of private schools, public schools as a group would be much better, and the experience of education as a cutthroat competition between parents would be moot.</p>
<p>One reason that this straightforward truth is often marginalized is that our national dialogue over schooling tends to happen from the perspective of “What should you as a parent do for your child?” rather than from the perspective of “What is the best way to design an educational system in a democratic country?” There is a significant difference between these two questions! To suggest that private schools should not exist is often interpreted as an attack on parents who opt out of public education, which means that the conversation immediately turns defensive and derisive.</p>
<p>There is no need for this. I take it for granted that parents will, above all, act in the interests of their own children. This is human nature and not worth criticizing people for. It is a reality that we can recognize and then incorporate into the design of the best possible public system, which is a more important topic than any individual’s situation. If a parent is trapped in a burning building, their primary goal will be to get their child out safely. But if you are the architect of a building tasked with fire safety, your goal is not “How do we best save that one person’s kid?” Instead, you ask “How do I design the building with enough sprinklers and fire escapes so that everyone inside can survive?” Personal experience informs all of us, yes, but we need to be sure that when we talk about public policy, we are thinking as architects rather than as individuals.</p>
<p>If you allow the most wealthy and powerful segment of society to opt out of public services, <a href="https://www.hamiltonnolan.com/p/everyone-into-the-grinder">those public services will get worse</a>. The reason is simple: You have removed the incentive for the most influential people to use their influence to improve public services. Indeed, we see this dynamic in action everywhere we look. If rich people can all go to a private hospital, the public hospital is worse. If rich people can buy their way out of the TSA security lines, the experience for the regular people who have to wait gets worse. And when the rich people’s kids all go to private schools, the public schools are worse than they would have been if they educated everyone’s kids.</p>
<p>From the perspective of an individual parent, the purpose of school is to give their own kid the best possible education. But from the perspective of society—from the perspective of an architect of public policy—the purpose of schools is to give <em>everyone</em> a good education. Wealthier parents often observe that poorer public schools are full of poorer students with more severe needs. They respond to this by opting out and putting their kid into a private school where the student population has less severe needs, and more resources to address them. This may be rational if the goal is to give their kid the very best, but by isolating the neediest children in poorer public schools, it produces a worse outcome from the perspective of public policy, where every student is equally important.</p>
<p>Consider the counterfactual: There are no private schools. Everyone goes to public school. The rich kids go to public school. The business leaders’ kids go to public school. The New York Times reporters’ kids go to public school. The politicians’ kids go to public school. If those parents observe that the public schools have needs, they will exert their influence and their political pressure and their fundraising prowess to improve the public schools. The public schools will get better. This will benefit their kids, but it will also benefit everyone else’s kids as well. By not allowing all of those influential people to segregate themselves, their influence becomes a public benefit. It decreases inequality, rather than increasing it.</p>
<p>Pretty simple. It feels silly to pretend that there are any secret, high-minded off ramps from this basic observation. Trying to abolish private schools in America might provoke shrieks of outrage unheard since Brown v. Board of Education, sure—but that is an observation of political reality, not a moral argument. The honesty of our national conversation about education would be greatly improved if we would at least acknowledge that private schools will always have the side effect of making public schools worse. If you believe in the right to a public education, and you believe that every child’s education is equally important, then of course you must acknowledge that removing from public schools the segment of kids whose parents are most involved in their education and most capable of taking action and spending money to improve their education will leave the remaining public schools worse off than they would have been had that segment of kids gone to the public schools and brought their parents’ concern and money and influence with them.</p>

<p>For some reason we pretend that declaring that schools could be “separate but equal” by race was absurd, but that “separate but equal” by income and social capital is reasonable. It is not true.</p>
<p>You, as a parent, may admit this fact, but also feel torn, because you love your kid, and you want to do the best for them today, and your local public school is not the best, and you feel obligated to send your kid to a private school. Fine! I’m not insulting you! Recognize that your personal experience, though informative, does not mean that you should craft a public policy preference solely for the purpose of assuaging your own conflicted feelings. It is perfectly honest to say, “I chose a private school because I want the best for my kid, but it is also true that private schools are bad for society and do more to reproduce inequality than to remedy it.” We could then have a productive discussion about what a path to getting rid of private schools would look like, instead of always finding the discussion short-circuited by feelings of guilt and self-justification and resentment. We all live in a flawed system.</p>
<p>Educating a nation full of people well will always require hard work and dedication and innovation and heroism from teachers and principals and coaches and janitors and cafeteria workers and bus drivers and superintendents. There will always be much to be said about the best teaching techniques and curriculum design and standardized testing and funding design. There will always be questions about neighborhood schooling and zoning and busing and teaching very different people to get along with one another. All of this will always be true. The difference is that as long as we allow people with means to opt out of public education, the benefits of educational advancement will not be shared as widely or as fairly as it would be if everyone were educated in a system of public schools.</p>
<p>There are many people who do not care about this, and view education as just another competition to be won, and view other people’s children stuck in worse schools as the losers, while their own children are the winners, and that suits them just fine. If that is your belief then this argument will not sway you. If, however, you believe that all people are created equal, and that every child’s life is equally worthwhile, and that in a democratic nation we are collectively responsible for providing the best possible education to every child whether rich or poor, then you are obligated to admit that allowing certain, lucky children to opt out of public education is not actually a solution to any of public education’s problems. It’s a lifeboat for some, while others drown. We can do better than that. Put everyone in the same boat, and watch how quickly it gets fixed.</p>
