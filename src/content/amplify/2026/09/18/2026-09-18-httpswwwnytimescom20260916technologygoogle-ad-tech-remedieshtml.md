title: Judge Orders Data Sharing and Other Fixes to Solve Google’s Ad Tech Monopoly
---
<p>A federal court unsealed a ruling on Wednesday that said that Google must allow its ad technology to work with rival products and share more data with customers to weaken the company’s dominance in online marketing, effectively ensuring that the tech titan’s business will continue largely unscathed.</p><p>Judge Leonie M. Brinkema, who sits on the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Virginia, <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2025/04/17/technology/google-ad-tech-antitrust-ruling.html">found last year that Google broke the law</a> to protect its control over technology that places ads on pages across the web. Earlier this month, the judge said she had decided against <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/02/technology/google-ad-tech-remedies.html">ordering Google to divest</a> part of its ad tech business, something the Justice Department had demanded. But the details of her order had remained under seal.</p><p>The opinion, which was unsealed on Wednesday, ordered Google to make its products interoperable with rivals. The company must also share data about its ad auctions with publishers, a group of customers that run websites and use the technology to sell ad space. It must also appoint an internal monitor to track its compliance with the ruling.</p><p>Measures to break up Google’s ad tech business were “neither realistic nor needed,” Judge Brinkema added in her 106-page opinion.</p><p>The ruling was a victory for Google, which has twice been deemed a monopolist by federal judges in the wake of major government lawsuits over its <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2024/08/05/technology/google-antitrust-ruling.html">dominance in both search</a> and ad technology. But the courts have now twice declined to order the tech giant to break up its business.</p><p>Instead, judges have mandated narrow changes to Google’s practices, allowing the company to forge ahead in the race for artificial intelligence without major shifts to its underlying business. The company has poured money into A.I. as it looks to fend off challengers like OpenAI and Anthropic, weaving the technology into its search engine and other products.</p><p>Judge Brinkema acknowledged in her order that A.I. had not yet changed the ad tech industry in the way it has disrupted the search business. But she said that the lengthy timeline to break up the company risked being outpaced by changes including “imminent industry disruptions caused by A.I.”</p><p>Associate Attorney General Stanley E. Woodward Jr. said in a statement that the ruling “marks a significant victory” for the Justice Department’s “efforts to protect and restore competition.”</p><p>“We will continue to review the opinion to consider the Department’s options,” he said.</p><p>“We’re very pleased the court rejected the D.O.J’s proposal to break apart tools that help small businesses reach new customers and grow,” said Lee-Anne Mulholland, Google’s global head of regulatory affairs.</p><p>Courts have shown a reluctance to break up companies, said Nikhil Lai, a principal analyst at Forrester. Instead, they have favored penalties that might make it easier for competitors to challenge Google.</p><p>“Innovation might need to be the more competitive force than litigation,” Mr. Lai said. The changes ordered by the court are designed to let other ad platforms flourish. Still, some ad tech and publisher executives said they did not go far enough.</p><p>Millions of advertisers use Google’s tools to place ads around the web. The Justice Department successfully argued that the company had used its control over ad technology to take a larger cut from ad sales than it could have taken in a competitive market. Those ads run on pages, like a news or recipe site, and are sold by Google’s system as the pages load.</p><p>Google’s ad tech business brought in $30 billion last year, or about 8 percent of the revenue for its parent company, Alphabet. Its ad tech revenue has declined for 16 straight quarters, and analysts estimate that it accounts for less than 1 percent of the company’s profit.</p><p>The changes unveiled by Judge Brinkema on Wednesday largely aligned with overlapping recommendations made by Google and the Justice Department to correct the company’s monopolistic behavior, according to the opinion.</p><p>Google had argued at a hearing in May that it could not divest its online ad exchange tools, which are tightly integrated with other parts of its business. Instead, Google offered to make its ad pricing more transparent to marketers and rival ad businesses, and to appoint a trustee to oversee the changes.</p><p>“We’re disappointed the court stopped short of requiring a breakup after finding Google illegally monopolized and tied together multiple parts of the digital advertising market,” Jason Kint, the chief executive of Digital Content Next, said after the court’s ruling earlier this month. “Google has an extraordinary ability to manipulate markets and turn constraints to its advantage.”</p>
