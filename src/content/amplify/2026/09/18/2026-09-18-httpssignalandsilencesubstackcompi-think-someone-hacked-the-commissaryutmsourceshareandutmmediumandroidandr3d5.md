<p><em><strong>Originally published: Aug. 28, 2026 at 1:18 p.m. PT.</strong></em></p>
<p><em><strong>Last Updated: Sep. 1, 5:55 pm PT</strong></em></p>
<blockquote><p><em>Since publication, Stars and Stripes, Military Times/Navy Times, and multiple others have independently reported on the multi-base refrigeration failures, with the Pentagon now acknowledging a “possible refrigeration disruption” at numerous DeCA commissaries.</em></p></blockquote>
<p><strong>Near-simultaneous refrigeration failures or significant issues impacting at least six military installations have now been confirmed through official sources during the last few days, with additional independent confirmation of multiple other incidents.</strong></p>
<p>Self-aware enough to know this sounds insane, but I need you to stick with me.</p>
<h4><strong>I think there is a nonzero chance someone has hacked the commissary freezers.</strong></h4>
<p>Not one freezer, not one grocery store, not just ANY grocery store, either.</p>
<p>The refrigeration systems at military commissaries (tax-free grocery stores for military personnel and their families located on bases across the country) appear to be under some type of siege; either their own aging fleet of equipment is deciding to seppuku in perfect harmony, or by something (or someone) more nefarious.</p>
<p>Where to even begin?</p>
<h1>It started with doomscrolling, obviously.</h1>
<p>Flipping through my normal rotation of social media, I started seeing scattered posts lamenting the commissary suddenly losing their entire refrigerated &amp; frozen sections.</p>
<p>All cold food spoiled, or removed from shelves.</p>
<p><em>Unfortunate and wasteful,</em> I thought, but inconsequential to me personally. I don’t shop there, I have no stake in the availability of my frozen favs, plus the commissary is not exactly known for smoothly functioning operations, thus, moving on.</p>
<p>But then I saw another post, and another… with a chorus of comments</p>
<p><em>huh, how odd, the same thing is happening here. </em></p>
<p><em>What are the chances</em>!</p>
<p>The pattern caught my attention, and what followed was a deep dive into military social-media chatter, commercial refrigeration, defense procurement contracts, and network security refreshers in an attempt to resurrect the rudimentary cybersecurity knowledge my degrees required.</p>
<p>‘Twas never my strongest subject. <em>When would I <strong>ever</strong> need to use this</em>, I distinctly remember thinking. (Freezers didn’t have networks, in the olden days)</p>

<h1><strong>Meet DeCA</strong></h1>
<p>I learned that commissaries (of which there are ~235 worldwide) aren’t actually independently operated by whatever military installation or base they happen to sit on.</p>
<p>They’re run by the Defense Commissary Agency (DeCA), an agency seated within the Department of Defense.</p>
<p>DeCA, as expected, has a whole refrigeration-control infrastructure.</p>
<p>In March 2026, DeCA issued procurement documents seeking support for <strong>“Facilities Maintenance, Call Center Support, and Remote Monitoring Control System (RMCS) Management.” </strong></p>
<p>It covers approximately <strong>182 DeCA locations</strong>, encompassing all 14 on my original list.</p>
<p>That alone doesn’t mean anything, however. They’re DeCA stores… of course they’re in a DeCA facilities document.</p>
<p>What matters is what the system <em>actually</em> does.</p>

<p><strong>“Defrost shall be controlled through the RMCS”</strong></p>
<p>Which brings us back to Fort Huachuca.</p>
<p><strong>Every freezer entered defrost mode.</strong></p>
<p>The installation itself says the power didn’t fail, and that defrost actually heated the food.</p>
<p>DeCA’s own engineering documentation says <strong>defrost is controlled through its refrigeration monitoring/control system.</strong></p>
<h2><strong>And these systems aren’t necessarily isolated inside the stores.</strong></h2>
<p>Another <a href="https://prod.commissaries.com/sites/default/files/2020-05/MG-7%20Nelson%20HDEC03-20-F-0035.pdf">DeCA refrigeration contract</a> describes <strong>Refrigeration Monitoring and Control Systems (RMCS) </strong>located at individual commissaries whose refrigeration and HVAC alarms are <strong>monitored remotely </strong>24/7.</p>
<p>Per the contract, the contractor was required to maintain a <strong>“master control system for all of the RMCS” </strong>somewhere in the continental United States.</p>
<p>Important caveat, because this is where it’s really easy to jump ahead (spoken by a professional jump-aheader): this does <strong>not</strong> mean someone at DeCA headquarters can remotely hit a proverbial DEFROST EVERY COMMISSARY button. It establishes centralized monitoring infrastructure.</p>
<p>Exactly how much remote control exists, where current master systems are hosted, and whether affected stores share equipment remains unclear. Publicly available information is limited here, and it’s not exactly a hotly discussed topic, as you can reasonably imagine.</p>
<p>But we can establish networked control at individual commissaries independently.</p>
<p>The contractor that allegedly built the newer commissary at <strong>Robins AFB</strong>, one of the installations with reported problems, describes the building as having <strong>RSMS controls managing all of the store’s refrigeration and HVAC systems.</strong></p>
<p>Again: centralized refrigeration control is not suspicious. It’s (apparently) how modern supermarkets work.</p>
<p>What it does is change the options for what a “freezer failure” can mean.</p>

<h2><strong>Here’s where the timing becomes important:</strong></h2>
<p>On <strong>August 9, 2026</strong>, industrial cybersecurity researchers at Claroty’s Team82 published an <a href="https://blog.claroty.com/team82/research/freeze-the-controller-defrost-the-food-uncovering-vulnerabilities-in-danfoss-refrigeration-controllers">article</a> titled <strong>“Freeze the Controller, Defrost the Food: Uncovering Vulnerabilities in Danfoss Refrigeration Controllers.”</strong></p>
<p>Yes, that is the actual title.</p>
<p>The researchers examined the <strong>Danfoss AK-SM 800A</strong>, a supervisory controller used to centrally manage commercial refrigeration systems. And what did they find?</p>
<p>Vulnerabilities capable of allowing <strong>serious unauthorized access</strong>.</p>
<p>Before anyone screenshots that paragraph and runs away with it:</p>
<p><strong>I have </strong><em><strong>not</strong></em><strong> established that Fort Huachuca uses a Danfoss AK-SM 800A.</strong></p>
<p>I <em>have</em>, however, found a searchable copy of a <a href="https://govtribe.com/file/government-file/06-appendix-03-equipment-inventory-dot-xlsx?">DeCA equipment inventory list</a> identifying a Danfoss AK-SM880 refrigeration monitoring/control system at NAF El Centro - notably, not one of the commissaries on my affected list.</p>
<p>So, Danfoss AK-SM technology exists within the DeCA environment.</p>
<p>Interesting, but not attribution.</p>
<h2><strong>It gets better. Or worse?</strong></h2>
<p>Claroty published a second refrigeration <a href="https://blog.claroty.com/team82/research/chilling-discoveries-unpacking-vulnerabilities-in-copeland-xweb-pro-controllers">investigation</a> on the <em>same </em>day, this time, about something called the <strong>Copeland XWEB Pro</strong> supervisory controller (<a href="https://iqelectro.com/products/xweb500d-8k000-copeland-xweb-remote-monitoring-and-alarm-management-module?currency=USD&amp;country=US&amp;variant=51063631413546&amp;utm_source=google&amp;utm_medium=cpc&amp;utm_campaign=Google%20Shopping&amp;stkn=2aa40958dfb3&amp;srsltid=AfmBOoqUY9v0NSumv29SrYWJ_gPLqXyAaDGjcdvOj1hz_7t_W1t_cZo8zvQ">$5,509</a> + shipping, in case you’re in the market; fair warning, this isn’t exactly a glowing sales pitch).</p>
<p>They found <strong>23 vulnerabilities, 21 rated ‘high severity’</strong>, and ultimately demonstrated the part I actually care about: After compromising the supervisory controller, they could physically manipulate the refrigeration equipment.</p>
<p>Copeland itself already issued a <a href="https://webapps.copeland.com/dixell/Content/Pages/SystemSoftwareUpdate/XWEB-PRO/Security%20update%20%20XWEB-PRO%20-%20Product_Bulletin_February_2026.pdf">security bulletin</a> acknowledging vulnerabilities and explicitly advising customers <strong>never to expose the control system or its web interface to the broader internet</strong>.</p>
<p>So: <em>Can someone actually hack a commercial refrigeration controller and make it do things?</em></p>
<p><strong>Yes.</strong></p>
<h2><strong>Does that mean </strong><em><strong>this</strong></em><strong> was a cyberattack?</strong></h2>
<p>No. Too early to call it that.</p>
<p>And this is where I am currently stuck.</p>
<p>A common software or configuration problem, update-gone-wrong, a communications failure, or some boring DeCA-wide maintenance issue could explain it too.</p>
<p>A bunch of unrelated aging refrigeration systems deciding to off themselves during August is also not exactly unimaginable. DeCA’s own March procurement specifically identified <strong>aging infrastructure</strong> as something it is trying to manage. If you’ve ever shopped at a commissary, you can attest to the fact that the facilities are not what I would call <em>top of the line</em> boutique shopping experiences.</p>
<p>There are also historical examples of commissary refrigeration outages. Refrigeration equipment does, in fact, break.</p>
<p>But the thing I can’t get past is <strong>Fort Huachuca’s failure mode</strong>.</p>
<p>Not: <em>the freezer compressor died.</em></p>
<p>Not: <em>the power went out.</em></p>
<p>Not even:<em> the refrigeration system stopped cooling.</em></p>
<p><strong>Every freezer went into active defrost.</strong></p>
<p>The function that did it, per DeCA’s own engineering documents, is controlled through the RMCS.</p>
<p>This is not proof of a cyberattack, but it is quite a series of coincidences.</p>
<p>There is still no public evidence that the affected stores share the same RMCS vendor, controller, firmware, contractor, or network.</p>
<h1><strong>The IoT of it all</strong></h1>
<p>This is where we have to talk about the Internet of Things (IoT), because if I had to think about how the internet works inside a grocery-store freezer today, you do too.</p>
<p>We’ve spent years connecting <em>everything</em> to networks because, obviously, being able to monitor and control stuff remotely is convenient. We’ve seen Wall-E.</p>
<p>Your printer, camera, washing machine, smart ring, toothbrush, car, and <em>so </em>many more create the IoT. In-store refrigeration systems are, apparently, also things that we have connected to computers. How progressive.</p>
<p>The con? Every time we make an object network accessible, we create a potential way to exploit it.</p>
<p>Remember that <a href="https://blog.claroty.com/team82/research/chilling-discoveries-unpacking-vulnerabilities-in-copeland-xweb-pro-controllers">Copeland experiment</a> from back yonder? Once Claroty compromised the supervisory controller, they could remotely set the compressors, cooling fans, or defrost cycle, heating or cooling what’s inside.</p>
<p>That is the important part.</p>
<p>The computer doesn’t have to “hack” the freezer in some sci-fi sense. The computer is <em>supposed</em> to tell the freezer what to do.</p>
<p>Claroty’s separate <a href="https://blog.claroty.com/team82/research/freeze-the-controller-defrost-the-food-uncovering-vulnerabilities-in-danfoss-refrigeration-controllers">Danfoss investigation</a> found security bypass and remote vulnerabilities in another commercial refrigeration controller, plus <strong>thousands of its management interfaces exposed to the public internet.</strong></p>
<p>Again, <strong>none of this is proven to be connected to DeCA.</strong></p>
<h3>It does, however, explain why <strong>someone hacked the military’s freezers</strong> is an objectively less stupid sentence than I initially thought.</h3>
<p>I seem to have picked an <em>exceptionally</em> timely week to become concerned about industrial controllers.</p>

<p>On August 19, only eight days before Ft. Huachuca announced its freezer failure, the NSA and its partners <a href="https://www.nsa.gov/Press-Room/Press-Releases-Statements/Press-Release-View/Article/4578318/nsa-and-others-release-report-on-active-threats-of-programmable-logic-controlle/">warned</a> that cyber actors are currently conducting <strong>“targeted reconnaissance and capability development” against U.S.-based industrial controllers</strong>. Targets include energy, water, manufacturing, food production and commercial facilities.</p>
<p>Their concern is also physical: successful exploitation could cause <strong>equipment damage, downtime, potential harm to health and life, as well as disruption of industrial processes</strong>.</p>
<p>Different equipment. No demonstrated connection to DeCA.</p>
<p>But suddenly, my concern about computers making physical equipment, ya know, <em>do</em> things feels exceptionally timely.</p>
<h1><strong>Why does this matter beyond spoiled groceries?</strong></h1>
<p>This is where my silly little freezer saga collides with <em>actual</em> national security.</p>
<p>The <a href="https://media.defense.gov/2026/Jan/23/2003864773/-1/-1/0/2026-national-defense-strategy.pdf">2026 National Defense Strategy</a> identifies cyber capabilities among the growing direct threats to the American homeland.</p>
<p>In June, the Department of Energy <a href="https://www.energy.gov/ceser/articles/ceser-supports-oil-and-natural-gas-subsector-critical-energy-security-exercises">warned</a> that nation-state adversaries are actively <strong>pre-positioning inside U.S. critical-infrastructure networks</strong>, while other actors increasingly target the OT/industrial-control systems that make physical equipment <em>do things</em>.</p>
<p>Obviously, losing frozen meals is not the same as losing the power grid.</p>
<p>But network-connected software controlling physical infrastructure?</p>
<p><strong>Same security problem, considerably lower stakes.</strong></p>
<h1><strong>Why yes, the foreign-state actor possibility crossed my mind, now that you ask!</strong></h1>
<p>There is a possibility my security-trained brain can’t tune out when thinking about what the purpose of an incident like this could be.</p>
<h3><em><strong>If </strong></em><strong>this were a cyberattack,</strong><strong>why mess with freezers?</strong></h3>
<p>Well, we already know one answer is in the playbook: <strong>Reconnaissance and pre-positioning.</strong></p>
<p>A <a href="https://www.cisa.gov/sites/default/files/2024-02/aa24-038a-jcsa-prc-state-sponsored-actors-compromise-us-critical-infrastructure_1.pdf">2024 Joint Cybersecurity Advisory</a> from CISA, NSA, FBI and international partners concluded <strong>with high confidence</strong> that Chinese-sponsored ‘Volt Typhoon’ hackers were positioning themselves inside U.S. critical infrastructure networks.</p>
<p><em><strong>Why?</strong></em></p>
<p><strong>To enable future disruption.</strong></p>
<p>The agencies confirmed compromises across communications, energy, transportation and water systems. The hackers gained access, and then conducted extensive reconnaissance to understand victim networks. In some instances, they <strong>maintained access for at least five years.</strong></p>
<p>That sounds considerably less theoretical.</p>
<p><strong>I am absolutely not saying </strong><em><strong>China hacked the commissaries.</strong></em></p>
<p>But if this ultimately proves to be a cyber incident rather than a mundane hardware failure, the national-security question wouldn’t just be <em>who wants to ruin frozen pizzas?</em></p>
<p>It would be <strong>whether the refrigeration failures had anything to do with access to a mundane piece of network-connected infrastructure operated by a DoD agency, and if so, what the purpose of that access was. </strong></p>
<p>A freezer is relatively low-stakes. Access controls, utilities, HVAC, water systems? Not so much. Different systems, obviously, but the same broader security problem: physical infrastructure sitting behind network-connected controls.</p>
<p>Speculative, but not an entirely hypothetical threat.</p>
<h2>And then the FBI seized a Chinese hacking platform</h2>
<p>Because the timing of this all wasn’t already odd enough: On August 26, the DOJ and FBI <a href="https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/justice-department-and-fbi-seize-platforms-operated-and-used-china-state-sponsored-hackers">announced</a> the seizure of infrastructure belonging to a PRC state-sponsored hacking group, QTFY.</p>

<p>QTFY’s QScan wasn’t merely infecting IoT devices. NSA describes it as a <strong>reconnaissance and exploitation platform used to find vulnerabilities in networks</strong>, while QTFY used compromised IoT devices themselves to disguise subsequent hacking activity.</p>
<p>The group has targeted the <strong>Defense Industrial Base</strong>, and DOJ says targets included NASA, DOE, the Federal Reserve, DOJ and the U.S. Senate, alongside power companies and defense contractors.</p>

<p>Again: <strong>zero evidence connects QTFY to DeCA.</strong></p>

<h1><strong>Onto the fun part: Action Items</strong></h1>
<p>I decided to exercise my God and country-given right and ask for the boring paperwork, which I’m sure will be produced to me in a timely and reasonable fashion.</p>
<p><em>/s, of course</em></p>
<p>I’ve put together requests to DeCA for the Fort Huachuca refrigeration work order, RMCS alarm/event records, maintenance findings, and whatever root-cause determination exists.</p>
<p>I’m also requesting records concerning whether DeCA identified a common technical problem affecting multiple commissaries during August - including network, controller, software/configuration, or cybersecurity incidents.</p>
<p>Most importantly, I want the equipment inventory. DeCA maintains remarkably detailed equipment records, and ideally I can get my paws on the RMCS/controller manufacturer and model for <strong>every commissary</strong>, not merely those on my list.</p>
<p>Because then, this becomes testable.</p>
<p>If affected commissaries disproportionately share a controller, firmware iteration, contractor, or recent change? That becomes quite interesting.</p>
<p>If 90% of DeCA uses the same controller, finding it at affected stores tells us basically nothing.</p>
<p>And, if the maintenance logs come back <em>relay failed/compressor died/lost refrigerant/blown fuse</em> across unrelated stores, my little theory dies an appropriately boring death and I begin my apology tour.</p>
<p>Sorry in advance (just in case).</p>
<h1><strong>Bottom Line</strong></h1>
<p>For right now, <strong>“DeCA was hacked”</strong> is not a substantive enough argument to make, however I'd be lying if I said it wasn’t still my hunch.</p>
<p>Publicly, I'm not one for frivolous claims. So I'll take off my tin hat for you, dear reader, and stick to what <em>is</em> supported:</p>
<p><strong>At least six military installations across the country have now officially acknowledged refrigeration/freezer failures or significant refrigeration issues during the same general period, with additional incidents reported elsewhere. At least one involved </strong><em><strong>every</strong></em><strong> freezer entering a defrost state while power remained on; DeCA engineering specifications state that defrost is controlled through the RMCS. A cybersecurity incident remains a plausible hypothesis, but there is not yet enough evidence to support that fact.</strong></p>
<p>So, that was today’s deep dive. This is perhaps the longest my brain has idled on refrigeration-related topics. Unfortunately, I need the government to answer my FOIA request in order to free myself from The Pointed Questions That Persist, as I call them… and we all know how quickly that goes.</p>
<p>As I stood in front of my own freezer while making dinner, I caught myself pondering the network security risks of those ridiculous <a href="https://www.samsung.com/us/refrigerators/french-door/">fridges</a> with the integrated wifi-enabled touchscreens.</p>
<p>Every day, I draw closer to becoming a luddite.</p>
