<p>There is a house in Clinton Hill that’s haunted. Zoe Kazan is sure of it. She feels it in her bones. We’re on a rambling walk from Prospect Park to Fort Greene; we’ll just pass by it, I suggest, and see if I can tell which one it is. It doesn’t look haunted, she explains, just in case I’d search for broken shutters or a dilapidated roof or a sign that reads GHOST LIVES HERE, but when she did this with her former babysitter, she felt the haunting immediately. We walk down one shady block after another until she slows in front of an average, well-kept brownstone across from a school on one of the nicest blocks in the neighborhood. It’s not at all spooky, especially in broad summer daylight, and yet. “Look,” she says, holding up her goose-pimple-covered flesh. “Do you really not feel it?”</p>
<p>Kazan feels ghosts everywhere she goes. “I had so many encounters as a child,” she says. “I have started a practice of going into new spaces and being like, <em>I am not interested. Do not talk to me. Do not appear to me. I am not curious. I am not open.</em>” She knows how things from the past can affect her if she’s not prepared to encounter them.</p>
<p>Sometimes a ghost is not what you’d expect; you can be haunted by an author’s legacy or by the character imprinted upon you when you first read a book, as Cathy Ames, one of the most delicious anti-heroines in literature, was upon Kazan. When she read <em>East of Eden</em> at 15, she had an immediate feeling of ownership. “Long before I ever started the process of adapting this,” she says of the 1952 novel that John Steinbeck considered his magnum opus and that her grandfather the director Elia Kazan adapted into a film in 1955, “I was like, <em>That one belongs to me.</em>”</p>
<p>Not long after she discovered Steinbeck’s novels, her parents rewarded her obsession by taking her on a trip up the California coast. They drove from L.A. all the way to Steinbeck’s hometown of Salinas, where much of <em>East of Eden</em> takes place. It turned out to be nothing like she’d imagined from the book’s descriptions. “It’s concrete. You know, it’s a town very changed by time,” she says. Steinbeck’s childhood home is now a restaurant. But there were things that felt exactly as the book described them: the surrounding countryside; the quality of the light. She’d watched her father listen to recordings of Steinbeck’s voice in the museum dedicated to the author and was moved by how much they meant to her family.</p>
<p>Maybe it was because of that, or because she’d read this big important book at an age when everything means the most it will ever mean, that the feeling of ownership stuck with her well into adulthood. She would read about another <em>East of Eden</em> project in development, a film version directed by Ron Howard or Tom Hooper or to which Jennifer Lawrence was attached as a star, and she’d say to her partner, the actor Paul Dano, “Oh, it’s not how I would do it.” She’d always envisioned it as a series. Eventually, she wrote to her agents: “Ugh. This is so annoying, but, like, if the rights to <em>East of Eden</em> ever come up, can you let me know? I’d really like to pitch on it.”</p>
<p>Our walk in the park is not an athletic one. It’s a languid stroll befitting the already too warm morning and Kazan’s outfit — Adidas, blue ankle socks, running shorts, and an oversize button-down. We are wandering through summer camp after summer camp when she stops dead and starts furiously searching for something on her phone. “Motherfucker. Where is it!” She’s determined to find the text message she got from her agent in 2019, the one telling her that a production company had the rights to the book and needed someone to adapt it into a show that would focus more on Cathy Ames: “You interested?”</p>
<p>As power walkers and stroller pushers divert from their path to avoid us, she keeps scrolling through years of conversations, muttering to herself, “I can do this. I can get to 2019. It’s not like there’s so much; it’s just like, ‘Oh, I love Willa Cather,’‘Oh, I’m getting induced on this date.’” Then, more scrolling: “Fuck me. Motherfucker. This is so embarrassing.” Until, six minutes later, she proudly produces what she wrote back:</p>

<p>She was on set in Australia. She flew home the week before Christmas with plans to meet the team the day after she landed. She had a 17-hour flight to prepare. She downloaded the book to her iPhone and reread it while trying to entertain her then-1-year-old daughter. She landed, went to her parents’ house, slept, then put on her lucky shirt: a vintage ’50s button-down with a map of California on it. She hopped into her dad’s car and headed to the production company’s offices in Hollywood and, on the way, sketched out her ideas for an eight-episode limited series on the back of an envelope she found in the glove box.</p>
<p>“I don’t believe in God,” she explains, “but what happened during that pitch was a gift from God, or a higher power, or the universe.” She laid out her personal attachment to the book, how she wanted to tell the story of its main family, the Trasks, and also the story of our fractured country. And if that wasn’t enough to sell it, she talked about her own complicated family legacy.</p>
<p>There’s nepo baby, and then there is Zoe Kazan. Her grandfather is known for so much: his direction of <em>Death of a Salesman</em> and <em>A Streetcar Named Desire,</em> his introduction of a kind of naturalism into American theater acting, and the way he pushed actors to deeply inhabit their characters’ psychological states, arguably the bedrock of performance in theater and film today. He was a revered artist and intellectual who was friends with James Baldwin.</p>
<p>When Elia Kazan directed <em>East of Eden,</em> Steinbeck was considered one of the greatest living American novelists, the scribe of the West and of America’s underclass. The two became friends in the early ’50s, when Steinbeck wrote the screenplay for another film Kazan directed. There’s a letter between Zoe’s grandmother and Elaine Steinbeck, John Steinbeck’s wife. Her grandfather shows up in journals Steinbeck kept during the process of writing <em>East of Eden.</em> Though there was a 1981 miniseries adaptation of the novel starring Jane Seymour, it’s Zoe’s grandfather’s version, starring James Dean, that is best known. Elia’s son, Zoe’s dad Nicholas Kazan, became a screenwriter who then married another screenwriter, Robin Swicord, and they went on to write or adapt movies that meant something in most millennial childhoods (<em>Little Women, Matilda, Practical Magic</em>). That’s one hell of a legacy.</p>
<p>All the elder Kazan’s success, all that innovation, is often complicated by a single decision he made in 1952, the same year <em>East of Eden</em> was published. Kazan testified before the House Un-American Activities Committee, naming eight artists who were affiliated with the Communist Party. While he would go on to direct cinematic masterpieces, the artists he named were blacklisted for decades. He became a hero to McCarthyite anti-communists, but to many others, he was a traitor who had sold out his friends. His work thereafter was seen by some as the result of a deal with the devil. In 1954, when he directed <em>On the Waterfront,</em> starring Marlon Brando as a dockworker who testifies against a corrupt union boss, many read it as an allegorical justification for his own decisions. It is also one of his most acclaimed films. In his autobiography, <em>A Life,</em> published in 1988, he defended his choice to testify, asserting he was forced to choose between two evils. “I’d rather hurt them a little than hurt myself a lot,” he told the Washington <em>Post</em> at the time. His actions became a family burden, his damaged reputation metastasized into his granddaughter’s burden, and the byzantine legacy became part of her pitch for her version of <em>East of Eden,</em> a book that is at its core about shame and inheritance and self-determination. “I think I’ve tried to stand on my own feet in whatever way I can,” she says. “This book is the thing that I felt like I had to tell, I had to take on. So I had to talk about that” — meaning her grandfather — “smack on.”“Otherwise, there’s, like, a huge elephant in the room.”</p>
<p>Despite the success of the first pitch, Kazan had to prove herself to another set of executives before, in the spring of 2020, the deal closed and the adaptation was in her hands. It seemed strange to me, given her family connections, that her getting the job was ever in question. I wonder, out loud, if maybe we’re in a post-nepo-baby world or at least have reached an era when having a famous last name no longer means something negative.</p>
<p>“It’s news to me if that’s true,” Kazan replies with a snort.</p>
<p>“I feel like it’s true,” I say encouragingly but beginning to sweat under her skepticism.</p>
<p>“Okay, great. Um, I’ll take your word for it,” she says with just the slightest sharpness.</p>
<p>The idea hangs between us for a beat longer than is comfortable.</p>
<p>“I don’t think it was helpful for me to be burdened by my family’s connection when adapting this. I had to put it aside,” she says after assuring me I haven’t made things as awkward as they feel. “There’s a mimetic process I was going through trying to reconcile my relationship with this material as being both my own and belonging to my family.” When she decided to adapt <em>East of Eden,</em> she knew it would require her to confront a legacy she’d spent most of her life and career avoiding. And in doing so, she feels she can finally accept it. She wonders if other people will be able to as well.</p>

<p>Zoe Kazan’s <em>East of Eden </em>is seven episodes. (She wanted eight. But, alas, budget constraints.) Florence Pugh is her Cathy. Christopher Abbott and Mike Faist are her Adam and Charles Trask. From the first frame of Pugh as Cathy, dragging herself, bloody and battered, across a field to the Trask house, there is very little doubt that this is not your grandfather’s <em>East of Eden.</em></p>
<p>Elia’s version focused on the teenage Trasks, Aron and Cal — the final quarter of the book. (And really, James Dean’s performance makes it feel like it’s just about Cal.) Zoe’s takes on the whole thing. It seems unwieldy (and ambitious and delusional and maybe egotistical) to choose to adapt a work like this. At more than 600 pages, the story is unfathomably big. The novel centers on the stoic Adam Trask, who moves to the Salinas Valley to build a new life for himself and his wife, Cathy, but it spirals out, giving ample space to Adam’s brother, Charles, as well as his servant, Lee. It follows an entire other family, the Hamiltons, who bear real-life similarities to Steinbeck’s maternal family. (Steinbeck makes a brief appearance as a child.) It switches perspectives often, spans two wars, includes digressions on good and evil and free will and the California landscape. There is an entire section about Steinbeck’s mother taking an airplane (which Kazan adores). There is a long discussion of the biblical interpretation of the Hebrew term <em>timshel,</em> which Steinbeck translates (controversially) to “thou mayest,” that asks readers if they are doomed to repeat the sins of their forebearers or can choose another path. How does one even start the process of adapting something like that?</p>
<p>In June 2020, Kazan started to try. First, the practical. It was summer during COVID, and she had no child care. “Paul and I were trading three hours a day,” she says. “I took the kid in the morning so he could write, and then she would go down for her nap and I would write. Then he would take her for a few more hours.”</p>
<p>She wanted to do right by Steinbeck, she says, but there was “a chance to improve on some things.” Some decisions, like the choice to excise the story of the Hamiltons, were heartbreaking — Tom Hamilton is her favorite character. Others, like deciding to “keep the racial slurs out of it,” were not. “Because who needs that,” she says matter-of-factly. Her goal wasn’t to modernize the book: “It’s a pet peeve of mine when works that purport to be historically accurate import the politics of the time they are being made in. It makes me fucking crazy!”</p>
<p>While preparing for his audition, actor Hoon Lee, who would go on to play Lee, read the script and figured that a moment when Lee, who speaks in a thick pidgin, is revealed to be an American-born man who code switches was a new addition for contemporary audiences. “That moment felt so incredibly modern,” he says. But in fact it’s taken directly from the book. Kazan’s addition was to devote more time to Lee’s story after he leaves his position at the Trasks. “It would be like a Wong Kar-wai movie,” she says she imagined. “I thought, <em>This character is so private</em>; I felt like he was even keeping secrets from Steinbeck. I wanted to get to know him better.”</p>
<p>Lee was an exploration of how to be good. But Cathy Ames meant something different. Cathy has one of the hardest introductions in literary history: “There was a time when a girl like Cathy would have been said to be possessed by the devil.”</p>
<p>“Cathy seemed to be like no one I’d ever read in my whole life,” Kazan says. “It’s not like I thought she was a role model, but the strength of her insistence that the only thing that matters is what is good for her was so shocking and such an antidote to the good girl that I was.”</p>
<p>Steinbeck wrote Cathy as the personification of Original Sin, and so she’s no-holds-barred levels of evil: She drives a man to suicide (though readers may argue he deserved it); she shoots, poisons, robs, and abandons; and that’s just what she does to the people who profess their love for her. Kazan had to figure out how to treat a monster. Her take isn’t overtly feminist. “That would go against her character,” she explains, “but I’m a feminist coming from a feminist viewpoint. When I read in the book that young Cathy staged a rape and she’s livid because no one believes her, I read that differently. I don’t think it excuses her behavior or makes her a less complicated character if I look at that a little differently than Steinbeck did.”</p>
<p>When Kazan reread the book, she noticed how, “in a weird way, Steinbeck loses control of Cathy as a character and punishes her for that. He starts being really mean about her, like her physical appearance.” Fueled by a soundtrack of Olivia Rodrigo, Kazan tapped into Cathy’s psychology. Her Cathy isn’t a justification; it isn’t even a humanization. It’s an explanation. Monsters aren’t necessarily made, but monsters have a backstory worth being curious about. Pugh was excited to try to pull off the Cathy that Kazan created. “She had it from her perspective, and she didn’t berate her. She tried to understand who this woman was,” Pugh says. “We were aware that we were going to have to do so much thinking to make this person watchable, and that was just a beautiful ode to this woman in that era who wasn’t really given much forgiveness.”</p>
<p>It wasn’t until Kazan was on her flight to New Zealand — a cheaper stand-in for California — to begin shooting that she rewatched her grandfather’s 1955 version. She’d been too superstitious to watch it again while she was figuring out her own. “I felt immense relief because I was like, <em>Oh, it’s even less like the book than I remembered,</em>” she says. “It’s such a loose interpretation of such a small part of the book. And I think I felt like, <em>Oh, I don’t need to worry about this at all!</em>”</p>
<p>Many of the things she really loved about the book weren’t onscreen. No Lee. Cathy wasn’t a central character. The intergenerational aspect of the story was missing. Kazan was free to make her own version. When she looks back, she says, “my whole life there’s been this shadow of my grandfather, and I’ve wanted to not be in that shadow. And then I had to walk right into it in order to get to do this thing I always wanted to do. I asked the universe for that, and it required me to change.”</p>
<p>There’s emotional distance gained by making thousands of small decisions on behalf of the 600-plus people working for you. The stakes were high, but with every choice Kazan made about budget, or costumes, or lighting, or the set, the project somehow became less personal. “She never showed me the cracks of undue pressure because of her family’s legacy with the property. I never felt that,” says Lee.</p>
<p>Being on set wasn’t without stress. Kazan describes it as “the hardest thing” she’s ever done. Pugh says Kazan was a formidable showrunner: “I had to do a lot of really vulnerable things, whether that’s crawling across the field with a broken face or doing five sex scenes in one day. She provided so much protection.”</p>
<p>We exit the park and trade the shade trees for the full force of the summer sun. Kazan pulls a sunbonnet out of her tote bag and ties it on. “I’m a bonnet girl,” she says enthusiastically. She has more, all made for her by <em>East of Eden’s </em>costume designer, Kirsty Cameron.</p>
<p>Our conversation is interrupted by an incoming text from Kazan’s former nanny: “I didn’t expect you to have beef with Swifties.” Kazan sighs. Earlier that week, she’d posted what she considered a fairly innocuous take on some Taylor Swift wedding drama, analyzing why Blake Lively appeared not to attend the Madison Square Garden nuptials when it seemed like every person Swift had ever encountered in her life was there: “i think she didn’t invite *her* because she has come to the end of her marriage plot, thus cutting off access to her first great subject. rather than root for her own divorce, she is generating the next chapter of interpersonal drama for herself via female friendship.”</p>
<p>Kazan seems genuinely surprised at the backlash she received. In her defense, she was thinking of a Jungian writer she loves, Robert A. Johnson. Oh, and also, in her defense, there was a <em>New Yorker</em> article that mirrored her tweet, she says, ever the Yale student. She wound up deleting the tweets because “I don’t need that in my life. <em>No,</em> thank you.” The Swiftie episode gets her thinking about the press cycle she’s about to be in. At 42, she feels disinclined to give fucks, but it doesn’t mean she wants to put her foot in it.</p>
<p>Recently, a women’s website asked her to make a list of her five favorite books and she struggled not to include any books by her favorite author, Alice Munro. After Munro’s death, her daughter Andrea Robin Skinner published an essay alleging that Munro’s second husband sexually abused her starting when she was 9. She revealed that she’d told her mother about the abuse but Munro chose to stay with her husband rather than stand up for her daughter.</p>
<p>“I needed Munro to have been a better mother than that. As a young mother, I was like, <em>Alice Munro wrote during the naps, so I can write during the naps,</em>” she says, her eyes getting teary. Like many people with fierce attachments to specific creative works, she’s been trying to resolve the complicated feeling of loving someone’s artistic output — and needing their work in your life — while acknowledging the harm they’ve done. “Munro’s really painful for me,” she says. “I have not worked it out.”</p>
<p>Since post-production wrapped in March, Kazan has been back in Brooklyn. Her life, while waiting for the big drop, is pleasant, even mundane by some standards. The day we meet, she’s negotiating who will do camp drop-off and who will be home for the kids after.</p>
<p>That isn’t to say Kazan isn’t recognizable. She has an “I just see her around the neighborhood” ease, but when she walks into the Clinton Hill restaurant for our next meeting in another <em>East of Eden</em> bonnet, eyes linger on her. Her early film career (and big blue doe eyes, perky ponytail, and air of gentle quirkiness) initially established her as a twee “It” girl of the early aughts, which means she gets double takes from a certain Brooklyn demo (middle-aged hipsters; people who eternally debate if they should get bangs again; people who found <em>Garden State</em> profound in 2004 but have scrubbed it from their Letterboxd accounts). Kazan wrote and starred in 2012’s <em>Ruby Sparks</em> in an attempt to reclaim that manic-pixie pigeonhole.</p>
<p>These days, she writes more than she acts. Her writing investigates and dismantles things that are, if not totally personal, at least a close cousin to “about her.” That work means she feels less pressure to say “yes” to whatever role is put in front of her. It’s something she learned from her parents. “My parents bought their house when they were pregnant with me,” Kazan says. “My dad used to say this thing when people would ask him, ‘What is the thing you’re writing about? What’s the script about?’” He would answer, “It’s about a house.” (Or, more specifically, paying for one.)</p>
<p>Kazan grew up in Santa Monica and recalls how all the “grown-ups” in her life were in the business. “It just seemed really normal and not fancy or different,” she says, and while she acknowledges she comes from a “really privileged background,” she appreciates the way her parents tried to model values to her and her younger sister that had nothing to do with materialism or awards or fame: “They were trying to turn us into decent people. They used their resources and privilege to try to do that for us.”</p>
<p>In the middle of a conversation about her parents’ careers, I notice her hands have dropped to her lap and are clasped. Her lips are pressed in a tense line. She senses what I’m doing, working up the family tree, edging gently toward the Kazan with the elephantine legacy. Her mother’s story she gives freely (came to New York from Florida, never knew screenwriting could be a career, initially did copywriting). About her father she offers a more careful response.</p>
<p>“He had to come into it on his own,” she says slowly. She isn’t trying to be cagey, she explains. “I think a thing can happen in families where there’s a very famous person; there becomes almost, like, a code of silence. I respect his privacy, and he’s worked very hard for that, so I try not to tell on my parents too much.” The code extends to her cousins on her father’s side, too, with whom she shares a group-chat-and-family-reunion kind of closeness. She’s aware of the exposure they went through when her grandfather released his autobiography. It forced into daylight things they may have preferred not to discuss; it strained relationships they would have rather preserved. (Though she claims it isn’t autobiographical, her first play, 2009’s <em>Absalom,</em> is about the reunion of an aging narcissistic patriarch who has just published a memoir and his pissed-off adult children.)</p>
<p>Kazan has been asked about her grandfather before, including once in a 2007 interview in <em>Backstage</em> during a modest press run for an Off Broadway play. It was early enough in her ascension that she didn’t avoid responding: “I had an uncomplicated relationship with my grand-father … I wasn’t around in the ’50s and can’t really understand the climate, but I do understand why someone would make the choice he did.” Then in 2014, she went on a Canadian talk show, <em>George Stroumboulopoulos Tonight.</em> The host played a clip of her grandfather from 1977. As he described fleeing Turkey as a persecuted Greek at age 4 and coming to America, her expressions revealed 15 different emotions before settling on tense discomfort. Her rambling response ended with “My grandpa was so attacked for his political stuff; his passion as a person superseded all of that. He was his own master, and he knew himself, and he was the person he answered to.”</p>
<p>More recently, in 2020, while she was doing promotion for HBO’s adaptation of Philip Roth’s <em>The Plot Against America, </em>a reporter asked Kazan to talk about her “family’s history during this period.” (The book is set in a counterfactual America during the lead-up to World War II.)</p>
<p>“Your grandfather — I don’t want to bring up hard times for you, I’m sorry,” the reporter said when Kazan asked what he was referring to. “You’re not bringing up hard times for me,” Kazan replied in a tone that was not without some edge. “You’re bringing up hard times for our country.” Then she gave an answer: “I have not wanted to weigh in on my family’s political history, partially because of the other people involved in my family who have prized their privacy over a public life,” she said. “So I’m not going to go into it.” At the time, she had just begun working on <em>East of Eden,</em> so she talked about that instead. “I will say that I thought a lot about how the history of our country affected my family’s history, what it meant for my grandfather as an immigrant to this country to have his Americanness tested, and the choice that he made from that,” Kazan said. She then evoked a phrase from the book, <em>thou mayest,</em> already borrowing from the novel’s moral center to help her unpack her own stuff.</p>
<p>It was the answer of someone in the process of rethinking things. Now, her previous decision to follow a code of silence is at odds with her desire to talk. “I feel like doing this job burned the bullshit out of me,” she says. “As a result, I feel like it’s much harder for me to erect the mask that I used to erect in interviews. That’s a wonderful thing for my life, and that’s not about this interview right now, but I’m figuring this out in real time in front of you.” And, after a long pause, she tells me about her grandfather. She understands now who Elia Kazan is to the world, famously and infamously, but it’s still hard for her to grapple with his notoriety: “Like, a family’s a system. And so I loved my grandfather within the system of our family. He’s a singular person in the world, and to me that’s all connected.”</p>
<p>To her, he’s Papou (Greek for “grandfather”). He was in his 70s when she was born. He’d stopped directing. He was just writing. “He really wanted to be a grandparent and really wanted to be present,” she says. “And even though he lived on the other side of the country, he was a very loving and present person when he was there.”</p>
<p>The memories of her grandfather that she feels comfortable sharing are fragments: the food, her grandparents’ coffee maker on the stove at their house in Connecticut. His charisma. She remembers that, as a child, it was easy to register his importance in a room. “I think I didn’t really have a sense of him as a famous person until I was much older. My early childhood memories of 2, 3, 4, I remember feeling even then that he had a lot of power.”</p>
<p>She opens the camera roll on her phone and shows me some photos: her tiny and walking confidently with her hand in his. “It’s very sweet. We went and got tomatoes from the garden,” she says. Then one of her reading <em>Gone With the Wind</em> at his kitchen table and another with him in a <em>She’s Gotta Have It </em>T-shirt; she’s still trying to find it among his belongings. When she was studying theater in college, she would bring him playbills from the shows she was in: “Those years are really precious to me.” He died in 2003, before she graduated.</p>
<p>“What I do is predicated on the way that he changed the American theater and the way that we think about acting,” she says. “It’s cosmic. I think for better or worse, I’ve always felt no need to compete with that. It’s too big. I’m like, <em>Okay, well, there’s that. What am I supposed to do about it? I can’t do anything about it.</em>”</p>
<p>But there were moments that made her aware of who he was to the world, beyond her. In middle school, a teacher asked if Elia Kazan was her grandfather. Benign enough, but she was so stunned she said “no.”</p>
<p>In 1999, he received an honorary Oscar for his work; she was 15, and the subject was unavoidable, she remembers. Many thought he deserved it, but just as many thought his betrayal was too deep. There were protests outside the ceremony and news stories with footage of hundreds of people holding up signs: KAZAN SNITCH, KAZAN — THE LINDA TRIPP OF THE ’50s. All of which Kazan saw. “A lot of schoolkids were coming up to me and being like, ‘Do you defend what your grandfather did?’ And also, this is so dumb, but I was in AP European History that year. You know what I mean? AP American History was the next year.” She didn’t really understand. She went to her parents for context. “My father was very factual and made it clear that the way my grandfather had felt about this had changed over the years. And that my feelings might change over time,” she says. “I felt incredibly protective toward him because he was my grandpa. And also because he was really frail in his life.”</p>
<p>At Yale, during her freshman year, she auditioned for several plays, including <em>The Crucible</em>. She expected to get a role as Pitchfork Carrier No. 4 but wound up with two lead offers. It’s hard to ignore the fact that she chose a role in a play widely understood as a critique of McCarthyism that was written by Arthur Miller, her grandfather’s former friend who disavowed the HUAC hearings. (And yes, she ended up playing Abigail, who falsely accuses others of witchcraft in order to deflect scrutiny.) A reporter from the <em>Yale Daily News</em> interviewed her under the pretense of writing a story about 9/11 but used the opportunity to ask Kazan about her grandfather. “I will never forgive her. It’s an unbelievably fucked-up thing to do,” Kazan says. “What I mostly remember was a feeling of great shame and that I had done something wrong.” She called her dad afterward in tears. She pauses. “But I made my own mark on that campus,” she says, referring to her work in the theater department. Kazan talks a lot about shame. How she’s trying to break free of it for herself through the life she’s created. And, more important, the career she’s created, which she feels is autonomous from her grandfather’s.</p>
<p>Her own feelings around him have changed with time, as her father said they might: “As an adult, I’ve made a real effort to educate myself. I think this country really failed my grandfather, and I think my grandfather really failed a lot of other people. And that’s a thing that I really understand more deeply now. I think growing up, becoming a more cognizant person, learning more about his history, moving through two Trump administrations, yes, my tolerance for his choices has gone down, but my empathy for him has remained.”</p>
<p>Kazan likes to do crafts while she works — in keeping with her whole bonnet-girl persona — and while she was deep in edits for <em>East of Eden,</em> she was also reworking a quilt. It’s from the 1800s, she explains. “And in the 1800s, one of the most popular quilting patterns was, quote, unquote, windmills. You can guess what those look like.” (I am forced to say “swastikas” for confirmation.)</p>
<p>Instead of throwing it out, she decided to sew on patches from other quilts until the windmills looked less like a hate crime. “I was just adjusting it for the moment we’re living in right now,” she says, holding out a metaphor to help me understand.</p>
<p><em>East of Eden</em> will arrive into a cultural climate that’s ready for it. The Hot Girls are already reading the book<em>. </em>You can imagine that somewhere, someone is advising Dua Lipa to select it for her book club. When Netflix announced the show, the teaser photographs of Pugh as Cathy, dressed in a menacing black lace dress and tiny sunglasses (how did Kazan know we’d be deep in a tiny-sunglasses moment?), sparked a frenzy. People will appreciate the faithful dialogue; they will love Hoon Lee as Lee. They might, as I did, long for more views and vistas, or they might be satisfied with New Zealand as a stand-in.</p>
<p>I ask Kazan how she hopes <em>timshel</em> will be interpreted by viewers. She isn’t sure. She made a whole show about what it means to her. “I’m kinda like, <em>I adapted this with so much care and love and curiosity. I’m really proud of it. If they don’t like what they see, they can read the book. It’s theirs.</em> Like, that belongs to them,” she says.</p>
<p>For the entire time she worked on the show, she clung tight to Steinbeck’s journals. She sent me photos of all of the passages she’d highlighted. Steinbeck on Cathy: “She is a fascinating and horrible person to me … I almost hesitate to put her down.”</p>
<p>And then one more, underlined in red pen: “They will forget I said she was bad. And they will hate her because while she is a monster, she is a little piece of the monster in all of us.”</p>
<p>Kazan sends a follow-up text: “This is a !!!! One.”</p>
