<p>Technically, it is now really fall. We got a nice solid rain in Portland to celebrate. This will, I am certain, be the regular fall fakeout; summery weather will return like it always does. But right now it’s damp and crisp and every time I look outside, I notice more trees changing color. (There’s a hike I’ve been trying to go on at just the right time for <em>years</em>, so I have to pay attention to these things. But I’d do that anyway.) If you can’t tell, I am one of those Fall People. We’ve become a cliche in recent years thanks to the internet, but at this point, the internet turns everything good into a cliche, so who cares? Let me at the pumpkin beer and the cozy sweaters and the foggy mornings! Just, you know, let them <em>last</em>. The thing about being a fan of a shoulder season is that sometimes the window is very small. You do your appreciating while you can, for lo, tomorrow it may be freezing. (Not just yet. But eventually.)</p>
<p>Fall is also simply the best time to sit inside in soft pants and read and watch things. It’s kind of a weird week this week, though! Weird movie choices! I can’t help but feel like this whole season is going to feel a little off and weird. At least until we get to November. But we’ll see, I guess. Pick out your favorite sweater, <a href="https://5calls.org/">call your reps</a>, hug your friends, and keep your socks dry.</p>
<h3><strong>To Retcon or Not to Retcon?</strong></h3>
<p>Well, yes, you <em>could</em> go see <em>Avengers: Endgame</em> again, and see how the Russo brothers and Marvel have decided to change a movie released seven years ago so that it better suits the story they’ve decided they’re telling now. You <em>could</em> do that. Or you could do literally anything else! You could see <em>Primetime</em>, in which Robert Pattinson plays what seems like a very smarmy guy from TV (I don’t know anything about the subject of this film); you could see <em>Forgotten Island</em>, which is an original animated film set in the Philippines in the 1990s, which honestly looks like piles of fun. If you live in select cities, you can see the documentary <em>Pulp: What Do You Do for an Encore?</em>, and then probably have “Common People” in your head for several days. There’s also a movie about Brad Pitt and a dog, if you are the kind of person who can watch Man and Dog movies (I am not! The dog always dies!). And all of <em>Party Down</em> is on Netflix! All of it! Including that later season I have yet to enjoy! My point is, there are so many new movies this weekend. Don’t get tricked into seeing the one you’ve already seen!</p>
<h3><strong>There Is Never a Bad Time to Read</strong> <strong><em>Pilgrim at Tinker Creek</em></strong></h3>
<p>When I’m not reading SFF, I am often, or at least sometimes, reading books about going outside. My origin story for that kind of book is Annie Dillard’s <em>Pilgrim at Tinker Creek</em>, the book for which then-28–year-old Dillard (who died earlier this month) won the Pulitzer Prize. I read this book in college and it blew my mind. I grew up on fantasy, and had read a few other books, and I had no framework for understanding that people could write about things like Dillard wrote about—about the seasons changing, and the plants and animals near her home. Nature, time, the world. Books were full of big things! Quests! Drama! And yet Dillard saw all the drama and beauty in one small corner of this world. I’ve been doling her books out to myself ever since, reading one every few years, trying not to run out. But there was so much I didn’t know about her, back then; I didn’t even know she taught writing to college students. It never occurred to me that a person could meet and learn from a writer like that. But Alexander Chee did just that, and wrote a fantastic essay about the experience: “<a href="https://themorningnews.org/annie-dillard-and-the-writing-life/">Annie Dillard and the Writing Life</a>.” If you don’t think a fifty-year-old book of nature writing is for you, you might at least read this piece.</p>
<h3><strong>But What If You Got the Paper a Day Early?</strong></h3>
<p>I need to start this by saying that, as far as I can tell, you can’t watch <em>Early Edition</em> on streaming anywhere. But I wanted to briefly talk about it anyway, because this show, with its ludicrous premise, apparently premiered on September 28, 1996. So it turns 30 on Monday! And as Kyle Chandler is presently quite visible playing Hal Jordan on <em>Lanterns</em>, it seems like a missed opportunity. Streamers, what are you doing? At any rate, when I first heard about this show, I kind of didn’t believe it was real. Most ’90s TV missed me (except <em>X-Files</em> and <em>Buffy</em>, basically, and various reruns of <em>Seinfeld</em> and <em>The Simpsons</em>). But everyone has the thing they remember Kyle Chandler from—<em>Friday Night Lights</em>, that time he got exploded on <em>Grey’s Anatomy</em>—and I have friends who <em>definitely</em> think of him as “the guy from <em>Early Edition</em>.” A show, as referenced above, in which one man gets the paper a day early. <em>The newspaper!</em> This is so quaint and adorable I can hardly stand it. There are ninety episodes of this show. In 2022, a <a href="https://reactormag.com/cbs-early-edition-reboot/">reboot</a> was announced, but it never happened. I feel like this is fine. Perhaps they could just put the original on streaming? It’s an anniversary! Let the peculiar yet charming-sounding television show celebrate!</p>
<h3><strong>Vampires Don’t Age, But</strong> <strong><em>Interview with the Vampire</em></strong> <strong>Is Turning 50</strong></h3>
<p>When I first read Anne Rice as a teen, I didn’t know anything about her except that she lived in New Orleans (on my one trip there, I wandered the Garden District, wondering where she lived). Over the years I’ve accumulated small details here and there, in interviews and articles. But none of them are as heartbreaking as the story her son, Christopher Rice, tells in a new piece in <em>The New York Times</em>. “<a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/23/books/review/anne-rice-interview-with-the-vampire-50th-anniversary.html?unlocked_article_code=1.DlE.KSLp.6hSAXybMlJeT&amp;smid=url-share">How Anne Rice Channeled Grief Into <em>Interview With the Vampire</em></a>” is a title absolutely designed to get my attention. But at first, the piece—adapted from the foreword of the book’s upcoming 50th anniversary edition—is about more than that. It’s about young Christopher, and about a gaggle of early <em>Vampire</em> cosplayers, and about the way that book was hardly a smash hit on publication.</p>
<p>But then it becomes about something else: Christopher’s sister, whose existence he didn’t know about for years of his life. It’s about loss and love and inspiration. It’s elegantly written, yet feels raw and heartbreaking. It makes me want to read a whole biography of Anne Rice. But for now, I’ll return to the Vampire Chronicles once more.</p>
