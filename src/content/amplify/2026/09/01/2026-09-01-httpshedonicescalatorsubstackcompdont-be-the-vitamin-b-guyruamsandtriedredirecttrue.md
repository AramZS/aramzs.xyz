<p>When I was eleven years old, I watched my favorite YouTuber surgically implant a magnet into his finger.</p>
<p>In the <a href="https://archive.org/details/CodysLab_me/20140227-Diy+Magnetic+Implant.mp4">since-deleted video</a>, beloved mad scientist <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cody_Reeder">Cody Reeder</a> covered a neodymium magnet with gold using a homemade electroplating rig. Then he cut open his finger, inserted the magnet, and sutured the wound closed with horsehair he had taken from his own horse.</p>

<p>The footage is an uncomfortable watch for medical professionals and squeamish laymen alike. The scalpel was chipped, there was no anesthetic, and at one point, Cody dips a mechanical pencil in alcohol and uses its tip to push the magnet deeper into the incision. A sense that wasn’t even human. Like a fish learning about water, I woke to the injustice of my biological limitations, and since then, I’ve considered myself an aspiring biohacker.<a data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" href="#footnote-2" id="footnote-anchor-2"><sup> [2] </sup></a></p>

<p>With an emphasis on the “meat.” I’ve never been anywhere that made me so acutely aware of the fact that I am made of it.<a data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" href="#footnote-3" id="footnote-anchor-3"><sup> [3] </sup></a> It is one thing to browse pictures and videos of unusual body mods, another to meet a biohacker in the altered flesh and see the LEDs shining from under her skin or from the iris of his <a href="https://www.instagram.com/bsmachinist/">prosthetic eye</a>.<a data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" href="#footnote-4" id="footnote-anchor-4"><sup> [4] </sup></a></p>
<p>On the event grounds was a lab with an attached operating room - picture electronics in varying states of disassembly, bottles of chemicals labeled with Sharpie, and a modified dental chair, all smelling faintly of antiseptic - referred to by one attendee as the ripperdoc clinic. Of the many activities hosted in that space, the most pedestrian were the talks, typically tutorials for useful skills or presentations on personal projects. One such presentation turned out to be extremely memorable for all the wrong reasons.</p>
<h2>The tale of the vitamin B guy</h2>
<p>The vitamin B guy was an older gentleman, somewhat unkempt and wholly unremarkable. At a place like Grindfest, a person like him might work as a straight-laced government contractor securing municipal systems from cyberattacks, or be a minor celebrity in the BDSM scene for his hook suspension performances, or spend his spare time building terrifying contraptions from disassembled microwaves, or all of the above. This particular individual was presenting a slideshow on the power of vitamin B supplements.</p>
<p>He started the talk with a classic list of vague health complaints - fatigue, brain fog, and the like - and followed up with an equally classic amateur-pharmacologist screed on the necessity of B12 supplements in individuals with certain genetic variants,<a data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" href="#footnote-5" id="footnote-anchor-5"><sup> [5] </sup></a> to which he attributed all of his problems. He then claimed that oral supplements were inadequate, and that intramuscular injections were required, and then proceeded to attribute increasingly incredible benefits to B12. In one alarming anecdote, he said he woke up in the middle of the night in a cold sweat, fumbled for his vitamin shot, and felt instant relief.</p>
<p><em>Holy shit</em>, I thought. <em>The placebo effect is a hell of a drug.</em></p>
<p>At this point, he retrieved a giant, crusty, half-empty bottle of injectable B12. He’s been taking a veterinary formulation to save costs, he said. If it’s good enough for racehorses, it’s good enough for him.</p>

<p>Then he pulled out a handful of needles and syringes, and invited the audience to get an injection on the spot.</p>
<p>It’s perfectly safe, he said, showing off a scar on his leg. He only got an infection once.</p>
<p>An organizer enthusiastically remarked that this was a great opportunity to practice injection technique, and demonstrated on a volunteer. Several attendees lined up to get their B12 shots, placing a remarkable amount of trust in the vitamin B guy’s ability to keep his bottle of horse vitamins sterile for however many months or years he had been drawing from it.<a data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" href="#footnote-6" id="footnote-anchor-6"><sup> [6] </sup></a> The more credible of the B12 recipients reported feeling energized immediately after the shot. I did not get an injection.</p>
<p>During Q&amp;A, I asked the vitamin B guy if he had ever done a blinded test of the injections, to ensure they worked. He didn’t need to, he said, he was certain it worked.</p>
<p>I asked if he had ever screened himself for the genetic variants he blamed his symptoms on. He hadn’t. I asked if he had ever checked his B12 levels.</p>
<p>He hadn’t.</p>
<h2>Lessons for biohackers</h2>
<p>At Grindfest, I found an undercurrent of contempt for the perceived appropriation of the term “biohacker” by health and wellness gurus. Attendees floated alternate terms, such as “biopunk”, or the OG “grinder,”<a data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" href="#footnote-7" id="footnote-anchor-7"><sup> [7] </sup></a> in an effort to distinguish themselves from the rich, corporate normies seeking to rebrand their turmeric supplements and semaglutide as edgy and transgressive. Having attended biohacking events by the latter type, I can sympathize.<a data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" href="#footnote-8" id="footnote-anchor-8"><sup> [8] </sup></a> But there’s more overlap between the two crowds than some grinders would like to admit. <a href="https://www.bryanjohnson.com/">Bryan Johnson</a> comes to mind, the wannabe immortal weird and wealthy enough to <a href="https://andykong.org/blog/vitalia1/">make his presence felt at Vitalia</a>, alongside the Augmentation Lab run by Grindfest host Cassox. So does the vitamin B guy.</p>
<p>What makes the wellness gurus derisible is not their lack of appetite for unusual body mods,<a data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" href="#footnote-9" id="footnote-anchor-9"><sup> [9] </sup></a> but their poor scientific literacy and uncritical embrace of woo. Had vitamin B guy simply been taking his supplements on his own, who cares if it’s placebo? But once someone is presenting their ideas in public, they should aim for a higher level of rigor: gather better evidence whenever they can, and disclaim what uncertainty remains. Vitamin B guy could’ve had his genome sequenced, could’ve gotten his B12 levels tested, could’ve done a blind comparison of his injections with saline (even just one test would provide decent evidence on the immediate effects he reported), and at the very least, he could’ve proactively discussed the implications of not having done these things. The biohacking community at Grindfest could’ve been more skeptical of his claims.</p>
<p>Part of the motivation for my stance is practical. The potential harms of overstating the benefits of B12 injections may not be too high, but many interventions promoted among biohackers are much more dangerous.<a data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" href="#footnote-10" id="footnote-anchor-10"><sup> [10] </sup></a> Mostly, though, I just find this embarrassing.</p>
<p>Do you wanna promote an experimental peptide? Think you’ve cured your chronic illness? Have an opinion on a medical controversy? Ask yourself:</p>
<ul><li><p>Is there anything you can do to further test your claim? Be ambitious. It’s not out of the picture to recruit 20 friends<a data-component-name="FootnoteAnchorToDOM" href="#footnote-11" id="footnote-anchor-11"><sup> [11] </sup></a> to run a blinded trial on a research drug; even if you’re only experimenting on yourself, you can spend a few months gathering a respectable number of data points. If your claim relies on facts that could be verified through a commercially available blood test, genome sequencing, etc., then go do it.</p></li><li><p>Is your degree of confidence justified? Can you think of alternate explanations for your observations, or discoveries that could prove you wrong? Disclose where your knowledge falls short. If all you want to do is share an anecdote and some wild speculations, be honest about it and don’t overstate your position.</p></li><li><p>If you are contradicting expert consensus: do you have an explanation for why the mainstream viewpoint is wrong? That is, why haven’t all the smart people in a position to correct this error noticed it? <p>I’m a huge fan of weird people doing weird things. I want to live in a world with mad scientists implanting magnets into their own fingers to reclaim their lost superpowers - though maybe we can promote better surgical techniques. I want to support the self-styled biohackers, citizen scientists, and DIY medicine enthusiasts out there, but some of you people are really hard to support. If you want to do serious science, you have to take yourself seriously. We can do better.</p>
