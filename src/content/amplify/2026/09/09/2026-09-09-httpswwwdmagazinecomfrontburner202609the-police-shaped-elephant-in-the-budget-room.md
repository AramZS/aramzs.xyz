<p>As we approach the end of this scorching summer, the Dallas City Council is nearing the end of a heated budget season. In April, Dallas City Manager Kimberly Bizor Tolbert effectively said the city is <a href="https://www.dmagazine.com/micropost/dallas-is-almost-broke/">almost broke</a>, facing a $30 million budget shortfall and a projected deficit of $51 million for the upcoming fiscal year. The key reasons: police and fire overtime, declining sales tax revenue, and ballooning employee health insurance costs (chief among them: <a href="https://www.dallasobserver.com/news/after-layoffs-furloughs-dallas-looks-to-cut-employee-healthcare-40706304/">GLP-1 drugs</a>).</p>
<p>The Council is still in the process of hammering out the final details, but the broad strokes are clear. To balance the budget without increasing property taxes, the city furloughed thousands of employees and is now poised to make layoffs and cuts across a number of departments, including the Dallas Public Library, the Office of Environmental Quality and Sustainability, Municipal Courts and Detention Services, and Emergency Management and Crisis Response. Other proposed changes fall heavily on city employees, including <a href="https://www.dallasnews.com/news/dallas-local-government/article/dallas-budget-healthcare-22397051.php">eliminating</a> the preferred provider organization (PPO) plan that the majority of city staff use. Despite these cuts, the city’s <a href="https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/TXDALLAS/bulletins/424044e">general fund budget</a> will increase, “with nearly all of the growth directed to the Dallas Police Department and Dallas Fire-Rescue budgets.”</p>
<p>The <em>Dallas Morning News </em>editorial board has called for cuts big and small, <a href="https://www.dallasnews.com/opinion/editorials/article/dallas-budget-city-council-spending-22388777.php">praising</a> some aspects of the proposed budget while also <a href="https://www.dallasnews.com/opinion/editorials/article/dallas-eviction-pools-library-budget-22418182.php">castigating</a> City Council for other decisions, such as preserving the $700,000 budget for six community pools. The elephant in the room is the Dallas Police Department (DPD) budget, which consumes more than 40 percent of the city’s general fund and will see its funding increase by $65 million to a total of $823 million. But due to a number of structural factors, the creeping police budget has largely remained out of the conversation.</p>
<p>“We can’t decrease our police budget,” Councilmember Paula Blackmon told <em>D Magazine</em>. “Then we have the pension, which we also have no ability to raise revenue to cover. And our revenue gains are about as much as our increase for DPD. We live in a police state, a police city.”</p>
<p>A quick civics refresher to help you understand what Blackmon means. Here are the four governmental realities that make the cops’ budget untouchable.</p>
<p>First, due to a state law passed in 2021, cities can only cut their police department budgets in proportion to overall reductions in the municipal budget. If a city’s budget remains flat or grows, the police budget cannot be cut. Defying this law can result in the state taking sales tax revenue and freezing property tax increases.</p>
<p>Second, due to <a href="https://www.dmagazine.com/?p=984959">Proposition U</a>, which was passed in 2024, the city of Dallas must increase police salaries and its number of police officers to 4,000—even if DPD Chief Daniel Comeaux <a href="https://www.keranews.org/government/2026-08-11/dallas-police-fire-funds-to-increase-by-83m-other-departments-see-budget-cuts-city-council-proposition-u">thinks that number isn’t necessary</a>. Another thing Proposition U does: it devotes 50 percent of all year-over-year unencumbered revenue growth toward the police and fire pension, which has been <a href="https://www.dmagazine.com/frontburner/2024/06/the-dallas-police-and-fire-pension-just-isnt-making-its-money-go-far-enough/">insolvent for nearly a decade</a>. Comeaux’s current plan is to reach 3,800 officers by 2028. Citing Proposition U, the Dallas Police Association <a href="https://www.nbcdfw.com/news/local/dallas-police-fire-associations-express-concern-with-city-amid-contract-negotiations/4053569/">has argued</a> that current contract negotiations and pay proposals fail to satisfy Proposition U’s mandate to make Dallas’ starting salaries rank among the top five in the region.</p>
<p>Third, due to Proposition S, Dallas residents <a href="https://www.dmagazine.com/micropost/prop-s-may-finally-bite-dallas-in-the-rear/">can sue the city</a> for not following its own laws, such as Proposition U, and the city must forfeit its sovereign immunity in such cases. Both Propositions U and S were put forward by <a href="https://www.dmagazine.com/frontburner/2024/10/monty-bennett-and-the-shadowy-dallas-hero-crew/">Dallas HERO</a>, a nonprofit principally backed by hotelier and Republican megadonor Montgomery J. Bennett. The former proposition passed by a margin of less than 1 percent, the latter by nearly 10.</p>
<p>Attorney General Ken Paxton, to whom Bennett has donated $410,000 since 2015, <a href="https://www.dmagazine.com/frontburner/2026/03/ken-paxton-is-suing-dallas-over-police-funding-city-answers/">sued</a> the city of Dallas in March for allegedly not following Proposition U’s police-funding requirements. Paxton was joined in the suit by two Dallas residents who are represented by an attorney who previously worked with Dallas HERO. Future lawsuits could seek to force DPD to reach 4,000 officers sooner and, if successful, would impose additional costs to recruit, train, equip, and create facilities for new officers.</p>

<p>And fourth, due to state laws that <a href="https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/?tab=1&amp;code=TX&amp;chapter=TX.321&amp;artSec=">limit local sales taxes</a> and <a href="https://www.houstonpublicmedia.org/articles/news/texas/2019/06/12/336451/gov-greg-abbott-signs-bill-designed-to-limit-property-tax-growth/">cap municipal increases in property taxes</a> without voter approval, cities have few choices outside of bond packages when it comes to raising cash to cover major budgetary gaps. Increasing property taxes is a <a href="https://www.texastribune.org/2025/11/06/texas-property-tax-cuts-election-2025/">politically unpopular move</a>; the proposed Dallas city budget reflects this by seeking to reduce the rate by 1 cent. Voters approved a $1.25 billion infrastructure bond in 2024, and now the city is asking voters to approve another <a href="https://lakewood.advocatemag.com/dallas-voters-will-see-a-400m-public-safety-bond-on-nov-3/">$443 million bond</a> in November to fund public safety initiatives, including new law enforcement training centers.</p>
<blockquote><p>“This is the coolest summer that we’ll have for the rest of our lives. This is the cheapest budget, too, because the pension payments are going to go up.” —Paula Blackmon</p></blockquote>
<p>Taken together, these laws create a ratcheting effect on the police budget, pushing it higher while simultaneously tying the city’s hands in terms of revenue generation. When the budget crunches, the pressure primarily falls on other city services, even if there are clear inefficiencies within the police department. (DPD processes and tracks overtime costs on paper; millions of dollars in overtime have been <a href="https://www.dmagazine.com/frontburner/2021/09/what-is-the-real-cost-of-dallas-police-overtime/">unaccounted</a> for in past audits; and the overtime budget is routinely exceeded.) Certainly, there are numerous areas of potential savings and opportunities to trim fat across the city budget, but the fattest slice of the pie is largely off limits.</p>
<p>“In fire, they have better systems in place for their controls,” Blackmon said. “However, they don’t in DPD, and we don’t dare question it.”</p>
<p>In August, Councilmember Paul Ridley remarked that the city could face another budget crunch in the future as costs continue to exceed revenues. Blackmon, too, believes that future budget seasons will require City Council to make increasingly difficult choices, which state law, Prop U, and Prop S make all the more challenging.</p>
<p>“Think of it like this,” Blackmon said. “This is the coolest summer that we’ll have for the rest of our lives. This is the cheapest budget, too, because the pension payments are going to go up. It’s going to get real intense pretty fast.”</p>
