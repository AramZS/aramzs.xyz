<p>If you only read what critics wrote about the new <em>Wolverine</em> videogame, you might think it was one of the biggest flops of the year. They largely describe the combat as repetitive or<a href="https://www.ign.com/articles/marvels-wolverine-review"> “one-note.”</a> Its story<a href="https://giantbomb.com/reviews/marvels-wolverine-review"> “frequently falls apart”</a>. One critic even recalls putting his fist through someone’s “eyes, face, heart” and <a href="https://www.eurogamer.net/marvels-wolverine-review">“feeling absolutely nothing.”</a></p>
<p>Would you believe that <em>Wolverine</em> has a 79/100 average review score on OpenCritic?</p>

<p>Now, when it comes down to the score itself, this is easy to explain. There are over 70 reviews in the aggregation, many of which rate the game positively. There are even a few perfect scores.</p>
<p>What’s strange to me is how often the positive scores belong to reviews that sound pretty negative. I’ve been reading so much about boredom and confusion and disappointment, but then also landing on a number that seems to describe a pretty good videogame. I don’t particularly care to litigate the numbered score of any or all reviews, but I am interested in what this exposes about what values are being privileged among the critical consensus.</p>
<p>Occasionally, the reviews themselves demonstrate what I am talking about. After spending paragraphs describing Wolverine’s combat as lacking weight, challenge, and eventually leaving him so disengaged that he lowered enemy health just to end fights faster, GameSpot’s Tamoor Hussain does clarify: “It may be hard to believe but all of that doesn’t mean the combat is bad. It is perfectly functional.”</p>
<p>Perfectly functional. As opposed to bad!</p>
<p>So often I find that functionality has the power to rescue something from being bad even after the critic has described the experience as bad.</p>
<p>As someone that plays a lot of videogames, I think it’s fairly easy to understand where this perspective comes from. Anyone looking at <em>Wolverine</em> can suss out that there is something of quality here. There’s a cinematic flair to everything happening on screen. The amount of polish Insomniac has applied to every fine or brutal detail seems guaranteed to provoke a response—whether it’s the genuine shock of watching Wolverine jam a claw through someone’s skull or the delicate handling of a quiet conversation with Jean.</p>
<p>But lately I am finding these qualities less and less impressive.</p>
<p>I am almost entirely uninterested in how well an object has been made and instead I want to know what it becomes when we experience it.</p>
<p>Allow me to steal a concept from Roman Ingarden’s <em><a href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/2105148">Aesthetic Experience and Aesthetic Object</a>.</em></p>
<p>Ingarden separates art into two concepts: the work of art itself and what he calls the “aesthetic object.” The work is the literal thing that has been made. The aesthetic object is then formed through a process called the aesthetic experience, which is what the work becomes in our experience of it. Ingarden writes that “The essential mistake” is to think that the object of aesthetic experience is “identical with an element of the real world and the object of our activities or cognition.”</p>
<p>This is not so hard to understand when we use tangible examples: Wolverine is the work. Playing Wolverine is the aesthetic experience. What Wolverine becomes through your experience is the aesthetic object. Easy!</p>
<p>This explains why two people can play the exact same game and come away with vastly different impressions. I am not trying to elaborately clarify that “all art is subjective,” but I do think it is worth recognizing that saying animations are detailed and controls are responsive and performances are good and combat is satisfying are not quite the same as describing the aesthetic experience those qualities produce. These are qualities anyone can identify in the work, often without even touching the game in question, but their presence does not determine what the work becomes when someone experiences it.</p>
<p>In IGN’s review of Wolverine, Simon Cardy describes the combat as initially satisfying, but says that “halfway through Wolverine’s 15-hour string of missions, I felt like I’d had my fill.” Eventually, all that violence becomes a “one-note dance.”</p>
<p>This is absolutely a strong description of an aesthetic experience. I completely understand what Cardy is communicating and, again, as someone who plays a lot of videogames, it’s an extremely familiar feeling.</p>
<p>But I wonder what happens if “it wears thin” is the beginning of criticism instead of the verdict.</p>
<p>Does it mean anything at all that <em>Marvel’s Wolverine </em>has produced an experience in which violence becomes routine?</p>
<p>I am not an expert on Wolverine by any stretch, but I can be fairly certain this is a game about a character frequently defined by finding himself in situations where he must regenerate his own body and tear apart the bodies of others. According to Cardy and many other critics, the violence starts exciting, but eventually becomes routine.</p>
<p>I am curious about this!</p>
<p>Does the player’s sense that violence is a chore begin to resemble Logan’s? Does his ability to endlessly regenerate make all of this violence feel as if it is without consequence? Does everything about the violence eventually give way to a feeling of monotonous and consumable spectacle?</p>

<p>Unfortunately, I have no idea. At the time of writing, I will be starting the game tomorrow! My actual point is just that these are the kinds of critical questions produced by an aesthetic experience. While I understand the function of recognizing that the combat becomes repetitive, I find myself wanting an interpretation that asks what meaning is generated from that experience.</p>
<p>It may also be the case that I think videogame criticism has developed a habit of stopping at recognition and resisting interpretation. Though, how could one ever paint with such a wide brush?</p>
<p>Fortunately, Ingarden was willing to paint with an enormous brush in 1961.</p>
<blockquote><p>“People who have much to do with works of art . . . develop a peculiar routine to take notice of various derivative details . . . [and] infer . . . whether a given work of art may or may not lead to a valuable aesthetic object.”</p></blockquote>
<p>Basically, the more often one plays and critically evaluates videogames, the more able one is going to be at recognizing the qualities that look like they should produce a high-quality experience.</p>
<p>I think I recognize the “peculiar routine” when I read <a href="https://gameinformer.com/review/marvels-wolverine/relentless-momentum">Game Informer’s 8.5/10 review for </a><em><a href="https://gameinformer.com/review/marvels-wolverine/relentless-momentum">Wolverine</a></em>. The linear structure “doesn’t always land,” but the fights “will not disappoint.” The quieter sections are “on rails, with little to no choice,” but character interactions are “excellent.” Though, the story becomes confusing and fails to provide “a complete, satisfying resolution,” but the battles are “deeply satisfying and smartly balanced,” even though we admit the game just ends “before you might tire” of its combat and offers little motivation for replay, but it is “beautiful to look at and listen to” and “plays like a dream.”</p>
<p>There’s a critical arithmetic you can see playing out here if you are familiar enough with shorthand in the videogame space. Linear design is obviously a negative, while responsiveness and balance in combat are a positive. A high-fidelity, expensive-looking game is a positive, while limited replayability is a negative.</p>
<p>Ingarden argues that the judgments of professional critics are often an “indirect estimation not of the value of an aesthetic object itself, but only of a work of art as a means” that might produce a valuable aesthetic object. Even if these critics are correct in their evaluation of the work, he argues, they may ultimately know or say “very little about the real contents of the corresponding aesthetic objects.” A review can add up the parts. An aesthetic experience is what they become together.</p>
<p>I say all of this just to swing us back to “perfectly functional.”</p>
<p>When Ingarden refers to the work of art as a “means,” that sounds a lot like functionality to me.</p>
<p>In Hussain’s review he told us a story that makes me believe the combat is bad. It became a chore! He literally lowered enemy health because it was so painful to be stuck in combat encounters for extended periods of time. But then, as if to protect the combat system from the aesthetic experience he had with it, he clarifies that it’s “perfectly functional.” Everything he literally experienced “doesn’t mean the combat is bad.”</p>
<p>So, I guess my next question is, is it functional?</p>
<p>If the function of <em>Wolverine’s </em>combat is to produce something engaging, then surely if you are so disengaged that you would rather skip it that must mean something. Or, is our standard, in this case, that the buttons literally work and it looks like games we have been trained to recognize as visually appealing.</p>
<p>I think I could have told you the work was going to pass inspection after watching the first trailer, but I want to know what happens when you play it.</p>
<p>My interest is increasingly fleeting in the kind of game criticism focused on witnessing for the sake of inspection above all else. I keep encountering critical terms to which I have become a stranger. So many videogames are polished and beautiful and varied and smooth and well-paced. The writing is good and the gameplay is tight. It’s not that these words are useless, but accumulated together they can begin to remove the critic from criticism. They describe qualities of the object in such an interchangeable way. Surely, I will encounter some form of media that tells me how many enemy types are in the game. I don’t need the critic to count just because they have the privilege of telling me first. I need the critic because they are the only part in the review that I won’t eventually witness for myself. They were there! I want them to take me along.</p>
<p>Ingarden argues that we return to an aesthetic experience to “analyze and to rationalize” it. The goal is to figure out what the experience actually gave us. The experience itself, while interesting, is only the beginning of interpretation. I don’t just want to know what was in the game. I want to know what happened between the game and the critic.</p>
<p>Otherwise…</p>
<p><em>It’s not that great, but the buttons work.</em></p>
<p>8/10.</p>
