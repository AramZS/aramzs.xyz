title: How President Trump Broke the Kennedy Center
---
<p>A storm brought down a five-foot piece of ceiling plaster at the John F. Kennedy Center for the Performing Arts this month and its leadership sprang into action. One thing they did not do was repair the damage.</p><p>Instead staff members left the mess on the carpet for nearly two weeks, posting photos on social media with <a href="https://x.com/romadaravi/status/2096248945760444420">commentary like</a> “the place is falling apart and @realDonaldTrump wants to fix it.” They measured plaster shards and mapped the “debris field” like investigators after a plane crash, for a 55-page briefing for the center’s board about the building’s decay. No one was injured but Mr. Trump claimed online that a security guard had <a href="https://truthsocial.com/@realDonaldTrump/posts/117275843063089895">narrowly avoided “certain death.”</a> Less than two hours later, his allies on the board voted to close the center, citing safety concerns.</p><p>Mr. Trump’s threats this past week suggest that saving face, not lives, better explains why he shuttered the Kennedy Center on Tuesday for what officials said <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/17/arts/music/kennedy-center-closure-emergency-hearing.html">would be a temporary closur</a>e. </p><p>The president, who is the center’s chairman, first announced in February that <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/02/01/us/politics/trump-kennedy-center.html">it would close for two years of renovations</a>. Democratic lawmakers on the board said it was an excuse to avoid the humiliation of plummeting ticket sales and fleeing artists. Since then Mr. Trump has <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/13/arts/design/trump-kennedy-center-bankruptcy-closure.html">fought to add his name to the memorial</a> to a slain president, drawing multiple court injunctions. When a judge denied him again last week, the president made his priority clear. He decreed that the closing would take place immediately but that the renovation would not begin until courts — potentially the Supreme Court — allow his name on the building front.</p><p>Then, as heavy construction fencing went up around the building, he went further. “The Trump administration should certainly have recognition,” he said, arguing that his name on the center would help lift it out of physical and financial turmoil. “Because frankly if we don’t do that it’s going to close, it’ll end up being ripped down.”</p><p>On Friday night, <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/18/us/politics/kennedy-center-protest.html">a large crowd of demonstrators locked arms</a> to form a human chain around the building in protest of Mr. Trump’s efforts to close the center amid fears that he might ultimately bulldoze it.</p><figure><img alt="A crowd of people holding signs protesting outside the Kennedy Center." src="https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/09/21/us/dc-kennedy-politics-88-PRINT-SUB/dc-kennedy-politics-88-articleLarge.jpg"/><figcaption> Demetrius Freeman for The New York Times</figcaption></figure><figure><img alt="President Trump, wearing a red baseball hat, looking at a piece of poster board and seen through the window portal of Air Force Once." src="https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/09/17/multimedia/dc-kennedy-politics-11-klfm/dc-kennedy-politics-11-klfm-articleLarge.jpg"/><figcaption> Kenny Holston/The New York Times</figcaption></figure><p><em>Hundreds of demonstrators gathered outside the Kennedy Center on Friday to protest President Trump’s efforts to close and remake the institution, two nights after the president was photographed reviewing a poster board on Air Force One that appeared to show a potential demolition plan.</em></p><p>The closure is the apotheosis of the wreckage and chaos created in the less than two years since Mr. Trump’s takeover of the center, once a bipartisan global model of public access to the arts. Internal emails and documents obtained by The New York Times demonstrate the political capture of the institution by the whims of Mr. Trump, who at the start of his second term <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2025/02/12/arts/music/trump-kennedy-center-chairman.html">installed a board that made him chairman</a>. Audiences and donors fled, and its finances cratered.</p><p>The center’s first Trump-appointed director, Richard Grenell, pursued a plan, not previously reported, to replace works in the center’s art collection, including gifts from foreign nations honoring Kennedy, with art selected by Trump patrons. “You can definitely start sending stuff back to the countries who donated it now,” Mr. Grenell said in an email to Josef Palermo, a curator he hired. “Figure out everything you don’t want and just send it back A.S.A.P.”</p><p><strong>From:</strong> Richard Grenell<br/><strong>Sent:</strong> Monday, Oct. 20, 2025 8:12 a.m.<br/><strong>To:</strong> Palermo, Josef; Floca, Matt<br/><strong>Cc:</strong> Loughery, Rick; Meade, Nick; Floca, Matt<br/><strong>Subject:</strong> Re: [EXTERNAL]: Art inside KenCen<br/><br/>You can definitely start sending stuff back to the countries who donated it now. Figure out everything you don’t want and just send it back ASAP.<br/><br/>ric</p><p>Conservative groups and friends of the president <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2025/11/20/us/politics/democrats-kennedy-center-investigate.html">staged events for free or at steep discounts,</a> including a conference on “ending Christian persecution.” The center threw parties and dedicated rooms to Trump donors and political allies.</p><p>Workers <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/07/11/arts/design/kennedy-center-trump-renovations.html">renovated Mr. Trump’s three theater boxes in the center</a>, installing gold plumbing fixtures and tearing up newly laid tile whose color was not to the liking of the White House. In one email, a Kennedy Center staff member, Lisa Dale, alerted colleagues to Mr. Trump’s demand for a certain type of tree on campus: “Our Chairman loves maple trees and is insisting we plant maple trees from a very specific Grove by a very specific gardener. :) I actually love this about him!”</p><p>Mr. Trump and center officials blame past leadership for the maintenance and financial problems. Despite recent removals of art from the campus and from public display, they say there is no active plan to dump the collection.</p><figure><img alt="The lobby area outside the presidential box with red plush chairs and gold decor including a seating area, light fixture and framed photographs of the Mr. Trump." src="https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/09/17/multimedia/dc-kennedy-politics-2-lvkh/dc-kennedy-politics-2-lvkh-articleLarge.jpg"/><figcaption>The lobby area outside the presidential box in July. Allison Robbert for The New York Times</figcaption></figure><p>“Our nonpartisan approach is evident by the consistent practice of welcoming everyone and continued invitations to bipartisan members of Congress and their staff for briefings, tours and our programming,” a spokeswoman, <a href="https://www.instagram.com/officialtkc/p/DdWv1fPCos6/">Roma Daravi</a>, said in a statement on Friday. (Hours later she lost her job in a round of layoffs, one of several this year.) On Wednesday she told Real America’s Voice, a pro-Trump television network, that Democratic critics “are knowingly stopping any work that is going to save this institution because they don’t want President Trump to get the credit.”</p><p>Democrats scoffed. “It is bonkers,” Representative Chellie Pingree of Maine, the top Democrat on the House appropriations subcommittee that oversees funding for the Kennedy Center building, said in an interview. “The No. 1 problem the Kennedy Center has had is President Trump.”</p><h2>Five Bipartisan Decades</h2><figure><img alt="President John F. Kennedy, far left, in a suit and tie being shown a model of the Kennedy Center by two other men." src="https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/09/17/us/DC-KENNEDY-POLITICS-3-copy/DC-KENNEDY-POLITICS-3-articleLarge.jpg"/><figcaption>President John F. Kennedy reviewing a model of the Kennedy Center in 1963. National Archive, via Getty Images</figcaption></figure><p>It was first known as the National Cultural Center, conceived in the 1950s by a Republican president, Dwight D. Eisenhower, and then championed by Kennedy, a Democrat, as an artistic oasis for all. The two presidents built public support and raised money for the center, and their wives were honorary co-chairs of its board.</p><p>After Kennedy’s assassination in 1963, Congress passed a law that named the center for him and required it to operate as a living memorial. The governing framework that developed aimed to insulate the institution from partisanship. The building would be maintained with federal money, and its arts programming paid for by private donors and ticket sales. Board members included legislators from both parties and presidential appointees whose six-year terms overlapped administrations.</p><figure><img alt="Clouds are reflected on a marble wall inside the Kennedy Center that displays a list of names of chairmen including Mr. Trump." src="https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/09/17/multimedia/dc-kennedy-politics-9-hbzf/dc-kennedy-politics-9-hbzf-articleLarge.jpg"/><figcaption> Alex Kent/The New York Times</figcaption></figure><figure><img alt="The bonze bust of Mr. Kennedy atop a pedestal inside the lobby of the Kennedy Center." src="https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/09/17/multimedia/dc-kennedy-politics-8-qbfh/dc-kennedy-politics-8-qbfh-articleLarge.jpg"/><figcaption> Alex Kent/The New York Times</figcaption></figure><p><em>Mr. Trump is the first sitting president to hold the role of Kennedy Center chairman.</em></p><p>For five decades, legislators of both parties attended events like the Kennedy Center Honors, the center’s biggest night of the year. The Supreme Court justices Ruth Bader Ginsburg and Antonin Scalia sat together at the opera. A benefit concert after the Sept. 11, 2001, terrorist attacks included tributes from Laura Bush, the first lady at the time, and Senator Ted Kennedy, Democrat of Massachusetts.</p><p>“You had a whole bunch of Republicans and you had a whole bunch of Democrats and everyone was just trying to make art and arts education,” said Michael Kaiser, who ran the center from 2001 to 2014.</p><p>Mr. Trump, snubbed by many in the liberal-leaning arts world, was a no-show at center events for most of his first term. But less than a month into his second term, he purged the Kennedy Center board of Biden-era appointees and installed allies. They made him chairman, the first time a sitting president has served in that role.</p><h2>‘We’re Returning the Art’</h2><figure><img alt='A reflection of cranes lifting pieces of the sculpture, "Blue" into the air as it is disassembled on the grounds of the Kennedy Center.' src="https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/09/02/multimedia/dc-kennedy-art-1-flbh/dc-kennedy-art-1-flbh-articleLarge.jpg"/><figcaption>Workers disassembled the sculpture “Blue,” by Joel Shapiro, outside the Kennedy Center this month. The Joel Shapiro and Ellen Phelan Foundation/Artists Rights Society (ARS), New York; Photograph by Salwan Georges for The New York Times</figcaption></figure><p>Mr. Trump <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2025/02/10/arts/music/trump-kennedy-center-board-removed.html">brought on Mr. Grenell</a>, his pugilistic former ambassador to Germany, who stocked the center’s upper ranks with partisans. Internal emails and documents — including some from the office of Senator Sheldon Whitehouse of Rhode Island, the ranking Democrat on a Senate committee overseeing the center — show that politics shaped Mr. Grenell’s effort to replace the center’s art collection.</p><p>A proposal circulated by Kennedy Center officials late last year made it clear on its first page that “de-accession” of artwork “may include disposal via the following means: return to country of origin, transfer or exchange with another institution, public sale or destruction.”</p><p><a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/03/13/arts/kennedy-center-richard-grenell.html">Mr. Grenell left the center in March</a>, and Mr. Palermo, the curator, was laid off the same month. In an <a href="https://www.theatlantic.com/culture/2026/04/inside-kennedy-center-shutdown-drama/686801/">Atlantic article</a> published in April, Mr. Palermo described himself as opposed to the plan to purge the art collection. But the documents portray him as an eager participant who crafted a plan to recruit Trump-friendly philanthropists to a new “Collectors Council.”</p><figure><img alt="Richard Grenell in suit and tie with hands folded in front of a flag." src="https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/09/02/multimedia/dc-kennedy-art-3-bfzg/dc-kennedy-art-3-bfzg-articleLarge.jpg"/><figcaption>Richard Grenell, left, the Kennedy Center’s Trump-appointed chief, directed Josef Palermo, who was hired by Mr. Grenell as the center’s visual arts curator, to remove artwork in and on the grounds of the center to clear the way for a new permanent collection overseen by a “Collectors’ Council” that will help select art with “broad public appeal…rooted in shared American values.” Doug Mills/The New York Times</figcaption></figure><figure><img alt="Josef Palermo wearing glasses and a suit and tie in front of a blue backdrop." src="https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/09/02/us/dc-kennedy-art-2/dc-kennedy-art-2-articleLarge.jpg"/><figcaption> Taylor Hill/WireImage, via Getty Images</figcaption></figure><p><em>Richard Grenell, left, directed Josef Palermo to remove artwork in and on the grounds of the center to clear the way for a new collection.</em></p><p>A memo about the proposed council described some of its functions as de-accessioning “outdated or inappropriate artworks,” and replacing them with works that reflect “shared American values.” Donors would pay up to $100,000 to join.</p><p>“It was my job to take orders and try to implement them,” Mr. Palermo said in an interview. He added that Mr. Grenell told him that “the president wants all new art” for the center.</p><p>In an interview, Mr. Grenell said he did not discuss art with the president. “What I instructed Josef to do was go back to every gifted piece of art and tell the giver, ‘Thank you very much, we’re returning the art,’” he said. In a subsequent interview, Mr. Grenell said he was particularly focused on returning art in need of repair or restoration, which he said the Kennedy Center could not afford.</p><p>The plan described in the documents targeted more than 20 artworks inside and outside the building, including “Blue,” a towering outdoor sculpture given to the center by its artist, Joel Shapiro, that this month <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/01/arts/design/kennedy-center-sculpture-removal-blue.html">was hauled away without public notice or explanation</a>.</p><figure><img alt="An aluminum sculpture on the Kennedy Center grounds." src="https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/09/04/us/DC-KENNEDY-CENTER-ART-28/DC-KENNEDY-CENTER-ART-28-articleLarge.jpg"/><figcaption> Salwan Georges for The New York Times</figcaption></figure><figure><img alt="A bronze relief with intricate detailing on the Kennedy Center grounds with the center in the background." src="https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/09/04/us/DC-KENNEDY-CENTER-ART-25/DC-KENNEDY-CENTER-ART-25-articleLarge.jpg"/><figcaption> Salwan Georges for The New York Times</figcaption></figure><figure><img alt="A sculpture of Don Quixote on the Kennedy Center grounds." src="https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/09/04/us/DC-KENNEDY-CENTER-ART-27/DC-KENNEDY-CENTER-ART-27-articleLarge.jpg"/><figcaption> Salwan Georges for The New York Times</figcaption></figure><figure><img alt='A sculpture in the shape of a mare titled "Milk River" stands on the Kennedy Center grounds.' src="https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/09/04/multimedia/DC-KENNEDY-CENTER-ART-10-zbkv/DC-KENNEDY-CENTER-ART-10-zbkv-articleLarge.jpg"/><figcaption>“Milk River,” a bronze-cast sculpture of a mare by Deborah Butterfield. Deborah Butterfield/Artists Rights Society (ARS), New York; Photograph by Salwan Georges for The New York Times</figcaption></figure><p><em>The artworks at the Kennedy Center that had been targeted for removal include a sculpture by the Colombian artist Eduardo Ramirez Villamizar; a bronze relief panel, "Amerika,” by the German artist Jürgen Weber; a sculpture of Don Quixote by the Spanish artist Aurelio Teno; and “Milk River,” a bronze-cast sculpture of a mare by Deborah Butterfield. </em></p><p>Inside the center, the removal plan targeted 15 artworks in the <a href="https://www.kennedy-center.org/rentals/event-rental/african-lounge/">African Lounge</a>, an events space near the Opera House that opened in 1977 and was closed and locked some time after Mr. Trump took over. The lounge’s textiles, artwork and a carved wood sculpture from Ghana representing Africans’ grief over Kennedy’s death were removed without public explanation.</p><p>In response to questions about the art removal plan, the center said the works remained “fully safeguarded with no planned or imminent changes” and that the collection “has been comprehensively cataloged.” It said the African Lounge works are being preserved within the center and declined requests to see the art.</p><figure><img alt="President Jimmy Carter wearing a suit looking up and gesturing at a piece of Senegalese artwork." src="https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/09/04/us/DC-KENNEDY-ART-CARTER/DC-KENNEDY-ART-CARTER-articleLarge.jpg"/><figcaption>President Jimmy Carter looking at “The Students,” a tapestry by the Senegalese artist Ansoumana Diedhiou, during the dedication ceremony for the African Lounge in 1977. John Duricka/Associated Press</figcaption></figure><p>Staff members and former Kennedy Center officials speculated that “Blue” was removed because Mr. Trump, who has said he considers much of modern art “a con,” did not like it. But a White House official said Mr. Trump was unaware of the plan to remove “Blue.” The center declined to comment. The sculpture has since been sold <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/07/arts/design/blue-sculpture-kennedy-center-rubenstein.html">to the philanthropist David Rubenstein</a>, the former Kennedy Center chairman ousted by Mr. Trump.</p><p>This summer a series of unsigned, unattributed gold-plated reliefs appeared in the Reach, a newer Kennedy Center annex that is slated to remain open during the main building’s closure. The images depicted include Mr. Trump raising his fist after he survived a 2024 assassination attempt, the billionaire Elon Musk and Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr.</p><figure><img alt="Gold-framed portraits of prominent figures including President Trump on display in a hallway in the Reach." src="https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/09/04/multimedia/DC-KENNEDY-CENTER-ART-11-jgmc/DC-KENNEDY-CENTER-ART-11-jgmc-articleLarge.jpg"/><figcaption> Salwan Georges for The New York Times</figcaption></figure><figure><img alt="Gold-framed portraits of prominent figures and scenes including the Lincoln Memorial, President Trump and Robert F. Kennedy Jr. and Mr. Trump adorns a wall in the Kennedy Center addition called the Reach.</em></p><p>The artist is believed to be Trish Duggan, a major Trump donor. The center declined to comment. Ms. Duggan did not respond to multiple requests for comment.</p><h2>The Name, the Tarps and the End</h2><figure><img alt="A contractor standing atop scaffolding pulling a tarp in front of Mr. Trump's name on the facade of the Kennedy Center." src="https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/09/17/multimedia/dc-kennedy-politics-18-qmpj/dc-kennedy-politics-18-qmpj-articleLarge.jpg"/><figcaption>Contractors erected a tower of scaffolding and tarps as they removed Mr. Trump’s name from the Kennedy Center’s facade. Pete Kiehart for The New York Times</figcaption></figure><p>In July 2025 Mr. Trump secured $257 million from Congress for a Kennedy Center renovation.</p><p>In December, the board <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2025/12/18/us/politics/trump-kennedy-center-name.html">voted to put Mr. Trump’s name on the building’s front</a>. More performers and patrons fled.</p><p>Representative Joyce Beatty, Democrat of Ohio and an ex officio member of the center’s board, sued over the name change.</p><p>In March Mr. Trump replaced Mr. Grenell with Matt Floca, a facilities manager with no arts programming experience, whom the president had been calling weekly for updates on everything from new boilers to theater seat armrests that he wanted to be marble.</p><p>Judge Christopher R. Cooper ruled that only Congress could change the name of the center and <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/06/12/arts/music/kennedy-center-trump-name.html">ordered Mr. Trump’s name removed</a>. Late at night in mid-June, journalists and a festival-like crowd gathered to watch.</p><p>Mr. Floca was worried that the public spectacle would enrage Mr. Trump, according to three people familiar with recent Kennedy Center events who did not want to be named for fear of retribution. Mr. Floca had workers cover a tower of scaffolding with tarps, blocking the crowd’s view as they <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/06/13/us/politics/trump-kennedy-center-name.html">pulled the letters off the wall</a>. Mr. Trump’s name is gone, but the tarps remain. Center officials have <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/06/19/us/politics/kennedy-center-trump.html">said they are there for maintenance</a>.</p><p>Barred by Judge Cooper from shutting down the building entirely, the center began closing off entire sections, citing safety concerns. Its sweeping Potomac terrace has been off-limits since midsummer. The fallen ceiling plaster led to the closure of the entire Grand Foyer, a space nearly two football fields long.</p><figure><img alt="Security fencing and signage blocking a terrace at the Kennedy Center." src="https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/09/17/multimedia/dc-kennedy-politics-21-hqgt/dc-kennedy-politics-21-hqgt-articleLarge.jpg"/><figcaption> Salwan Georges for The New York Times</figcaption></figure><figure><img alt="A hole in the ceiling of the Grand Foyer, lined by flags." src="https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/09/17/multimedia/dc-kennedy-politics-30-lfjv/dc-kennedy-politics-30-lfjv-articleLarge.jpg"/><figcaption> Salwan Georges for The New York Times</figcaption></figure><figure><img alt="Two people sit on benches amid screens and displays in an exhibition dedicated to Mr. Kennedy." src="https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/09/17/multimedia/dc-kennedy-politics-19-jpwq/dc-kennedy-politics-19-jpwq-articleLarge.jpg"/><figcaption> Salwan Georges for The New York Times</figcaption></figure><figure><img alt="Gold-framed portraits of Mr. Trump, Melania Trump, the first lady, Vice President JD Vance and Usha Vance, the second lady, on display near the Concert Hall stage door." src="https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/09/17/multimedia/dc-kennedy-politics-31-wjmk/dc-kennedy-politics-31-wjmk-articleLarge.jpg"/><figcaption> Salwan Georges for The New York Times</figcaption></figure><p><em>While the Potomac terrace has been fenced off since midsummer, and the Grand Foyer closed since the plaster fell from the ceiling, the Kennedy Center has seen dwindling visitors in other areas like the JFK Gallery.</em></p><p>The damage has made a cameo appearance in a <a href="https://x.com/WhiteHouse/status/2100224110051708984?s=46">White House video</a>, with voice-over by Mr. Floca, justifying the closure. Late last week employees in hard hats finally vacuumed up the debris from the carpet.</p><figure><img alt="A lock and chain connects a security fence on the perimeter of the Kennedy Center." src="https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/09/17/us/dc-kennedy-politics-41/dc-kennedy-politics-41-articleLarge.jpg"/><figcaption>Security fencing and tarps encircled the Kennedy Center on Thursday.
