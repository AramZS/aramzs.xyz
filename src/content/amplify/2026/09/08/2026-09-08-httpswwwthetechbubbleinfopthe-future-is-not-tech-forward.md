<p>Why is there so much energy behind consigning the future to the perpetual development of technology? Because it seemingly doesn’t require a vision of politics—even though technology is politics by other means. One can be for the future without actually thinking about who that future is for.</p>
<p>In many areas, Taylor Lorenz understands this pretty well. She has been, after all, an <a href="https://www.usermag.co/p/congress-is-about-to-break-the-internet">early</a> and <a href="https://www.theguardian.com/commentisfree/2026/mar/02/ban-children-social-media-biometic-data-surveilled">consistent</a> <a href="https://theintercept.com/2026/03/05/kosa-online-age-verification-free-speech-privacy/">opponent</a> of the ever-growing ongoing creep of surveillance as it is being grafted onto social media platforms and the internet at large. The tech industry and Congress (<a href="https://newrepublic.com/post/212522/kids-act-online-safety-house">with differences across chambers</a>) argue that child safety online requires the abolition of internet privacy: de-anonymize every user, verify their age (their identity), and hand over this censorious apparatus to some of the least trustworthy firms around (tech giants and their social media platforms). Lorenz, and a few others, have demonstrated over the years and argued convincingly these claims are bullshit. I’ve had her on <em>This Machine Kills</em> with my co-host Jathan Sadowski <a href="https://soundcloud.com/thismachinekillspod/94-stripping-the-content-mines-ft-taylor-lorenz">back in 2021</a> to talk about the influencer economy and its predatory conditions, an actual child safety issue that has come up in <a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/2023/04/08/child-influencers-protections-congress/">her reporting</a> for years and her 2023 book <em><a href="https://www.simonandschuster.com/books/Extremely-Online/Taylor-Lorenz/9781982146870?gad_source=1&amp;gad_campaignid=24153120658&amp;gbraid=0AAAAADv-uEl7LeX1iE-j_pwXmiYQx95_a&amp;gclid=Cj0KCQjw79nUBhCgARIsADSHka0Toz_P1U7dPHMqKlh2mghZzlZL0AH9fatT0dXlhKbRAfWq6C7QKzQaAq24EALw_wcB">Extremely Online</a></em>.</p>
<p>You’d be hard-pressed to dismiss those positions as regressive! Whatever debates may ensue, few look at the arguments against identity verification and say that it boils down to opposition to a “tech-forward future” even though it opposes new technologies that would make certain tasks easier (verifying who is saying what) and would purportedly be deployed to pro-social ends.</p>
<p>Which makes it a bit strange that the same skepticism, applied to other areas of technological development, is read as evidence of a primitivist conspiracy among the broader left by Lorenz.</p>
<p>In <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=36Y8poTkxtw">a recent interview </a>with Max Tani and Ben Smith on <em>Semafor</em>, Lorenz says:</p>
<blockquote><p>So much of the backlash to AI is the backlash to Big Tech. It’s people that are angry about the influence that tech has over their lives and they feel powerless against these Big Tech conglomerates.</p></blockquote>
<p>Lorenz is right here in offering a diagnosis based on power. People are concerned about the asymmetry between communities they live in and firms coming into them, keen on advancing an undemocratically planned and privately managed buildout that’s at times hysterically marketed as the prelude to the destruction of life as we know it.</p>
<p>And yet. A few seconds after making that observation, Lorenz adds:</p>
<blockquote><p>This is my big disagreement with the Left. I think a lot of people on the Left do not believe in a tech-forward future, they just want to go back.</p></blockquote>
<p>This doesn’t quite square with the idea she offers just earlier—that the backlash is downstream of a desire to regain power over your life. Unless, of course, you believe that the frustration leads people on the Left to wanting a technological regression in hopes of securing control over their lives. Lorenz seems to believe as much, citing Molly Taft’s reporting for WIRED on the politics of the data center backlash as evidence of “this far-left and far-right alliance of wanting to go back, wanting to use brick phones, believing the internet was a mistake.”</p>
<p>Lorenz is right when she says Taft’s piece and broader reporting offer some of the best looks at the anti-data center backlash, but she gets wrong what Taft’s reporting actually reveals.</p>
<p>The Taft piece in question (<a href="https://www.wired.com/story/how-data-centers-broke-american-politics/">“How Data Centers Broke American Politics”</a>) hones in on Gage Bailey: a Democrat living in Troup County, Georgia who holds a degree in environmental science. After learning of construction plans for a data center in his area, he was unable to find out how much power or water it would actually draw so he wrote up a 13-page memo exhaustively documenting his research into the environmental impacts of data centers. Through trial and error, he learned how to file public records requests in hopes of learning more, networked with other environmental advocates, came to moderate a Facebook group of 4,000 people, and help with anti-data center efforts in neighboring counties. Bailey eventually learns Google will be the data center’s tenant, is able to get together other advocates for one meeting in November 2025, and is promptly ghosted by the hyperscaler.</p>
<p>Reading the article, it’s clear that it’s not Bailey who thinks the Internet is a mistake as he uses it to carry out his research and organizing. Nor is it Bailey who pines for brick phones or some “trad” return to the village. Those traits belong to Joe Allen, Steven Bannon’s tech correspondent for his <em>War Room </em>podcast. Allen reveals to Taft that it was the Unabomber’s manifesto (“Industrial Society and Its Future”) that had a fundamental influence on his thinking here.</p>
<p>Taft writes:</p>
<blockquote><p>The influence is apparent. On <em>War Room</em>, Allen acts the part of a preacher at the pulpit warning us all of the coming dystopian age. A lot of his work is somewhat adjacent to body horror: What happens to our souls, he wonders, when our bodies and environments are increasingly digitized? I ask him why he thinks people hate data centers. He says: “If you ever see a fetus growing, and you see the brain growing and the neurons forming—it’s amazing. Data centers, if you think about them as neurons of the Antichrist, that’s what we’re witnessing. The false messiah.”</p></blockquote>
<p>Small wonder then that the Unabomber, who railed against the Left and the Right (though he saved most of his venom for the “industrial revolution and its consequences” as those who stop at the first page like to remind us), would serve as inspiration for one of Bannon’s disciples in the unending search for positions that peel from the left and right to a rejuvenated far-right vision.</p>
<p>Still, it’s unclear what any of this has to do with the claim that the Left wants to go back. The closest bit you might find in the article is when Taft discusses the appendix of Allen’s book, titled “My 55-Point Plan to Stay Human.” As Taft writes:</p>
<blockquote><p>suggestions like “Say hello to your neighbors” and “Buy a flip phone,” would be a hit at any leftist reading group in 2026 (perhaps with an edit to remove Allen’s insistence on “secure borders”). I agree with his assertion, moreover, that “it’s easy to imagine a day when … the specter of deadly AI is used to clamp down on freedom and erect miserable technocratic systems.” I do not, however, think the Covid-19 pandemic or “climate change” are examples of this already happening. Jennifer Bilek, a prominent anti-trans writer who claims that the medical-industrial complex has “infiltrated” gay and lesbian communities to push “transgenderism,” blurbed Allen’s book. When Allen recommends that I talk to her, I make an offhand remark that I don’t think she’d like me very much. “Oh, right,” he says, wiggling his eyebrows at me and referencing the pronouns listed on my X bio. “‘They/them.’”</p></blockquote>
<p>The piece also complicates the narrative that there’s a clean bipartisan alliance here. Humans First, Allen’s bipartisan AI safety group, ends up splitting along partisan lines!</p>
<p>More from Taft:</p>
<blockquote><p>Politics comes for Humans First too. In late March, there’s a high-profile spat online where pro-AI White House allies like Marc Andreessen and David Sacks retweet a piece critical of Humans First. Shortly after, the organization splits along partisan lines. The conservative wing keeps the Humans First name and makes the right-wing political activist Amy Kremer, who helped organize the January 6 “Save America” rally in DC, its chair; Allen leaves the group. When I ask Allen about the changeup, he is curiously coy (but characteristically body-horrible): The group was “undergoing mitosis,” he says. “I am now a lone organelle floating in the residual cytoplasm.”</p></blockquote>
<p>Taft suggests the movement is on the hunt for its own hallowed middle ground somewhere between rejecting technology for the village and giving up our future on a silver platter, adding that “even if this middle ground does exist, it’s going to be a journey to reach it—one that our political system might not be prepared for.”</p>
<p>Now, there <strong>is</strong> a primitivist thread that Taft’s reporting highlights—on the Right. It’s in MAHA and its anti-vaccine politics, through the hundreds of counties that have restricted solar and wind, through “crunchy hippie types” who—like their forebears in the 60s and 70s—may resemble some leftists aesthetically but are bastards of libertarianism above all else.</p>
<p>Where does Lorenz’s depiction of the Left appear in this? Nowhere. Bannon seized upon a gap in the discourse and understood the general population’s frustration better and earlier than others, but what is the man actually saying? As Taft points out, last year he was calling data centers “weapons labs” that were “being turned onto the population.” Is that a complaint about technology? Not really. In fact, it more closely mirrors Lorenz’s concerns about age verification—who is watching whom and what are we going to do about it—though differing, obviously, in register because it’s meant to be a Trojan horse smuggling in a host of other reactionary ideas about who belongs in our communities to begin with (and who’s attacking them).</p>
<p>Where is this supposed left-right horseshoe, then? Does anyone seriously believe the <a href="https://www.nybooks.com/articles/2025/09/25/the-war-app-the-technological-republic-karp-zamiska/">reactionary right is anti-technology</a>? Palantir, Anduril, biometric surveillance, predictive policing, automated assassinations, this is the stuff they live for. In between his ramblings about <a href="https://dissentmagazine.org/online_articles/know-your-enemy-peter-thiel-antichrist/">whether his latest fixation is secretly the Anti-Christ</a> (could it be the Pope? Or Greta Thunberg? Or regulation itself?), Peter Thiel has done more to build a tech-forward future than almost anyone drawing breath—especially those who consider themselves techno-optimists or accelerationists. The Right’s opposition to tech tends to be to green tech and certain platform speech codes and norms, to projects they understand as threats to their dream of a hydrocarbon death cult stretching sea from shining sea. They couldn’t care less about the machinery, they care about certain tech in certain hands, deployed towards certain ends. And they understand that they have to oppose attempts by the left to try and offer alternate articulations of that argument which don’t trade in the promise of blood and treasure.</p>
<p>All this to say that you can only believe there is a far-right and far-left alliance in which they mirror one another if you believe there is only one axis that matters: forward, or backward.</p>
<p>“Tech-forward” should have no place in how we think about the future. There is not a single axis. Things are not either forward or back, accelerating or retreating, primitive or modern, fast or slow. If we are having debates about who owns global compute, what AI infrastructure sits on the interconnection queue, who is paying for rate increases, who signs NDAs or negotiates the terms of data center projects, it becomes painfully obvious how shallow this framing is.</p>
<p>At the same time, however, it’s clear why collapsing tech debates to “forward or back” leads to such broad accusations of primitivism. Every political question, instead of being considered on its own terms, can just as easily be resolved as: which way do you want to go? Into the unmapped future where we can project our hopes and dreams and fears? Or the well-trod past, with its tired thorny debates about equality, justice, power, and blah blah blah.</p>
<p>Take Langdon Winner’s 1980 essay “Do Artifacts Have Politics?” where he spends some time showing “physical arrangements that contain explicit or implicit political purposes.” He fires off examples peppering the history of architecture, of city planning, and of public works, but it’s his examination of industrial machinery that we should revisit. Winner writes:</p>
<blockquote><p>Studies of industrial machines and instruments also turn up interesting political stories, including some that violate our normal expectations about why technological innovations are made in the first place. If we suppose that new technologies are introduced to achieve increased efficiency, the history of technology shows that we will sometimes be disappointed. Technological change expresses a panoply of human motives, not the least of which is the desire of some to have dominion over others, even though it may require an occasional sacrifice of cost-cutting and some violence to the norm of getting more from less.</p></blockquote>
<p>Winner goes on to write:</p>
<blockquote><p>One poignant illustration can be found in the history of nineteenth century industrial mechanization. At Cyrus McCormick’s reaper manufacturing plant in Chicago in the middle 1880s, pneumatic molding machines, a new and largely untested innovation, were added to the foundry at an estimated cost of $500,000. In the standard economic interpretation of such things, we would expect that this step was taken to modernize the plant and achieve the kind of efficiencies that mechanization brings. But historian Robert Ozanne has shown why the development must be seen in a broader context. At the time, Cyrus McCormick II was engaged in a battle with the National Union of Iron Molders. He saw the addition of the new machines as a way to “weed out the bad element among the men,” namely, the skilled workers who had organized the union local in Chicago. The new machines, manned by unskilled labor, actually produced inferior castings at a higher cost than the earlier process. After three years of use the machines were, in fact, abandoned, but by that time they had served their purpose—the destruction of the union. Thus, the story of these technical developments at the McCormick factory cannot be understood adequately outside the record of workers’ attempts to organize, police repression of the labor movement in Chicago during that period, and the events surrounding the bombing at Haymarket Square. Technological history and American political history were at that moment deeply intertwined.</p></blockquote>
<p>The machines were installed, despite the cost, to break labor—they were adopted because they foreclosed certain possibilities. Foreclosing the future in the name of the future is a potent intention for design and it’s on clear display with the data center buildout. NDAs to ensure tax abatements are negotiated before a public hears, behind-the-meter power generation where firms build out their own fossil fuel infrastructure to bypass debates over electricity, intentionally choosing communities incapable of saying no as part of a larger race to the bottom for communities nationwide (if <strong>you</strong> don’t sign, others will), these are all design intentions aimed at foreclosing alternatives that are inconvenient to the firms expecting (and in need of) a certain degree of permissiveness to satisfy investors, creditors, and perpetually growing industry expectations about the pace of the AI infrastructure buildout.</p>
<p>That some technical arrangements are inherently political also means they require certain social orders if they have any hope of surviving. Take the way in which firms pursue perpetually growing scale. At the moment, frontier-scale training requires gigawatts of power and tens of billions of capital (Chase Lochmiller, chief executive and co-founder of Crusoe <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GcCGzfKdCd0&amp;list=PLoROMvodv4rOAe_DtQYOCKy6_x3irEYV1&amp;index=3">estimates</a> $60 billion per gigawatt of compute capacity, Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang <a href="https://siliconangle.com/2026/06/01/five-thoughts-nvidia-ceo-jensen-huangs-gtc-taipei-2026-keynote/">estimates</a> it going up to $100 billion per GW). It needs ceaseless and prioritized access to energy. And it needs complete opacity. What’s the political and social order we can expect to follow from that?</p>
<p>A handful of firms dictating and colluding on the terms of this buildout. <a href="https://www.wheresyoured.at/premium-the-haters-guide-to-circular-financing-part-one/">Circular financing</a> that poses a systemic risk to the broader economy as it <a href="https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/dylan-patel-3">crowds out other productive capital allocations</a>. An <a href="https://finance.yahoo.com/energy/articles/private-equity-circling-utilities-ai-180000273.html?guccounter=1&amp;guce_referrer=aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cuZ29vZ2xlLmNvbS8&amp;guce_referrer_sig=AQAAAEvTsTUh4Arlh2TeDY6sVfl0XQ894JFBrUIvCW7LX3mJ1e8jAPQ_1nSB4663naGH67bf-S8yuQ4vpDOUSo0AI1i6oGwdsjqG8lSUhtQbUzb4Uf_n0uzv9yHKFPwpq-Lze5HpjsZRee65hF3EO9LnbQLyzO7HjCdZ3xxcjRTOuQ0a">ever-more privatized electrical grid</a> that threatens to offload as many costs to all but the firms driving this buildout. The convergence on innovations that do a better job at realizing a return than providing socially beneficial uses. And, of course, <a href="https://datasociety.net/research-library/the-big-ai-state-how-the-trump-administration-is-shaping-us-industrial-policy-toward-global-technological-dominance/">a state that intervenes on behalf of every single interest but the public’s</a>!</p>
<p>If, it turns out, the technical arrangements that sustain this technological development require a political and social order which further unravels the threadbare remnants of our democracy, it’s not clear why opposition to it should be labeled as a primitivist or atavistic. Foreclosure is seen as legitimate when imposed from above—as above so below! We can and should foreclose certain arrangements, in fact we already do! Do we embrace germline editing? CFCs? Do we read all concerns about forever chemicals or other bio-accumulative toxins as yearning for a simpler time? Do you reject a tech-forward future when you raise concerns about how the miracle of telehealth makes it cheaper than ever to open up your very own pill mill? When you recoil in disgust at the financialization of everything, to the metastasis of prediction markets beyond app-based sports betting to betting on any sort of event big or small, does that suggest you want to turn the clock back? Or does it suggest you view these developments as corrosive to the political and social order we have? Do we block certain technologies because we are ruled by a cabal of primitivists stalking the halls of power, or because everything we develop is not an unalloyed good which can and should be spread far and wide?</p>
<p>Refusal is not just some impotent act of nostalgia, and the extent to which we think so is a measure of our contempt for the prospect of taking democratic politics seriously. We should have a greater role in saying what does and doesn’t get designed or developed or deployed, because we should have a greater role in deciding what sort of politics and what sort of social relations we are party to! Why should these be left to the whims and dictates of the richest among us?</p>
<p>Now, I do think there are a few things Lorenz is partially right about. One is that the left is still searching for a compelling picture of what the future will look like (and the technical arrangements that’ll define it). For some, there’s a desire to restore and fortify so we can finally move into the future: to assemble the post-war compact and forge a New New Deal as a jumping pad for deeper reforms and transformations. Restoration isn’t quite nostalgia, but it’s also not quite a vision of the future—not to say there are no futures of the future, but that there are plenty of debates about whether they are enough, whether they fall short, whether they misunderstand what we should be prioritizing, and that Lorenz’s complaint rhymes with some of those.</p>
<p>Evgeny Morozov makes somewhat of similar observation in a <em><a href="https://jacobin.com/2023/12/evgeny-morozov-interview-technology-sovereignty-global-south-development-cybersyn">Jacobin</a></em><a href="https://jacobin.com/2023/12/evgeny-morozov-interview-technology-sovereignty-global-south-development-cybersyn"> interview</a> with where he’s asked about the socialist “debate on planning and technology that in recent years has given rise to the emergence of a current known as cybercommunism.” Morozov’s complaint boils down to: he believes there’s plenty of socialist theory of planning floating around, but relatively little when it comes to theorizing modernity. Which is to say it can imagine how computers can be used to administer Marx’s “realm of necessity” (housing, clothing, food, the basic reproduction of our social lives), but has relatively little to say about how technology could enable creativity, pursue novelty, allow for experimentation, and cultivate complexity outside of market logic.</p>
<p>As much as we may not like it, neoliberalism offers an answer and socialism doesn’t (for now). The market is offered up not just as a way to allocate goods, but a venue for actualization and development in part because novelty and complexity can emerge there. So it’s not enough for socialist alternatives to be nonmarket, they also have to have some sort of modernity, and so we need what he calls a “nonmarket modernism.” We do need a broad and compelling account of technological development that doesn’t just identify what we don’t want but envisions what we want to fight for, what we want to build towards, what we want to experiment with, foreclose, discover, things that I do think sit beyond discussions of labor productivity or the automation of cognition or the flowering of intelligence or whatever else is confused for these sorts of discussions outside of the left. What trajectory might we choose if we abandon the “tech-forward” arc which follows the path private capital lays out for it.</p>
<p>I also partially agree with Lorenz on her point about how anti-data center opposition only goes so far: some want to ban them, others bomb them, but what happens after we institute something like a moratorium? Do we want to “stop technological progress?” she asks in the interview.</p>
<p>I think that’s fair for some of the movement, sure, but, again, many on the left have been quite clear about what the next steps should be. On This Machine Kills, for example, we’ve talked about the possibility of communities forming compacts that establish minimum terms for data center development, allowing them to prevent firms from playing various jurisdictions against one another in a race to the bottom. Raising construction costs or increasing the uncertainty of approval, whether by a national moratorium or regional compacts, could be part of a strategy targeting vulnerable operations and making them untenable, creating openings for public expropriation or a publicly coordinated buildout starting with idle infrastructure—assuming we still decide hyperscale AI infrastructure is worth building at this frenzied pace.</p>
<p>Still, stepping off that, a moratorium is not itself an empty position even if there’s no next step offered. In a <a href="https://www.theideasletter.org/essay/socialism-after-ai/">debate</a> between Morozov and Aaron Benanav, a socialist offering some of the more interesting ideas about the future of technological development within the cybercommunism tradition Morozov critiques, Benanav <a href="https://www.theideasletter.org/essay/a-real-political-economy-of-technology/">offers</a> a different framing to think about the ongoing buildout. (In a future piece I’ll dive a bit deeper in the debate, which is full of many many fascinating ideas and threads worth following). There are two buildouts happening simultaneously, competing for the same resources, interconnection queue positions, transformers, turbines, capital, and skilled labor: the green transition and the generative AI buildouts. In this framing, is opposition to a data center about the technology itself or to the allocation of resources? In his reply to Morozov, Benanav writes:</p>
<blockquote><p>On technical grounds, the green transition appears the stronger candidate for a genuinely transformative revolution, but technical potential alone never guarantees realization. From an economic standpoint, what counts as promising is not passively discovered. It must be actively produced through investment decisions. Which systems are developed further, and hence which innovations are allowed to mature into sociotechnical systems, depends on how investment is organized and who controls it.</p><p>Under capitalism, that control is concentrated in a narrow set of hands—venture capitalists, corporate executives, and state bureaucracies—whose decisions are oriented toward profitability and geopolitical competition. This concentration sharply narrows the range of technological paths that can be pursued, while suppressing public contestation over the purposes technologies are made to serve. Breaking free from this constraint requires more than better regulation of individual technologies. It requires opening up investment decisions themselves to democratic participation, so that alternative futures can be articulated, debated, and pursued for reasons beyond profitability and geopolitical rivalry.</p></blockquote>
<p>Lorenz also invokes China as an example that we should emulate in limited ways—specifically its open models and how it powers data centers with clean energy. Sure, but that’s an arrangement where the buildout is state-directed and subordinated much more aggressively than left to the vagaries of market coordination! It’s also an arrangement that might have desirable outcomes but springs out of an authoritarian bent—a bit unthinkable in a country as permissive of corporate predation as ours. Still, how do we get there without contestation that forces new arrangements to animate how we allocate resources for our buildouts?</p>
<p>Here, I tend to invoke Hubert Horan’s <a href="https://www.nakedcapitalism.com/2021/08/hubert-horan-can-uber-ever-deliver-part-twenty-five-didis-ipo-illustrates-why-ubers-business-model-was-always-hopeless.html">comparison of Chinese and American national policies towards tech</a>, looking at Beijing’s tech sector crackdown which broadly spanned 2020-23. Western media tended to read Beijing’s probes and actions as authoritarian paranoia “so obsessively concerned with anything that might ever pose any threat to its absolute political authority” that it would risk stifling innovation and scaring off investors. Horan suggested a simpler read: Beijing looked at the American experiment and decided it had no interest in reproducing our failures.</p>
<p>As Horan lays out, US tech policy under laissez-faire has consistently produced equity bubbles “divorced from any industrial policy considerations,” where stock prices rise whether capital flows to productive manufacturing or sports gambling apps. “No one cares what causes stock prices to go up as long as stocks go up,” Horan writes. And since it is “far easier to boost stock prices by eliminating competition and exploiting workers and consumers than by developing new technologies,” innovation declines while predatory extraction rises. The result is a political system captured by tech oligarchs, one that makes it “virtually impossible to address externalities” such as inequality, the destruction of our ecological niche, the evisceration of our information environment, or rampant privacy violations.</p>
<p>It makes sense, then, that Beijing had simply concluded that giving “the Jeff Bezos[es], Mark Zuckerbergs, and Travis Kalanicks of the world...unfettered freedom to flaunt any rules they didn’t like” had not produced efficient outcomes. In China, tech promised to be the first sector where private capital had grown powerful enough to challenge central planning. And so when Jack Ma demanded Alibaba have Amazon’s freedom and when Didi ignored regulators in hopes of going public, Beijing understood this as political defiance <strong>AND</strong> economic mismanagement. If it developed any further, Beijing feared it would be unable “to control its currency and trade policies, or to fix problems with its fragile shadow banking system or to funnel capital to industries (such as semiconductors or Belt and Road investments) deemed to be major development priorities.” If the CCP sees itself as the “manager of the Chinese economy,” then doesn’t it make sense why it wouldn’t allow for a US-style tech sector (complete with the ascendance of oligarchs unburdened by political authority) to flourish?</p>
<p>Which is the point. If ‘what comes next’ means we prioritize a green transition, impose some sort of rationality to AI capex, redirect compute toward pro-social ends, or disentangle this investment boom from <a href="https://www.thetechbubble.info/p/how-much-a-dollar-cost">our Pax Silica gambit</a>, there have already been plenty of articulated alternatives to choose from or emulate.</p>
<p>It’s fair to object to a veto that you don’t think also offers some alternative. It’s another thing entirely to pretend no alternatives exist—and stranger still to conclude that, because our alternatives remain contested we should pre-emptively surrender the veto while self-regulation fails time and time again.</p>
<p>It does not take much effort to envision some immediate next steps or lodestars to reach for if you are unsatisfied with what’s been offered thus far, either!</p>
<p>Let a hundred Gage Baileys bloom, let a hundred memos contend! But what will they amount to given the massive asymmetry that exists between communities and the firms targeting them—not just in money but in actual details about what’s being offered and what alternatives are possible. Who can represent their county’s interests when everyone is kept in the dark? We need a public capacity to model and price these deals, as well as to contest and reject them—as well as infrastructure for communities to do that work together as a sort of coalition that deters a race to the bottom where firms can pick off the weakest link.</p>
<p>We can also look to the past for inspiration about building state capacity that the public, as opposed to the tech sector, can wield and capture. Take the <a href="https://rooseveltinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/4013_RI_IndustrialPolicyReport_2025.pdf">Reconstruction Finance Corporation</a>, which by 1934 held voting rights in thousands of American companies as the largest single investor in the country. Then, the RFC bought up as much preferred stock as it could along with common stock, giving a firm more capital while taking more control of said firm, and using that control to decide dividends, executive pay, and expenditures. During WWII, it built and operated defense manufacturing nationwide through eight subsidiaries and the Treasury Department’s final report noted the enabling legislation there said nothing about turning a profit through this enterprise. Might we try something with frontier AI labs and hyperscalers, subordinating them to public interest and non-market coordination to achieve certain aims while prioritizing a social and political order that isn’t decided solely by tech oligarchs and their reactionary ideologues?</p>
<p>And lastly, we could intervene in the sovereign wealth fund and social contract debates! Trump has taken stakes and golden shares in firms the government has deemed strategically significant—Intel, U.S. Steel, USA Rare Earth, and MP Materials, to name a few. This sort of <a href="https://law.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/31-STAN.-J.L.-ECON.-BUS.-166-Omarova.pdf">directed industrial policy</a> is already embraced by the right, it could be subordinated towards the left’s ends come the next administration. This move also brings us right back to Winner: one institution, one instrument, arranged to settle certain political questions one way can be used to settle them another, no?</p>
<p>We don’t need a tech-forward future, we need the future—on our own terms!</p>
