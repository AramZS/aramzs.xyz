<p>“What happened to me was horrific and deeply disturbing,” <a href="https://www.cornellsun.com/article/2026/09/tccsuykuvb4z">Jane Doe</a>, a former Cornell student, told the <em>Cornell Daily Sun </em>in a September 18 story. “Almost two years later, I still carry the weight of its impact every day and night.” According to the <a href="https://www.thecut.com/article/what-we-know-about-the-cornell-seven-rape-case.html">lawsuit</a> she filed earlier this month, Doe went to the Chi Phi fraternity house at Cornell University one night in October 2024, when she was 20 years old. She was meeting someone she considered to be a friend; she was visibly intoxicated. Nonetheless, she alleges, two frat brothers pressured her to snort ketamine and gave her marijuana and more alcohol. Then they began “engaging in sexual acts” with her, “all without her consent,” according to the <a href="https://iapps.courts.state.ny.us/nyscef/ViewDocument?docIndex=w3cAcu0L42eUEEFc9PeClw%3D%3D">lawsuit</a>.</p>
<p>At 1:42 a.m., one of the defendants then allegedly sent a message to a Snapchat group for active members of Chi Phi: It advertised “free pussy” upstairs. Doe says she tried to cover herself with a blanket as more men entered the room with her. She tried to push one of them away when he began to kiss her. But she felt unable to escape, she says, with her phone across the room and so many substances in her system. She alleges she was sexually assaulted for hours and was commanded to take more ketamine, <a href="https://www.cornellsun.com/article/2026/09/tccsuykuvb4z">including by snorting it off an erect penis</a>. The alleged assault continued until around 5:45 a.m., when she lost consciousness, according to her filing.<br/>
Jane Doe went to the Cornell University police with some of these allegations three weeks later, at the urging of friends to whom she had confided. That same day, the university issued a temporary suspension for the Chi Phi chapter; temporary suspensions were also issued for seven individual students. Later, Cornell launched a Title IX investigation. Following at least 12 hearings, two of the seven students were expelled. The others received lesser penalties, including suspensions, having to take workshops, and — now, notoriously — <a href="https://katiecouric.com/news/national-news/cornell-sexual-assault-lawsuit-what-we-know/">writing essays</a> in what is termed an “alternative resolution” process.</p>
<p>Seven Chi Phi members are named in the lawsuit: Matthew Ingalls, Jonathan Newell, Winston Lee, Gillio Lopes, Diego Sarabia, Scott Norris, and Scott Kretzschmar. But it also names Cornell, accusing the university of negligence, negligent supervision, breach of contract, violations of state human rights and education laws, and negligent infliction of emotional distress.</p>
<p>I began to do what I do — <a href="https://katemanne.substack.com/p/death-by-misogyny">reading and writing and thinking about misogyny</a> — because of my dismay at how girls and women are treated on campus, and at other college campuses across America. As a professor at Cornell, I have had many, many victims in my office, confiding in me their experiences of sexual assault and harassment. Many didn’t even try to report them to the police or the university, and, for those who did, the experiences and the outcomes have rarely been positive. There’s a prevailing sense that campus culture is rape culture, especially in fraternity houses: It’s a deeply embedded feature of life here that is widely known but rarely protested. A <a href="https://news.cornell.edu/stories/2025/11/survey-reveals-campus-sexual-assault-and-harassment">2025 survey conducted by the university</a> found that 35 percent of undergraduate women reported experiencing “nonconsensual sexual contact” during their time at Cornell, an increase from the 23 percent in the 2023 survey. <a href="https://iapps.courts.state.ny.us/nyscef/ViewDocument?docIndex=w3cAcu0L42eUEEFc9PeClw%3D%3D">In her lawsuit</a>, Jane Doe notes that, when she decided to participate in Greek life at Cornell by joining the Tri Delta sorority, she was unaware that the former president of Psi Upsilon, <a href="https://abc7ny.com/post/cornell-student-from-new-york-city-charged-in-sexual-assault-case/1189737/">Wolfgang Ballinger</a>, had been subject to criminal charges for the rape of a female student at the frat house in 2016.</p>
<p>It’s worth stating that Cornell is not unusual in this respect not because I’m defending my employer, but rather to give you a sense of the scale of the problem: Fraternity houses across the country are a <a href="https://ml-law.net/sexual-abuse-clergy-abuse/fraternities-rape-culture">hotbed of rape and misogyny</a>.</p>
<p>In a deeply misogynistic society, groups of boys and men routinely bond over humiliating and exploiting women, <a href="https://sorayachemaly.substack.com/p/10b245c7-59f2-41f1-acbf-6ec67ec3cb32?fbclid=Iwb21leAUmoc9jbGNrBSahzGV4dG4DYWVtAjExAHBkb2YFc3J0YwZhcHBfaWQMMzUwNjg1NTMxNzI4AAEevAoQ7BiTFG_pCnGzlvBM_3fcF9xtEeqjES8GAwTcQpNtaaPWGAfYNxj8YHc_aem_Gn3HMspL2Qy--paAS7Pg8g&amp;utm_id=97758_v0_s00_e0_tv2_a1dennhb545qu7">as Soraya Chemaly has written</a>. And the victim is not only physically and emotionally traumatized following one of the most fundamental imaginable violations: They are likely to be doubted and undermined at every subsequent step of the process. In Jane Doe’s case, the district attorney did not bring any charges after relying on the investigation done by campus police; they <a href="https://www.the-independent.com/news/world/americas/crime/jake-tapper-cornell-gang-rape-da-b3057916.html">didn’t even speak with the men involved</a>. The <a href="https://www.tompkinscountyny.gov/News-articles/District-Attorney-statement-regarding-Jane-Doe-v.-Cornell-University-et-al">district attorney just announced that they are reopening the case</a> on the basis of new information contained in the civil lawsuit, and in recognition of the fact that “it can take years of therapy and healing for victims of sexual assaults to fully process and understand what happened to them.”</p>
<p>To be sure, Jane Doe’s<a href="https://www.tompkinscountyny.gov/News-articles/District-Attorney-statement-regarding-Jane-Doe-v.-Cornell-University-et-al"> original statement to the Cornell police</a> described more of the activities that night as voluntary, including her consumption of alcohol and ketamine. And under New York State law, voluntary intoxication does not preclude consent unless it is completely immobilizing — a disturbing legal loophole that many of us, including me, were previously unaware of. But, whatever the wisdom of the DA’s original decision not to pursue the case — he described the students’ behavior as morally reprehensible but not necessarily illegal on the basis of the information they had about the case at the time — <a href="https://officeofcivilrights.cornell.edu/wp-content/uploads/Procedures-for-Resolution-of-Reports-Against-Students-Under-Cornell-University-Policy-6.4-8.5.2024.pdf">Cornell’s then-operative student code of conduct</a> defines sexual assault very differently. Specifically, it recognizes that intoxication <em>does </em>preclude consent if it is severe enough. This means that the university’s Title IX investigation faced different questions than the DA did. Centrally, did the plaintiffs commit sexual assault, as defined in this code, by a preponderance of the evidence? And, if so, how should they be punished?</p>
<p>We now know that the university’s processes ultimately left five of the seven students accused free to graduate. Meanwhile, Jane Doe dropped out of Cornell and <a href="https://www.the-independent.com/news/world/americas/crime/jake-tapper-cornell-gang-rape-da-b3057916.html">was denied her request to get her tuition refunded</a> so that she could transfer to a different college. She hence faced more severe consequences than most of the men who she alleges gang-raped her. And rape can effectively be a punishment for the crime of being female.</p>
<p>If you’re an outsider to American universities, you might think that students are simply out of control, and that disciplinary processes are similarly lax for other infringements. But that is not accurate. Compare this to how the university punishes protesters. In the spring of 2024, Cornell <a href="https://www.cornellsun.com/article/2024/04/breaking-cornell-suspends-four-student-protestors">issued temporary suspensions</a> for four students protesting the war in Gaza. Specifically, these students urged the university to divest its holdings in companies that supported the genocide of Palestinians. To draw more attention to their cause, they staged the protest in a central area of campus where protests are not permitted without authorization. One of these students was my own advisee, and having supported her throughout the the months-long case, I can state with confidence that she did nothing wrong. Moreover, she happens to be deaf and was suspended in part for being “unreasonably loud.” Privacy considerations prevent me from saying more about her case here, but let me just add that I will always be angry with Cornell for how they treated her. And <a href="https://www.cornellsun.com/article/2026/01/momodou-taal-suspended-cornell-ph-d-student-says-he-was-detained-by-uk-police">Momodou Taal</a>, an international Ph.D. student, was ultimately suspended twice by Cornell for protesting, following the university’s humiliating disciplinary processes (which I have witnessed firsthand as I supported my own student during them). Ultimately, the Trump administration demanded Taal surrender himself to Immigration and Customs Enforcement, and he chose to flee the country rather than <a href="https://www.nyclu.org/commentary/how-mahmoud-khalils-arrest-fits-in-a-nationwide-crackdown-on-free-speech">risk being abducted</a>. He remains abroad, having effectively self-deported.</p>
<p>You might wonder if student protesters are a special case in the current political environment. But again, that isn’t the case: Students who are found to have committed plagiarism and other academic-integrity violations regularly find themselves with marks on their permanent record and may be suspended, even expelled, for serious or repeated infractions.</p>
<p>This disparity in consequences is not a mere issue of proportionality and, therefore, fairness. Women, who make up a majority of sexual-assault victims, are simply not safe at Cornell, which has a moral as well as legal obligation to give them an equitable educational environment under Title IX. As things stand, students who commit infractions like plagiarism — which typically has no real victims — are often punished more harshly than the male students directly responsible for making the campus dangerous for female ones. Until there is zero tolerance for sexual assault on campus, women will not be protected here. And that is inexcusable.</p>
<p>There’s a feeling at times that, while we all know the statistics, protesting sexual assault on campus is a lost cause — and, it must be said, a deeply uncool one. As a feminist commentator, I feel every time I write about these issues that I am swimming against the tide of indifference and <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2018/09/26/opinion/brett-kavanaugh-hearing-himpathy.html">himpathy</a>: my term for the reflexive hand-wringing over the loss of a male perpetrator’s bright future instead of a female rape victim’s welfare. But maybe, finally, the currents are changing: <a href="https://www.npr.org/2026/09/28/nx-s1-5983480/cornell-fraternity-rape-chi-phi-case-updates">News story</a> after <a href="https://abcnews.com/US/district-attorney-reopens-case-alleged-cornell-university-gang/story?id=136806149">news story</a> about <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/28/nyregion/cornell-chi-phi-fraternity-assault.html">the case</a> has provoked a more vigorous discussion of this case than I, for one, am used to. When I went to work on Monday morning to teach a two-and-a-half-hour seminar, there was a palpable feeling on campus that this is <em>big</em>. This is serious. And where — as I heard several people wonder out loud, angrily — is the administration’s statement?</p>
<p>Cornell <a href="https://statements.cornell.edu/2026/20260928-da-investigation.cfm">did indeed release a statement</a> later on Monday defending its response, claiming it conducted a thorough Title IX investigation into these events. “None of the individuals charged were offered the opportunity to write essays as a sole consequence of their involvement … Any suggestion that the university did not impose consequential punishments for those involved is false.” It is hard, however, for me to understand how a student found by <a href="https://titleix.cornell.edu/support-and-resources/faqs/">the preponderance of the evidence</a> to deserve <em>any</em> sanction following this incident deserved to have the opportunity to stay at Cornell, as with five of the named defendants. At the very least, the university owes Jane Doe and the community more than a dubious insinuation that anyone demanding accountability here is somehow the real problem. “A continuation of the false narrative that there were minimal consequences in this case is irresponsible” and “will make survivors feel less safe and supported,” wrote Kyle Kimball, vice-president for university relations. I can think of larger and more systemic reasons for such a feeling in this moment.</p>
<p>On Wednesday night, Cornell will bring the distinguished sociologist Cynthia Miller-Idriss to campus for a <a href="https://news.cornell.edu/stories/2026/09/leading-scholar-extremism-give-university-lecture">prestigious university lecture</a>. The topic? The links between misogyny and violent extremism. But what good is this discussion if we don’t address the problems in our own backyard? What use was it to bring the writer and activist Chanel Miller to campus in the spring <a href="https://katemanne.substack.com/p/chanel-millers-hardest-gig">just for fraternity brothers to scroll, bored, on their phones</a> as she movingly shared her experience as <a href="https://www.npr.org/2019/09/04/757626939/victim-of-brock-turner-sexual-assault-reveals-her-identity">Emily Doe, the victim of Brock Turner</a>? As I wrote at the time, I was floored and dismayed as I sat in the audience with one of my brilliant undergraduates; we heard Miller’s shattering account of being sexually assaulted by Turner behind a dumpster at Stanford University after a fraternity party. Her talk was riveting, and there was so much to learn from it. And yet many of the male students who hailed predominantly from fraternities — 15 percent of each chapter was required to be in attendance — were so tuned out that Miller commented ruefully: “You know, this has been my hardest gig, because they didn’t turn down the house lights. I’m not sure how you professors do it, because it’s so hard looking out at this sea of mildly disgruntled faces. I mean, I’m sure you’re all very moved, but …,” Miller smiled. The implication being, it didn’t seem like it, from her angle.</p>
<p>We urgently need massive institutional change and an end to this indifference. The needle seems to be moving for a moment in public discourse. But it remains to be seen if a lackluster institutional response on Cornell’s part can be shifted. Meanwhile, what we need to demand from universities far and wide is clear: Cease to be a place where sexual assault is often subject to less punishment than protesting and plagiarism. Cease to be a place where the consequences for sexual-assault victims are often greater than for the perpetrators. Begin to be a place where parents can send our daughters and be confident that they will be as safe as it is possible to be under patriarchy. And become a place where our sons are not initiated into rape culture.</p>
