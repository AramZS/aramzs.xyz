<h2>A Father of the Internet Retires</h2>
<p>Vint Cerf is stepping away. The computer scientist who helped design the internet’s core protocols <a href="https://techcrunch.com/2026/06/30/the-father-of-the-internet-is-finally-retiring/">will retire</a> as Google’s chief internet evangelist next week, closing a run of more than 20 years at the company and one of the longest careers in computing. Cerf, 83, made the plan public during a panel, where a fellow pioneer marked the moment with a round of applause.</p>

<h3><strong>Key Takeaways</strong></h3>
<ul> <li>Vint Cerf, 83, will retire next week as Google’s vice president and chief internet evangelist, a role he has held since 2005.</li> <li>With Robert Kahn, Cerf co-designed TCP/IP in the 1970s, work honored with the Turing Award and the Presidential Medal of Freedom.</li> <li>Cerf argued that the rise of AI agents will push the industry back toward formal standards and interoperability rather than natural language.</li> </ul>
<p>Cerf’s fingerprints are on the internet itself. Working with Robert Kahn in the 1970s, he developed TCP/IP, the rule set that lets separate computer networks talk to one another. That work earned him a Turing Award, the Presidential Medal of Freedom, and a long list of honorary degrees. He joined Google in 2005 as vice president and chief internet evangelist, a title he kept ever since.</p>
<p>The news came out at the Open Frontier conference, hosted by the Laude Institute, where Cerf appeared by video. Dave Patterson, the UC Berkeley professor known for co-developing RISC processor architecture, recognized him from the stage. “Vint … has been at Google more than 20 years, and he is retiring a week from today, and so I think we ought to give him a round of applause for a relatively good career,” Patterson said, to cheers. Google did not respond to a request for comment.</p>
<h2>A Warning About Talking Machines</h2>
<p>Cerf shared the panel with other builders of durable open source projects, including Patterson, Keras creator Francois Chollet, Tcl author John Ousterhout, and Databricks co-founder Matei Zaharia. Much of the talk circled a worry: advanced AI models are concentrating in a few well-funded labs, unlike the open, decentralized internet Cerf’s protocols made possible.</p>
<p>Cerf offered a prediction. As software agents start acting on their own and coordinating with each other, he expects the industry to swing back toward shared standards. “The agentic model of AI, with multiple agents from multiple sources interacting with each other, is going to force composability, and a requirement for interoperability and standardization,” he said. That view matters as developers lean on <a href="https://www.technology.org/2025/06/26/google-introduces-gemini-cli-open-source-ai-agent-transforms-terminal-experience/">open-source agent tooling</a> and Google puts <a href="https://www.technology.org/2026/05/20/google-io-2026-cheaper-gemini-search-agents/">autonomous agents into Search</a>.</p>
<p>He also doubts plain language will do the job between machines. “I don’t think English is going to be the best choice. There’s a flexibility in it, but there’s ambiguity, and I think precision for interagent interaction is going to be very, very important. An agent really needs to be sure the other agent understands what it is that they just agreed to do together,” Cerf said. Then he reached for a memory. “Remember the old telephone game where you wish you’d whispered in somebody’s ear and then by the time it got to 10 people away the message was totally different? Imagine a bunch of agents talking to each other in natural language, you know, that’s kind of terrifying.” The concern grows sharper as <a href="https://www.technology.org/2026/01/29/googles-new-chrome-ai-agent-browses-the-web-for-you-while-you-sit-back/">agents take on real tasks in the browser</a>.</p>
<h2>The Best-Dressed Man in the Room</h2>
<p>The panel had a lighter beat too. Patterson recalled meeting Cerf as a grad student in the 1970s. “He’s always been the best dressed computer scientist I’ve ever met,” he said. “My memory of Vint is that he came as a grad student with a shirt and tie in the ’70s.”</p>
<p>Cerf owned it. “It absolutely is true,” he replied. “I even had a vest, and for some reason I always wanted to stick out, and instead of having long hair, and something in my nose, I thought just dressing differently was one way to do it.”</p>
<h2>Why the Prediction Already Has Legs</h2>
<p>Cerf’s forecast is not abstract. The industry has spent the past year building common rules for agents to work together across vendors. Google handed its Agent2Agent protocol to a neutral open source body, giving it <a href="https://www.forbes.com/sites/janakirammsv/2025/06/25/key-tech-firms-unite-as-google-donates-a2a-to-linux-foundation/">vendor-neutral governance now backed by more than 100 companies</a>. Anthropic’s Model Context Protocol took a similar route, and both now anchor <a href="https://www.technology.org/2026/01/05/ais-reality-check-2026-marks-the-end-of-empty-promises/">the standard plumbing for AI agents</a>. His point about precision over ambiguity maps neatly onto that work.</p>
<p>His exit carries a wider meaning. Cerf spent decades pressing for openness and interoperability, and he leaves just as the network’s biggest fights shift from getting people connected to deciding who controls the connection. That tension ran through <a href="https://techcrunch.com/2026/06/30/the-father-of-the-internet-is-finally-retiring/">the discussion where his retirement came to light</a>, a fitting last note for a founder-era voice. The internet he helped wire together will keep running long after he logs off, though the question of who writes its next rules is very much open.</p>
