<h2 data-type="text">Why the stars—and the math—won’t align for space compute anytime soon</h2>
<figure><picture><source srcset="https://spectrum.ieee.org/media-library/globe-wrapped-in-multicolored-pins-and-connecting-lines-symbolizing-global-networks.png?id=67007490&amp;width=3600&amp;height=3531 3x, https://spectrum.ieee.org/media-library/globe-wrapped-in-multicolored-pins-and-connecting-lines-symbolizing-global-networks.png?id=67007490&amp;width=2400&amp;height=2354 2x, https://spectrum.ieee.org/media-library/globe-wrapped-in-multicolored-pins-and-connecting-lines-symbolizing-global-networks.png?id=67007490&amp;width=1200&amp;height=1177 1x"/><img alt="Globe wrapped in multicolored pins and connecting lines, symbolizing global networks" src="https://spectrum.ieee.org/media-library/globe-wrapped-in-multicolored-pins-and-connecting-lines-symbolizing-global-networks.png?id=67007490&amp;width=1200&amp;height=1177"/></picture><figcaption>Edmon de Haro</figcaption></figure>
<p>“The lowest-cost place to put AI will be in space, and that will be true within two years, maybe three at the latest,” <a href="https://spectrum.ieee.org/tag/spacex">SpaceX</a> founder <a href="https://spectrum.ieee.org/tag/elon-musk">Elon Musk</a> told the World Economic Forum in Davos this past January, as his company was <a href="https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1181412/000162828026036936/spaceexplorationtechnologi.htm">preparing to go public</a>.</p>
<p>Later that month, SpaceX filed an application with the Federal Communications Commission for an orbital data center constellation of up to 1 million <a href="https://spectrum.ieee.org/tag/satellites">satellites</a> in <a href="https://spectrum.ieee.org/tag/low-earth-orbit">low Earth orbit</a>, 500 to 2,000 kilometers above Earth. And just three days before the <a href="https://spectrum.ieee.org/tag/ipo">IPO</a>, he discussed some initial design specifications for a new <a href="https://x.com/SpaceX/status/2064099405758906727">AI-1 satellite data center</a> in a video interview.</p>
<p>Musk is prone to hyperbole when it comes to timelines. Full <a href="https://techcrunch.com/2025/01/30/elon-musk-reveals-elon-musk-was-wrong-about-full-self-driving/">self-driving cars by 2017</a>. <a href="https://washingtonian.com/2026/02/12/how-elon-musks-sci-fi-hyperloop-failed/">First human mission to Mars in 2024</a>. <a href="https://washingtonian.com/2026/02/12/how-elon-musks-sci-fi-hyperloop-failed/">Ten thousand Optimus humanoid robots by the end of 2025</a>. Et cetera. For orbital <a href="https://spectrum.ieee.org/tag/data-centers">data centers</a>, which he says will be a cost-effective alternative to terrestrial data centers within three years, the math won’t make sense for several years, if ever.</p>
<p>Consider this: There are roughly <a href="https://satfleetlive.com/blogs/how-many-satellites-in-orbit/">14,500 active satellites in orbit</a>. Musk’s <a href="https://spectrum.ieee.org/tag/starlink">Starlink</a> constellation accounts for about <a href="https://spacenexus.us/blog/how-many-satellites-in-space-2026">two thirds of those</a>. Both the launch cadences and satellite-manufacturing capacity would have to scale up astronomically to deploy a million orbital data center satellites.</p>
<p>For context, there have been <a href="https://planet4589.org/space/gcat/data/derived/launchlog.html">roughly 7,000 orbital launches in all of human history</a>. To loft 1 million satellites into low Earth orbit on SpaceX’s Starship, which is designed to carry up to 60 satellites per vehicle, would require 16,666 launches exclusively devoted to satellite deployments. Considering that SpaceX launched a record 165 orbital missions in 2025, even at 10 times that cadence, it would take a decade. And how long would it take to build 1 million satellites, given Starlink’s <a href="https://www.advanced-television.com/2026/04/13/analyst-spacex-making-340-satellites-per-month/">current pace of around 4,000 per year</a> and a generous tenfold increase in capacity? Short of a manufacturing revolution, try 25 years.</p>
<p>The reality is that the vision of massive constellations of orbital data centers is nowhere close to being realized.</p>
<p>As this month’s cover story, “<a href="https://spectrum.ieee.org/orbital-data-centers-heat">Why Orbital Data Centers Are So Hard</a>” by <a href="https://www.abiresearch.com/staff/analysts/andrew-cavalier">Andrew Cavalier of ABI Research</a>, makes clear, the reality is that the vision of massive constellations of orbital data centers is nowhere close to being realized.</p>
<p>Dina Genkina, <em>IEEE Spectrum</em>’s computing and hardware editor, put the idea into perspective: “Starcloud (a startup that has applied to the <a href="https://spectrum.ieee.org/tag/fcc">FCC</a> for an 88,000 orbital data center satellite constellation) <a href="https://spectrum.ieee.org/nvidia-h100-space">sent one Nvidia H100 GPU in space so far</a>. Their radiator was too weak to let the chip run at full power.”</p>
<p>As Cavalier shows, cooling even a single <a href="https://spectrum.ieee.org/tag/nvidia">Nvidia</a> H100 GPU in space is difficult: It draws 700 watts, which will require 1.4 square meters of radiator at 60 °C. A 40-kilowatt rack of <a href="https://spectrum.ieee.org/tag/servers">servers</a> will need an 80-m² radiator; a 100-megawatt data center will require 2,500 of those radiators. Some astronomers are understandably concerned that a million satellites with giant radiative wings would blot out the stars.</p>
<p>So if the economics doesn’t make sense, if the chips are at the mercy of the radiative ravages of space, and if humanity will lose its view of the stars, not to mention increasing the risk of triggering the <a href="https://spectrum.ieee.org/tag/kessler-syndrome">Kessler syndrome</a>, why are the hyperscalers hyping orbital data centers?</p>
<p>Genkina offered the obvious answer: sweet, sweet moolah. “The Elon Musk part of it is honestly genius because he’s got xAI building the data centers, SpaceX sending them to space, and <a href="https://spectrum.ieee.org/tag/tesla">Tesla</a> building solar panels,” Genkina says. “It’s almost like he’s paying himself.”</p>
<h3 data-role="headline"> Two Analyst’s Views of SpaceX’s Proposed AI1 Data Center Satellite </h3>
<p><strong><a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/piercemichaelj/">Michael Pierce</a>, Principal at Technology Strategy Partners</strong></p>
<p>Musk’s timelines are notoriously overly ambitious, but I think SpaceX’s orbital data centers might reach cost parity with terrestrial data centers in 5 to 10 years. The Starlink laser-link network already exists as the communication backbone for any SpaceX compute constellation, and that infrastructure is what no new entrant can replicate quickly. The chip-agnostic payload design probably reflects their disclosed difficulty securing AI silicon as much as any modularity philosophy. My view is that the only realistic near-term application is a SpaceX mega-constellation for inference. Training workloads likely cannot tolerate the synchronization and latency constraints of a distributed orbital system.</p>
<p>Our <a href="https://t-s-partners.com/whitepapers/">report</a> analyzed the market from the integrator’s vantage point, but AI1 is what it looks like when one player has assembled all the necessary advantages simultaneously. The question is whether the terrestrial data center industrial base will degrade or improve on economics. I don’t have insight into SpaceX’s internal costs, as opposed to public pricing, on all their components, so it’s hard to say if they’ll completely dominate or not. Even if they are not cost competitive with terrestrial data centers for another 5 to 10 years, it may simply be faster to get new compute that just happens to be in space.</p>
<p><strong><a href="https://matthasan.com/">Matt Hasan</a>, AI strategist and independent consultant</strong></p>
<p>My initial view is that AI1 does not fundamentally change the rationale for space-based data centers as much as it changes the timeline and scale. The underlying drivers remain the same: escalating AI compute demand, growing power constraints on terrestrial grids, and the desire to colocate energy generation with computation.</p>
<p>What AI1 does signal is that the concept is beginning to move from theoretical discussion toward engineering and capital allocation decisions. The announcement adds credibility to the idea that hyperscale computing infrastructure may eventually expand beyond terrestrial constraints rather than simply competing for increasingly scarce grid capacity on Earth.</p>
<p>That said, significant economic and technical questions remain. Launch costs, maintenance, hardware replacement cycles, <a href="https://spectrum.ieee.org/tag/thermal-management">thermal management</a>, latency-sensitive workloads, and overall system economics will ultimately determine whether space-based data centers become a mainstream extension of AI infrastructure or remain a niche capability for specialized applications. The key development is not that these questions have been resolved, but that major industry players now appear willing to invest resources toward answering them.</p>
