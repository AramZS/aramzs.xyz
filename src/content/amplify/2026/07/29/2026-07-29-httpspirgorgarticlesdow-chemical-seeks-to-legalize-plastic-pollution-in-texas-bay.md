<p>Plastic pellets are small bits of plastic that serve as the building blocks of countless items we use every day, including plastic bags, styrofoam cups and disposable utensils. For years, Dow Chemical’s Seadrift facility in Texas has been <a href="https://environmentamerica.org/texas/center/articles/sued-for-plastic-pollution-dow-asks-texas-regulators-for-permission-to-discharge-pellets/">illegally discharging</a> them into the San Antonio Bay.</p>
<p>This routine, illegal practice of dumping plastic into the environment is making plastic pollution worse. But instead of adjusting to abide by current rules, Dow Chemical just asked Texas regulators to <a href="https://environmentamerica.org/texas/articles/sued-for-plastic-pollution-dow-asks-texas-regulators-for-permission-to-discharge-pellets/">change the language</a> of its wastewater permit to make their discharge of plastic pellets legal.</p>
<p>Americans experience too much plastic pollution already. Every 16 hours, <a href="https://environmentamerica.org/center/resources/trash-in-america-2/">enough plastic</a> is thrown away to fill the Dallas Cowboys football stadium, and much of that is single-use plastic that’s only used for a few minutes before polluting the environment for centuries.</p>
<p>If state regulators greenlight Dow’s proposal, they will be allowing even more plastic pollution—and setting a dangerous precedent that might lead to more pollution across the country.</p>
<p><b>For Americans’ health and waterways, that would be a step in the wrong direction.</b></p>
<h3><b>What are plastic pellets?</b></h3>
<p>Plastic pellets, sometimes called “nurdles,” are little pieces of plastic that are the building blocks for common products—from disposable water bottles to utensils to food containers. Because manufacturers work with vast volumes of them, and because they’re so small and so often moved, huge quantities get spilled or dumped directly into the environment.</p>
<p>In fact, plastic pellets contribute an estimated <a href="https://www.pew.org/-/media/assets/2020/10/breakingtheplasticwave_mainreport.pdf">10 trillion</a> plastic particles to the oceans each year.</p>
<p>Our national research partners at U.S. PIRG Education Fund are working to document the scope of the problem. In the recent <a href="https://publicinterestnetwork.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/International-Plastic-Pellet-Count-Report-2026.pdf">2026 International Plastic Pellet Count</a>, participants collected 1,073,433 pellets across 249 sites in 6 countries. By far the most—over 1 million—were found in Texas, near Dow’s Seadrift plant. In general, the most pellets were found near sites that use, produce or transport these pellets.</p>
<p>That’s why Dow’s move to loosen regulation is a bad idea—and a snapshot of a larger problem.</p>

<h3><b>Plastic pellets pollute our waterways and harm our health</b></h3>
<p>The health and safety consequences of plastic pellets are widespread and severe. They pollute ecosystems, spread toxic contaminants and break down into harmful microplastics that turn up practically everywhere.</p>
<p>Each year, millions of pellets wash up on shorelines around the world. Others are accidentally eaten by animals, who often mistake them for food. Pellets both contain and absorb toxic chemicals, and since the proportion of these toxics increases as they <a href="https://pirg.org/media-center/new-report-international-plastic-pellet-count-finds-more-than-a-million-tiny-nurdles-in-waterways/">move up the food chain</a>, they eventually pose a threat to human food supply.</p>
<p>Plastic pellets also act as “<a href="https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2019/mar/11/pathogens-hitchhiking-on-plastics-could-carry-cholera-from-india-to-us">bacterial rafts</a>,” meaning they can transmit diseases across long distances as they drift around the ocean. One <a href="https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2019/mar/11/pathogens-hitchhiking-on-plastics-could-carry-cholera-from-india-to-us">study</a> concluded that nurdles had the potential to carry pathogens such as cholera or E. coli between places as disparate as India and the U.S.</p>
<p>What’s more, these pellets gradually break down into smaller and smaller pieces until they become microplastics, which have been linked to a <a href="https://pirg.org/articles/heres-how-microplastics-are-harming-our-health/">host of health harms</a>, including reproductive issues and digestive damage. These microplastics have turned up literally everywhere people have looked—from the <a href="https://www.nsf.gov/news/researchers-discover-microplastics-all-ocean-depths">ocean’s deepest point</a> to the top of <a href="https://www.nationalgeographic.com/environment/article/microplastics-found-near-everests-peak-highest-ever-detected-world-perpetual-planet">Mount Everest</a>.</p>
<h3><b>What’s being done to address plastic pellet pollution? </b></h3>
<p>Understanding and publicizing the scope of the plastic pellet problem is an important step toward fixing it. But this issue demands more than just comprehension: We also need stronger rules in place to prevent this pollution in the first place.</p>
<p>Some states are there already. California, for instance, has strict laws regulating plastic pellet discharge, and Colorado recently joined the list by <a href="https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/SB26-016">passing</a> the Plastic Pellet Free Waters Act, which will go into effect in August 2027.</p>
<p>Proposals to follow suit have emerged in Virginia, South Carolina, Illinois, and, importantly, Texas. Strengthening discharge laws in these states would make a big impact on plastic pollution across the country, pave the way for other states to do the same and make it easier to pass stronger federal regulations.</p>
<p>Enforcement actions such as lawsuits can also be effective ways to oppose plastic pellet pollution. In 2023, <a href="https://environmentamerica.org/pennsylvania/media-center/pennenvironment-three-rivers-waterkeeper-settle-plastic-pollution-lawsuit-against-styropek-usa/">PennEnvironment sued</a> plastic manufacturer Styropek over their plastic pellet discharges in Pennsylvania—and won. Styropek paid a $2.6 million dollar settlement, and is now held to a strict zero-discharge policy.</p>

<h3><b>Take action on plastic pellet pollution </b></h3>
<p>Allowing Dow Chemical to loosen regulations on their plastic pollution would seriously harm Texans’ health and waterways. That’s a bad idea, point blank.</p>
<p>But the situation also clarifies a deeper issue:<b> It’s absurd that we’re producing all this plastic in the first place. </b>It makes no sense for water bottles and utensils we use for five minutes to pollute our environment for 500 years. And it also makes no sense to spill trillions of plastic pellets into the environment in the process of manufacturing those items.</p>
<p>Prohibiting the discharge of plastic pellets is a vital move. Strengthening laws and holding polluters accountable are both essential too. And we should also strive to reduce plastic use, because the economy of the future isn’t just zero-discharge—it’s free of unnecessary plastics altogether.</p>
<p>The first step toward that future is to convince your governor to prohibit plastic pellet discharge in your state. <b>Tell your governor: Act now on plastic pellet pollution.</b></p>
