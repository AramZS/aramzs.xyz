<p><em>Spoilers follow for Christopher Nolan’s adaptation of </em><a href="https://www.vulture.com/article/the-odyssey-is-a-natural-end-point-for-christopher-nolan.html">The Odyssey</a>.</p>
<p>You could call Christopher Nolan’s last three films his “society is fucked” trilogy.</p>
<p>In <a href="https://www.vulture.com/article/tenet-is-a-backward-movie-for-an-upside-down-world.html"><em>Tenet</em></a><em>,</em> warriors from the future punish the present because their world is ravaged by climate change caused by us, their ancestors. (Valid.) <a href="https://www.vulture.com/article/oppenheimer-review-a-tragedy-of-operatic-grandeur.html"><em>Oppenheimer</em></a> is about living with the awareness that your life’s work will make it easier for governments to kill millions. In <em>The Odyssey,</em> Odysseus (<a href="https://www.vulture.com/article/matt-damon-explains-odysseuss-relationship-with-athena.html">Matt Damon</a>) serves in the Trojan war, and his laborious journey home highlights how imperialism makes villains out of us all, no matter how good our intentions. But more specifically, Nolan’s <a href="https://www.vulture.com/article/christopher-nolan-on-making-the-odyssey-feel-intimate.html">adaptation of Homer’s poem</a> pinpoints how pervasively violence against women is a part of civilization’s moral decay. And it’s Lupita Nyong’o’s dual performance as twin sisters Helen (of Troy) and Clytemnestra, queens of their city-states and victims of their husbands, that clarify the way women are bartered, owned, and abused. Nolan is <a href="https://www.vulture.com/article/circe-is-the-best-female-character-nolan-has-ever-written.html">not always the best at writing female characters</a>, but here, he taps into an elemental anger that Nyong’o brings to exquisitely painful life.</p>
<p>Nolan’s <em>The Odyssey </em>doesn’t show how Helen, wife of Sparta’s King Menelaus (Jon Bernthal) and sister-in-law to Mycenae’s King Agamemnon (Benny Safdie), was taken from Sparta to Troy; that’s the <em>Iliad</em>’s problem. Was she abducted? Did she go willingly? We do know that whatever happened to Helen was then used by the men around her — not to defend her but to chase their own glory; not for Helen’s virtue but for better trading routes. <em>The Odyssey</em> doesn’t beat around the bush with this. Early in the film’s first act, Odysseus explains Agamemnon’s opportunism to his wife, Penelope (Anne Hathaway), remarking that the king wouldn’t “let it pass, ever” (it being the seeming insult to his brother, Menelaus). Even worse, Agamemnon sacrifices his own young daughter with his wife, Helen’s twin sister, Clytemnestra, to the gods for favorable winds to reach Troy.</p>
<p>Agamemnon in his ominous burnt-bronze armor, carrying his daughter toward a cliff and away from Nyong’o’s sobbing Clytemnestra, is one of the film’s ugliest images. The king looks nightmarish, his face hidden and turned away from his child, but Nyong’o’s raw agony is more memorable. She fights against the guards restraining her, lunges after her husband, and tries to reach her daughter. We haven’t heard Nyong’o’s voice at this point in the film, but she’s its first primal expression of the pain this war will cause for everyone involved in it.</p>
<p>Before we return to Clytemnestra, <em>The Odyssey</em> presents Helen, another woman wounded by the actions of her husband. Their symmetry extends to their husbands’ disregard. When Odysseus and Penelope’s son, Telemachus (Tom Holland), visits Sparta for information about his father, he’s invited to sit with Menelaus during a wedding feast for his and Helen’s daughter. As Telemachus takes his seat, Menelaus introduces Helen, her face a gorgeous profile — before he grabs her chin and turns her to Telemachus. The other side of her face, the side that Menelaus can’t see when he’s eating at this table, is heavily scarred, and Menelaus turns her face back and forth like he’s assessing his own handiwork. Maybe she got injured during the war. But Menelaus’ body language, the swagger with which he grabs Helen, feels like an admission that he punished his wife after her return. Helen’s face, in his estimation, could launch “maybe just 500 ships” now, and Nyong’o is cool, detached, and furious in response.</p>
<p>Nyong’o plays Helen like she’s letting her husband manhandle her now because it’s happened a million times before. But she also plays her like a woman who won’t give him the pleasure of a reaction; Helen looks at Menelaus like he’s an insect crawling across her dinner plate. She rarely speaks, but when she does, her voice is staccato crisp and detached, like she’s elevated herself above her husband’s earthly sins — until Menelaus dares call their war “righteous.” Then she reaches a breaking point. “Tell him about your brother … the righteous hero Agamemnon,” she commands Menelaus, her tone mocking and disgusted. Nyong’o’s Helen can only survive in her husband’s prison if she adopts an above-it-all affect, which is also why it’s so heartbreaking when she finally drops the act and steals a quiet moment with Telemachus before he leaves to return to Ithaca to set the record straight. She asks Telemachus to pass an apology on to Penelope: “I’m sorry for Troy and for everything that has happened in my name.” Nyong’o’s voice is beseeching as she grasps at Holland. Helen doesn’t want to be Helen of Troy; she probably doesn’t even want to be Helen of Sparta. Men have claimed her name and her story, and she needs someone to understand how violating, and unwanted, this is. (The real-life racists who leveled vitriol at Nyong’o for her casting as Helen are among those men using the figure of Helen for their own ends. In their case, those are demanding a submissive, lily-white hegemony and rejecting their own irrelevance.)</p>
<p><em>The Odyssey</em> is built on a pattern of girls’ fear and women’s dehumanization, and the two sisters embody how men’s violence is breaking apart the world. The thuggish suitors dining in Odysseus and Penelope’s castle see the queen as a prize, not a human being. The daughter of the Laestrygonians, whose scream summons the gigantic knights, is horrified to see men from the sea. <a href="https://www.vulture.com/article/samantha-morton-played-circe-like-shed-met-odysseus-before.html">Circe</a> transforms Odysseus’ surviving soldiers into pigs because she’s met warriors before and she knows who they are. “They raped and pillaged across the world for you,” she says to Odysseus, with no small amount of contempt. Women and girls in all corners of this world are accustomed to, and even expect, danger, including the priestess of Athena (Zendaya) whom Odysseus’ men behead during their sack of Troy. Which is also why Clytemnestra’s story becomes an instructional element of this film — not for Odysseus to sympathize with Agamemnon but for us to sympathize with Clytemnestra.</p>
<p>When Odysseus visits the underworld, Agamemnon’s shade form explains his “homecoming.” In Agamemnon’s memory, Clytemnestra is as soft as Helen is hard, as fawning as Helen is resentful. Nyong’o presses her body against Safdie’s Agamemnon as they walk together through Mycenae; she kneels before him to anoint him with oil on their bed; her cleavage is exposed and her gaze is warm. This fantasy of the obedient wife becomes Agamemnon’s undoing. When Clytemnestra stabs her husband to death, she drops the gentle façade and screams just as passionately as she did when watching him kill their daughter. All her emotions that were pent up for the decade-plus he was away come out in that moment. Nyong’o is as frenzied here as Helen was with Telemachus, each woman trying to stand up for herself. Does Nolan go so far as to say Clytemnestra’s actions are justified? Not exactly, but he presents her decision as understandable. Through Nyong’o’s fluidity and range, she reminds us that these sisters are, at their core, human women controlled by human men, reacting purely and vehemently to the circumstances they’re in thanks to their husbands. The twins are the canaries in the coal mine, the victims of the world’s anomie, and they know it. Neither Agamemnon nor Menelaus grasp that their actions toward their wives were immoral. Agamemnon expects his son, Orestes, to avenge him by killing his mother, Clytemnestra, and Menelaus is proud to tell Telemachus that Orestes did just that. When Telemachus is shocked that Orestes “killed his mother,” Menelaus instead insists, “He avenged his father.” Only one parent matters in this world, and it’s not the one who actually carries the child.</p>
<p>Nolan uses Zeus’ law in <em>The Odyssey </em>as a metaphor for the idea that when we stop treating people like they deserve basic kindness, we fray the threads holding civilization together until the whole thing is unraveled like the burial shroud Penelope destroys every night. The strongest indicator of that disintegration is how women are treated, and Nyong’o’s dual performance crystallizes the way women are trapped in a system that can strip them of everything — their looks and their privilege and their motherhood and their agency — whenever a man wants. It was that way then, and it’s that way now. “Our mistakes will once again be forgotten,” Odysseus worries in the film’s final moments as he sails into exile with Penelope. The thing about misogyny, though, is that it wasn’t unique to the Bronze Age. The reactions to Nyong’o’s casting alone can tell you that.</p>
