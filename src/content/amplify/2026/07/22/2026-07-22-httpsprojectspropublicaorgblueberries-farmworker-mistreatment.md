<p>As I wrote those stories, there was something else I couldn’t stop thinking about — something I keep thinking about: Whenever I go to the grocery store, I have no easy way of knowing if the fruits and vegetables I put in my cart are part of the problem I’ve documented.</p>
<figure><video autoplay="" loop="" muted="" playsinline="" src="https://static.propublica.org/projects/graphics/2026-h2a-supply-chain/h2a-supply-chain-farmworkers_mobile.jpg"></video><figcaption>Footage from labor contractor Javier Sanchez Mendoza in 2018 on a blueberry farm. Mendoza was charged in a federal indictment and pleaded guilty to conspiracy to engage in forced labor.</figcaption></figure>
<p>I eat a lot of blueberries, which happen to be the fruit grown most in the state where I live, Georgia, and the state where I tracked these abuses. During the long drives back and forth between my home in Atlanta and my reporting trips in the rural South, I had a lot of time to consider how little we know about the farms that feed us.</p>
<figure><video autoplay="" loop="" muted="" playsinline="" src="https://static.propublica.org/projects/graphics/2026-h2a-supply-chain/h2a-supply-chain-road_mobile.jpg"></video><figcaption>Max Blau/ProPublica</figcaption></figure>
<p>I struggled with the disconnect between the problems on America’s farms and the limited awareness of those problems in most American households. I felt that disconnect in my own kitchen, every time I reached for the berries in my fridge.</p>
<figure><video autoplay="" loop="" muted="" playsinline="" src="https://static.propublica.org/projects/graphics/2026-h2a-supply-chain/h2a-supply-chain_food-washing-blueberries.jpg"></video></figure>

<p>I wanted to know: With enough digging, would it be possible to determine if the blueberries I’m buying come from farms with a record of mistreating workers?</p>
<p>Federal regulators don’t require blueberry brands to disclose on their packaging any information about the individual farms they buy from. So I had to spend time — a <em>lot</em> of time — trying to figure that out.</p>
<p>I started by looking at labels on the blueberry pints in my grocery stores.</p>
<p>I snapped photos. I researched the brands. I read government records.</p>

<p>If you look closely, you’ll see that the pints often list the state where the berries are grown or the city they’re distributed from. But not the farm itself.</p>
<figure><video autoplay="" loop="" muted="" playsinline="" src="https://static.propublica.org/projects/graphics/2026-h2a-supply-chain/h2a-supply-chain-label.jpg"></video><figcaption>Mauricio Rodríguez Pons/ProPublica</figcaption></figure>
<p>As it turns out, the pints sold by a specific brand aren’t always sourced to a single farm. To keep up with demand, and to help keep prices low, brands buy berries from a whole bunch of farms and sell them under a single label.</p>
<figure><video autoplay="" loop="" muted="" playsinline="" src="https://static.propublica.org/projects/graphics/2026-h2a-supply-chain/h2a-grocery.jpg"></video><figcaption>Illustration by Andrew B. Myers for ProPublica</figcaption></figure>
<p>That makes it even harder to know if the berries you’re buying come from farms where workers were harmed.</p>
<figure><video muted="" playsinline=""><source src="https://static.propublica.org/projects/graphics/2026-h2a-supply-chain/max-interview/h2a-supply-chain_max-04.1.mp4" type="video/mp4"/></video></figure>
<p>That case, <a href="https://projects.propublica.org/h2a-visa-farmworkers-operation-blooming-onion/">which I wrote about at length</a>, revealed that thousands of foreign farmworkers had been illegally charged fees by labor contractors to work in the U.S. Some were forced to pick crops for little to no pay in what prosecutors described as a form of modern-day slavery.</p>
<p>This wasn’t the first case of the abuse of farmworkers at the hands of labor contractors. Or the last. Many of these harms might have been prevented if the workers had received the protections they were promised by the U.S. government. And there have been no substantial reforms to address the abuses, either.</p>
<figure><img alt="A dimly lit red room filled with mattresses stacked vertically." src="https://img.assets-d.propublica.org/v5/images/h2a-mattresses-evidentiary_preview_maxWidth_3000_maxHeight_3000_ppi_72_embedColorProfile_true_quality_95.jpg?crop=focalpoint&amp;fit=crop&amp;fm=webp&amp;fp-x=0.5&amp;fp-y=0.5&amp;h=1419&amp;q=75&amp;w=2000&amp;s=1621c9f1b72af3011f7529890e3837c0"/><figcaption>A dimly lit red room filled with mattresses stacked vertically.</figcaption></figure>

<p>The reason contractors exist in the first place is because farmers struggle to find workers in the U.S.</p>
<p>Many farmers speak only English. So they end up hiring contractors to bring foreign laborers to the U.S. and oversee their work.</p>
<p>That outsourcing can shield farmers from responsibility for — or even awareness of — harm against their workers.</p>
<p>Labor regulators have repeatedly <a href="https://www.propublica.org/article/h-2a-visa-farmworker-exploitation-regulators">failed to make sure that contractors do what the government requires them to do</a>: keep workers safe and pay them what they’re promised.</p>
<figure><video muted="" playsinline=""><source src="https://static.propublica.org/projects/graphics/2026-h2a-supply-chain/max-interview/h2a-supply-chain_max-08.mp4" type="video/mp4"/></video></figure>
<p>Those experts, who study farm labor conditions and food supply chains, said <a href="https://www.propublica.org/article/h-2a-farmworker-visa-safety-suggestions-experts">the gold standard for preventing worker abuse is the Fair Food Program</a>. Under the program, supermarkets and other corporate buyers commit to paying a little more for produce — a cost so negligible, at a few pennies per pound, that one expert said it shouldn’t drive up prices.</p>
<p>For those few pennies, grocers get crops from farms that offer their workers stronger protections.</p>
<figure><img alt="Two farmworkers wear long pants and long sleeves, and bend over a row of tomato plants, with red buckets at their feet. Green tomatoes are scattered on the dirt around them." src="https://static.propublica.org/projects/graphics/2026-h2a-supply-chain/h2a-supply-chain-20251022-Melton-H2A-CMA_3782_maxHeight_3000_maxWidth_3000.jpg"/><figcaption> Pacific Tomato Growers is one of a few dozen farms to participate in the Fair Food Program, which protects workers against abuse and exploitation. Audra Melton for ProPublica</figcaption></figure>
<p>Fruits and vegetables from those farms are labeled with a green sticker of a woman holding a basket of tomatoes. The stickers give consumers like me a clear indication that the workers who picked that produce were treated fairly.</p>
<p>When I’ve found these tomatoes in my grocery stores, they don’t cost measurably more than others without the label.</p>
<figure><img alt="Tomatoes with “Fair Food” stickers in a wooden bowl." src="https://static.propublica.org/projects/graphics/2026-h2a-supply-chain/h2a-supply-chain_food-ffp.jpg"/><figcaption>Tomatoes with “Fair Food” stickers in a wooden bowl.</figcaption></figure>
<p>Dozens of farms <a href="https://www.propublica.org/article/fair-food-program-farmworker-abuse-exploitation">have signed on to the Fair Food Program</a>, and participation in it has been shown to prevent abuse in the fields.</p>
<p>But only a few small blueberry farms participate in the program.</p>
<p>Until that or something else changes, consumers will know virtually nothing about the farms their blueberries come from. And they won’t be able to make choices that could make a difference in the lives of the people who pick them.</p>
<figure><video autoplay="" loop="" muted="" playsinline="" src="https://static.propublica.org/projects/graphics/2026-h2a-supply-chain/h2a-supply-chain-max-eating.jpg"></video></figure>
<p>ProPublica reached out to more than 75 blueberry brands, grocery stores, fast-food chains, farmers, trade groups and labor contractors over the course of reporting this series.</p>
<p>Only one brand responded. Dole wrote in a statement that it requires contract farmers to comply with labor laws to protect “worker welfare.” Dole added it can trace berries back to the farms where they came from but that it has not made that information available to consumers.</p>
<p>ProPublica also reached out to over 30 of America’s largest grocery and fast-food chains to ask why they haven’t joined the Fair Food Program or expanded their limited participation. None of the companies answered questions about the program.</p>
<p>Over two dozen trade groups declined to talk about why so few farmers participate in the program. Some have opposed efforts by regulators to enact protections like the ones that the program requires. They said the changes would “burden the whole industry for a few bad apples” who disregarded the rights of workers.</p>
