<h1>These Drivers Are Taking Extreme Measures to Avoid New Car Technology</h1><h2>Curmudgeonly roadblocks or the last beacons of sanity? These car owners disable alarms and reconfigure dashboards in search of low-tech bliss.</h2><p>Brett Doyle maintains a meticulous spreadsheet of the latest automotive tech—electronic door handles, massive touch screens, cruise control that automatically goes with the flow of traffic.</p><p>It isn’t detailing his dream car. It’s his worst nightmare.</p><p>“I don’t want any of that,” said the computer scientist living in St. Louis, who sees the technology as a pile of problems waiting to happen.</p><p>Doyle is part of a vocal minority fighting the auto industry’s obsession with packing new technology into cars. Call them curmudgeonly roadblocks to progress, or the last beacons of sanity on the road. Either way, they’re pushing back.</p><p>Some, like Doyle, are rejecting new cars altogether, opting to stick with used models or keeping their older cars <a href="https://www.wsj.com/business/autos/americans-are-keeping-their-cars-longer-than-ever-and-remaking-the-auto-industry-c169e494?mod=article_inline">running longer than ever</a>. The lifelong gearhead and his teenage sons maintain the family’s four cars—all bought in the 2010s—so they can stay analog as long as possible.</p><blockquote><img alt="Brett Doyle, with his 2006 Scion tC that he bought used, sees technology as a pile of problems waiting to happen." src="https://images.wsj.net/im-28284928?width=700&amp;height=394"/><p>Brett Doyle, with his 2006 Scion tC that he bought used, sees technology as a pile of problems waiting to happen.</p></blockquote><p>If I get hot in my car and want some air conditioning, I just want to turn a knob and have cold air come out,” Doyle said. “There’s this fatigue of having to learn one more new thing.”</p><p>Others have gotten resourceful. They scour dealerships for models with the fewest bells and whistles. They take to online message boards seeking the most low-tech of modern vehicles. They figure out how to disable alarms and alerts, or stick tape on digital dashboards to mimic old-school buttons.</p><p>It’s not an easy feat. Offering the latest in-car gadgetry has become a game of one-upmanship among automakers, from dashboard-spanning display screens and cars that start with a fingerprint, to automated driving-assistance technology and infrared cameras that scan a driver’s eyes for signs of fatigue.</p><p>Car companies boast it’s exactly what modern buyers crave: More iPhone on wheels, less rumbling, mechanical machine. It can be a boon for them, too. Some features are anchored in subscriptions, aimed at giving automakers a source of recurring revenue.</p><p>Even as the price of a new car soars—now hovering around $50,000—automakers argue buyers are getting more bang for their buck, including more safety and entertainment tech features.</p><p>For many buyers, that’s exactly the problem.</p><p>Karen Kefauver says the new features have her weighing a used car for the first time in her life.</p><blockquote><img alt="Karen Kefauver says she’s weighing buying a used car for the first time in her life." src="https://images.wsj.net/im-38413013?width=700&amp;height=525"/><p>Karen Kefauver says she’s weighing buying a used car for the first time in her life.</p></blockquote><p>Her beef is with the technology designed to help drivers drive. While going down a TikTok rabbit hole on car tech, she was alarmed by one clip in which a woman of similarly short stature said her car decelerated after she briefly slouched. The eye-scanner, she said, mistakenly clocked the woman as asleep.</p><p>“I’m no Luddite,” said Kefauver, a social-media consultant who lives in Santa Cruz, Calif., an hour outside of Silicon Valley. “I don’t want my face and heart rate to be monitored by my car.”</p><p>Nobody expects car companies to abandon the tech race.</p><p>“Your alternative is to have products in 2026 that don’t have state-of-the-art technology,” said Chris Lemley, president of the Sentry Auto Group dealerships outside of Boston.</p><p>He’s accustomed to complaints about things like blind-spot monitoring or rear cameras, but doesn’t see the industry returning to its analog days. Customers, for the most part, want modern features, he said.</p><p>There are some new cars that may appeal to the tech-averse. Automotive-research company Edmunds last year published a list of possibilities, including the Chevrolet Trax. The publication dinged the compact SUV for its lack of driving aids. “But buyers looking for a low-tech car might find that appealing,” it said.</p><p>The Mazda3 also pops up on lists of low-tech cars. The compact car has a screen and modern safety features, but relies on physical buttons and a rotary control knob for a more old-school experience. It even offers the now-rare manual transmission option.</p><p>EV startup Slate Auto is generating buzz with an electric truck so basic it has crank windows and no radio.</p><blockquote><img alt="EV startup Slate Auto has an electric truck with crank windows and no radio." src="https://images.wsj.net/im-95936926?size=1&amp;height=900"/><p>EV startup Slate Auto has an electric truck with crank windows and no radio.</p></blockquote><p>Nissan CEO Ivan Espinosa says the industry’s approach to new technology is a bit backward. Automakers tend to put the techiest features in their priciest models, but buyers of those cars skew older and may be more likely to reject new technology, he said.</p><p>“Sometimes, actually, your entry cars should start adopting these technologies because that’s where the younger customers are trying to go,” he said.</p><p>Ron Burda, 85, recently bought a new <a href="https://www.wsj.com/market-data/quotes/JP/XTKS/7203">Toyota</a> RAV4 and can attest that he hates the driver-assistance technology—but not because he’s older. The Gilbert, Ariz., resident thinks drivers shouldn’t need help staying on the road or avoiding running into things.</p><p>“People need to learn how to drive as they have for 100 years,” he said.</p><p>Burda found the digital speedometer hard to read compared with the physical gauges in his previous car. While the car has an option to switch to an analog-style reading, it took a tech at his dealership about a half-hour to figure out, he said.</p><blockquote><img alt="Ron Burda said he hates the driver-assistance technology of his new Toyota Rav4." src="https://images.wsj.net/im-04570125?size=1&amp;height=900"/><p>Ron Burda said he hates the driver-assistance technology of his new Toyota Rav4.</p></blockquote><p>He’s found a workaround for other annoyances: Sticking pieces of tape to the display so he can find critical buttons by touching them, instead of looking.</p><p>Burda recently wrote a letter to Toyota’s American headquarters recommending a separate model for expert drivers. No warning devices or digital displays required.</p><p>“It would be cheaper,” he said. “And promote better driving skills.”</p><p>The quirkier side of life</p><p>The A-Hed, named for a headline shaped like a capital A, has run on the front page of The Wall Street Journal since 1941.</p>
