<div><div>
<p>In four seasons on <em>Saturday Night Live</em>, Rob Schneider created one memorable character. He was the “makin’ copies” guy, whose gimmick was that he sat by the photocopier in an office and tried to befriend his coworkers by giving them nicknames like “the Steve-meister.” It was deeply tedious and annoying, but that was the point. Even in an era when even a moderately popular <em>SNL</em> sketch could lead to a movie deal (remember <em>It’s Pat</em>, the movie? probably not), no one made a <em>Makin’ Copies </em>movie; Schneider was in <em>Surf Ninjas</em> instead.</p><figure><p><div class="rw-embed-wrapper"><embed src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/h1Fk_mDem4o?feature=oembed" type="video/mp4"/></div></p></figure><p>Schneider also played Sylvester Stallone’s sidekick in 1995’s <em>Judge Dredd</em> and the title character in both <em>Deuce Bigalow: Male Gigolo</em>, in which a fish tank cleaner becomes a sex worker to pay off a debt incurred by an aquarium accident, and its sequel, <em>Deuce Bigalow: European Gigolo</em>. If none of those sound familiar, you might remember him as the guy who shouts “You can do it!” to Adam Sandler’s character in 1998’s <em>The Waterboy</em>, a four-word bit for which Schneider seems to have accepted he will forever be best known. </p>
<p></p>
<p>Until this past Sunday, anyway, when he decided to make “a formal announcement about the great state of Texas.” And the world awaited what that proclamation would be. Perhaps Schneider, who promoted a “Move to Texas” Facebook group for his fellow disenchanted right-wing Californians a few years ago (his pitch: “You can do it!”), would finally be relocating to the Lone Star State? (He currently lives in Arizona.) Or maybe the increasingly MAGA/MAHA-fied actor could be running for office? </p><p>Alas, the answer turned out to be weirder: a 1,100-word screed on X, the everything app, about how and why Texas sucks. The specific target of his ire? Greg Abbott, Dan Patrick, and Ken Paxton—a trio that, the fifth-billed cast member of Netflix’s <em>The Ridiculous Six</em> insisted, was responsible for allowing “the Islamic and Marxist threat” to result in “THE CONQUEST OF TEXAS” (caps his, obviously). To illustrate this point, he included a picture of the trio wearing star-and-crescent blindfolds, presumably generated with AI. </p><p>Schneider fits into a hyperspecific trope that has emerged in recent years: that of the conservative-leaning comedian who romanticizes Texas, finds out it’s not quite the place he (it’s always a he) imagined, and then rants about how Texas has failed him—in Scheider’s case, by way of its leadership, but those before him have bemoaned the state’s broader culture. “It’s a soulless city that should be burned to the ground and everyone that lives here should be summarily executed,” <a href="https://www.chron.com/culture/celebrities/article/joe-rogan-friends-austin-texas-20790258.php" target="_blank">comedian Tim Dillon said of Austin</a> on a podcast with Whitney Cummings. He was disappointed by the live music and the food after following Joe Rogan to the city. Shane Gillis, star of Netflix’s <em>Tires</em> and omnipresent in Bud Light ads during football season, moved to Austin from New York in 2023, dealt with the challenges presented by the electrical grid after a storm left his house without power for three days, and told podcaster Andrew Schulz that “Texas f—ing blows.” Comedian Mark Normand declared in 2025 that “moving to Texas is over.” Perhaps that, in addition to his hallucinations <a href="https://x.com/RobSchneider/status/2081694354238816538?ref_src=twsrc%5Etfw%7Ctwcamp%5Etweetembed%7Ctwterm%5E2081694354238816538%7Ctwgr%5E2bf63f67c9801e22b271b3600380e2b3cbbdc10d%7Ctwcon%5Es1_&amp;ref_url=https%3A%2F%2Fwww.sacurrent.com%2Fnews%2Ftexas-news%2Fcomic-rob-schneider-blames-gov-abbott-other-texas-leaders-for-islamic-and-marxist-takeover-of-state%2F" target="_blank">about sharia law</a>, is part of what’s kept Schneider in Arizona. </p><p>The evidence that the actor, who voices ten different anthropomorphic sea creatures in the English dub of the South Korean animated film <em>The Reef 2: High Tide</em>, presents, is scant. Mostly, he drops a bunch of debunked but ubiquitous far-right talking points: Schneider might well have just transcribed things that were said at the “Save Texas From Radical Islam” conference in January, at which Steve Bannon, Glenn Beck, and some mid-card Texas Republican elected officials <a href="https://www.texasmonthly.com/news-politics/texas-republicans-anti-muslim-midterm-elections/">made similar claims</a>. Mostly, the man who recently had a brief cameo as Tricycle Cowboy in Sandler’s <em>Happy Gilmore 2</em> is concerned about sharia law. “Your state has Sharia courts, Sharia judges, and Sharia patrols—and not one has been shut down,” Schneider insisted in his post. </p><p>Schneider and the other right-wing voices speaking out against “sharia law” don’t bother to actually define the term. Instead, they describe Muslim communities that are choosing to follow the traditional, scripture-based rules of Islam (in the same way that Christian communities attempt to follow the Ten Commandments) as obeying “sharia law,” with the implication that this is the same kind of “sharia law” practiced in an authoritarian theocracy like Iran. </p><p>No matter what the man whose second-most-famous <em>SNL </em>character was the Sensitive Naked Man claims, there are no sharia courts, sharia judges, or sharia patrols in Texas that resemble the latter. <a href="https://www.washingtontimes.com/news/2025/dec/1/shariah-courts-america/?srsltid=AfmBOop9XbQMLPaIxTOGIQP5twXJndyD9XS0jFtKo53hhgC14WWK2IFN" target="_blank">An angsty op-ed</a> from the conservative <em>Washington Times</em> decries the “Shariah courts” that exist in Texas—but it only describes private, voluntary mediation bodies (most notably the Dallas Islamic Tribunal) for Muslims to use to resolve interpersonal and interfamily disputes. Like Christian conciliation groups, Catholic tribunals, or Jewish beth din courts, these have no legal force behind them. They represent neither a “takeover” nor something that involves non-Muslims any more than the goings-on in a church meeting in Midland concern folks in McAllen. </p><p>Those seeking information on “sharia patrols” are stuck with <a href="https://www.houstonchronicle.com/politics/texas/article/greg-abbott-sharia-law-21045297.php" target="_blank">one example</a> of anything even remotely resembling such a thing. A Houston-area imam visited Muslim-owned convenience stores last year to tell owners that he would organize boycotts within the community if they continued selling pork, alcohol, cigarettes, or other items forbidden under Islamic guidance. Like the “sharia courts,” this contained no force of law; it was not much different from, say, Christians boycotting Target or Bud Light. </p><p>Even so, the claim from the man who performed as the Hamster on season six of <em>The Masked Singer</em> that Abbott, Patrick, and Paxton have failed to grandstand on these issues is baffling. Those leaders <em>love</em> grandstanding about them! In response to the Houston imam, <a href="https://www.houstonchronicle.com/politics/texas/article/greg-abbott-sharia-law-21045297.php" target="_blank">Abbott insisted</a> that “Texas law and Texas courts govern those businesses and those neighborhoods” and that “no ranting imam can change that.” The governor added that the Texas Department of Public Safety would be investigating the matter, though what crime the imam is alleged to have committed remained unclear. Paxton, meanwhile, vowed to use his office as attorney general to investigate the Dallas Islamic Tribunal, even as he was forced to note that the organization and the members of the Muslim community it serves have the right to govern themselves; his announcement of the investigation <a href="https://www.texasattorneygeneral.gov/news/releases/attorney-general-ken-paxton-takes-legal-action-part-landmark-investigation-alleged-effort-impose" target="_blank">mostly noted</a> that it would be making sure the org wasn’t telling its members that its decisions were made with “the approval of the Texas Judicial system.” And <a href="https://www.texastribune.org/2026/01/30/dan-patrick-interim-charges-senate-texas-legislature-2027/" target="_blank">back in January</a>, Dan Patrick put “preventing Sharia law” on his list of legislative priorities for the 2027 session. </p><p>All of these men, in other words, are fighting the same nonexistent boogeyman that Schneider, who presumably spent years of his life trying to find financing for <em>Deuce Bigalow: Latin American Gigolo, </em>has insisted they’ve allowed to prosper. “I have put my livelihood, my career, and my life on the line to speak out in an industry that has little interest in supporting conservatives,” Schneider wrote at one point in his post—but the entire thing reads more like an ad for right-wing filmmakers looking to cast a “hey, it’s that guy!” face that might trigger anyone to the left of Stephen Miller. Good luck, Rob. </p><p><em><strong>Editors’ note:</strong> This story has been updated to reflect the fact that Mr. Schneider also appeared in a film called </em>Surf Ninjas<em>. We regret the omission.</em></p>
