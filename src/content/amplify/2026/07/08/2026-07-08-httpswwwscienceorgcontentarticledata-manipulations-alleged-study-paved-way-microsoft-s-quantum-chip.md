<h2>Internal emails from 2021 reveal tensions among researchers hunting for elusive Majorana particle</h2>

<p>A version of this story appeared in Science, Vol 388, Issue 6747.<a data-offset="0,14px" data-original-title="DOWNLOAD PDF" data-toggle="tooltip" href="https://www.science.org/doi/epdf/10.1126/science.ady8077">Download PDF</a></p>
<p>Work relating to Microsoft’s new quantum computing chip has been called into question after an author on the study acknowledged it contained undisclosed data processing, according to emails obtained by <em>Retraction Watch</em> and <em>Science</em>.</p>
<p>The findings, published in 2017 in <em>Nature Communications</em> by a Microsoft-funded lab at the Delft University of Technology, were a key step in demonstrating how an ultrathin “nanowire” might be useful as a bit in a quantum chip, just as a transistor is used in a conventional computer. Last month, the paper <a href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-58136-3">received an editorial expression of concern</a> and extensive corrections to address gaps in data following an investigation by the journal.</p>
<p>But two of its authors say the correction is insufficient and the study should be retracted. Henry Legg, a quantum physicist at the University of St. Andrews, also has concerns. “This is the latest in a long line of issues we have seen with Microsoft-funded research,” he says. “It seems when they turn up with funding, standard research practices can go out the window.”</p>
<p>For years, researchers have been trying to build quantum computers that could, among many possible applications, <a href="https://www.science.org/content/article/quantum-computers-could-soon-speed-development-novel-materials-catalysts-and-drugs">dramatically speed up simulations in chemistry</a>, leading to the discovery of drugs and materials. But the delicate quantum bits, or qubits, in these devices are <a href="https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.369.6500.130">prone to errors</a>. Microsoft has pursued a dark-horse qubit technology made from so-called Majorana quasiparticles, which consist not of actual particles, but of a pattern of electron behavior. In theory, the qubits would be durable and immune to errors. Unpaired electrons at the end of the nanowires would conjure up the Majorana quasiparticles, <a href="https://www.science.org/content/article/ghostly-quasiparticle-rooted-century-old-mystery-unlock-quantum-computings-potential">the elusive target of a much longer quest in physics</a>, and function as the quantum bits.</p>
<p>But the field has suffered many setbacks. <em>Nature</em> <a href="https://retractionwatch.com/2022/04/24/authors-retract-second-majorana-paper-from-nature/">retracted two papers</a> from the Delft lab claiming to find evidence of the Majorana particles. In 2022, <em>Science</em> <a href="https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adf7575">retracted another Majorana paper</a> from the University of California, Davis for data irregularities, and several others have received <a href="https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aav3392">expressions of concern</a> or faced <a href="https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-021-00954-8">replicability issues</a>.</p>
<p>That didn’t stop Microsoft from announcing in February that it had <a href="https://news.microsoft.com/source/features/innovation/microsofts-majorana-1-chip-carves-new-path-for-quantum-computing/">cobbled together a quantum chip</a> of eight qubits, each made from two nanowires. The authors said they had found “peer-reviewed confirmation” of Majoranas, claims that have <a href="https://www.science.org/content/article/debate-erupts-around-microsoft-s-blockbuster-quantum-computing-claims">faced a recent storm of criticism</a>. A spokesperson for Microsoft says the company is “very confident in our approach and stand[s] behind the results in our papers published by the Microsoft team.”</p>
<p>The 2017 paper now under fire represents an early step in demonstrating the viability of nanowires as qubits. It claimed to show electrons could travel smoothly through nanowires without much scattering—disorder that could otherwise lead to false positives in searching for Majoranas.</p>
<p>But co-author Vincent Mourik had concerns when he saw drafts of the paper. Mourik, now at the Jülich Research Center, found signs that data had been cherry-picked to support the paper’s conclusions and began to question his co-authors more than a year before it was published.</p>
<p>Mourik and a colleague <a href="https://zenodo.org/records/6325378">published a critique</a> of the paper in 2022. Among their concerns: Twenty-one nanowire junctions were tested, but data from only four were included in the published paper.</p>
<p>In April 2021, Mourik sought answers from corresponding author Önder Gül, asking in an email: “Did you know about these undisclosed data manipulations prior to publication of the paper?” Gül responded, 1 day later: “Yes, I did.” Gül, who is still at Delft, told Mourik he didn’t share the information “because I didn’t think it was important at the time.”</p>
<p>In response to questions, Gül now says, “While some of these steps were not described in the original publication, I do not view them as ‘data manipulation.’”</p>
<p>In a 2023 exchange, co-author Michael Wimmer told Mourik he was “very appalled by the undisclosed data processing done by Hao and Önder,” referring to Gül and the study’s other corresponding author Hao Zhang, who did not respond to a request for comment.</p>
<p>Senior author Leo Kouwenhoven, a topological qubit pioneer who has led the Delft lab for years, says the allegations of intentional data manipulation “are unsubstantiated and we rejected them in the postpublication review process.” Kouwenhoven, who left Microsoft in 2022 and whose lab no longer receives funding from the company, also notes the authors “have already acknowledged our errors in the data processing and addressed them in our corrections.”</p>
<p>But Mourik says the correction does not go far enough. “They assumed the effect they were trying to prove, and achieved this through massaging of data and theory gymnastics,” he says. He and fellow researcher Kun Zuo <a href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-57863-x">asked to be removed</a> from the paper’s list of authors after publication.</p>
<p>Asked why the journal issued a correction rather than a retraction, a spokesperson from <em>Nature Communications</em> says, “Wider concerns raised regarding the handling of the data were outside the remit of our review.” Asked whether the journal would consider retracting, the spokesperson says, “We consider this matter closed.”</p>
<p><strong>Update, 13 May, 1:55 p.m.:</strong> The first sentence of this story has been changed to reflect that Gül has consistently acknowledged undisclosed data processing but not “data manipulation.”</p>
