title: Managing the pitfalls of private money
---
<p>In recent months, we have learned that the relationship between the US federal government and American science is not as stable as many of us thought it to be. While many grants that were abruptly (and perhaps illegally) cut earlier this year have been restored, the relationship between science and the federal government that has prevailed since the 1950s has shifted. The decades ahead are unlikely to look like the ones of the recent past.</p>
<p>In this context, many scientists and administrators are looking to an expanded role for the private sector in funding academic science. This is not necessarily bad: before the 20th century, most science was pursued by <a href="https://archive.org/details/gentlemenofscien0000morr_z7d9/page/n5/mode/2up">independently wealthy individuals</a> or <a href="https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/S/bo3626633.html">funded by private</a>, <a href="https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/S/bo3683923.html">princely</a>, and <a href="https://www.hup.harvard.edu/books/9780674005365">religious patrons</a>. There are good arguments for diversification of funding sources, not least because the past months have shown the risks involved in relying too heavily on one source.</p>
<p>But, recent revelations about corporate manipulation of science remind us that private sector funding of science is not without its pitfalls, and the public furor over universities’ relationships with Jeffrey Epstein reminds us that we are judged by the company we keep. In this context, it is useful to consider some of the risks associated with private money and how those risks might be reduced.</p>
<p>The most obvious risk is bias. Numerous studies have demonstrated that corporate sponsorship can lead to bias. The textbook example of this is tobacco. Over decades, the tobacco industry sponsored research across academia, including in medical schools and cancer research centers. Post hoc analysis of this work shows that studies sponsored by the industry were less likely to find evidence of harm from tobacco use than studies that were industry-independent. For this reason, the British Medical Journal group will not publish any <a href="https://bmjgroup.com/tobacco-policy-making-a-stand/">research funded by the tobacco industry</a> or by any author with financial links to the industry.</p>
<p>But it’s not just tobacco. A 2007 study of the relationship between funding and findings in nutrition research found that <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17214504/">articles sponsored by food and drink companies were four to eight times more likely to have conclusions favorable to the interest of those companies</a> than those independent of such ties. Numerous studies have shown how bias has been introduced into biomedicine by industry sponsorship, including through corrupt practices such as <a href="https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aec4187">ghost-writing</a> and <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34161371/">non-disclosure agreements</a> in which findings adverse to the funder’s interests may never see the light of day (see, for example, <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12533125/">here</a> and <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30252531/">here</a>).</p>
<p>In theory, a government sponsor could suborn bias, for example by signaling a desired outcome. But, unlike corporate funding, government scientific funding since the end of World War II has been structured to advance science broadly. This is very different than corporate funding seeking to advance the interests of that corporation.</p>
<p>Moreover, bias is not the only concern. Funders can shape the scientific research agenda by advancing some areas of interest at the expense of others, such as fossil fuel funding of carbon capture and storage and solar radiation management, but not carbon pricing systems or energy efficiency, or pharma investing heavily in diseases that afflict the wealthy but not the poor. My own research on the history of oceanography has shown that, <a href="https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/S/bo59258933.html">while the US Navy was in many ways a good patron, it necessarily focused its efforts on issues salient to the Navy mission</a>. In most cases, this meant physical and chemical rather than biological oceanography, which led, in turn, to a scientific vision of the ocean more as a theater of warfare than as an abode of life.</p>
<p>Corporate funding may purposely focus on one problem to distract attention from another, as when <a href="https://www.cdc.gov/lung-cancer/risk-factors/index.html">the tobacco industry promoted investigations of radon and asbestos as causes of lung cancer to divert attention from tobacco as the major cause of that disease</a>. It can also disproportionately shape public debate. A 2018 review by researchers at the University of Sydney found that <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30252531/">corporate sponsorship of research can have an anchoring effect in public debate and public policy</a>, if, by taking the first step in research funding, they disproportionately control how the relevant questions are framed. For example: if an early study claimed that a chemical was safe, then the burden of proof falls on subsequent studies to disprove that claim. If general practitioners began to widely prescribe a drug after an industry-funded clinical trial that showed it was safe and effective, they might keep prescribing it (and patients might keep taking it) even after clinical use or further study refuted the initial study. <a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6603445/">Some industries have developed strategies to reshape entire fields of research</a> through the prioritization of topics that support its policy and legal positions.</p>
<p>And then there is Jeffrey Epstein—the latest and arguably most high profile example of a long line of instances in which scientists have consorted with less than reputable potential funders. The public have been outraged to discover that dozens of academic leaders fraternized with Epstein, even after he was a convicted sex offender. (<a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/04/20/us/epstein-files-harvard-university-faculty.html">One Harvard department chair congratulated him when he was released from jail.</a>) Epstein also influenced the scientific research agenda, with many of <a href="https://www.motherjones.com/politics/2026/02/epstein-emails-eugenics-chomsky-altruism-billionaires/">his preferences having a decidedly neo-eugenic flavor</a>. (<i>The New York Times</i> reported that <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2019/07/31/business/jeffrey-epstein-eugenics.html">his interest in eugenics was “not a secret.”</a>) Scientists’ associations with Epstein don’t just cast doubt on the judgment of the researchers involved, but potentially on science generally.</p>
<p>If biased science delays appropriate action, misleads physicians, or falsely reassures regulators and the public, <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17214504/">this can have dramatic consequences for public health</a>. The editors of the British Medical Journal group remind us that tobacco kills up to two-thirds of long-term users, so the historic delay in understanding the risks led to millions of preventable deaths. Given this, it should be obvious that the academic scientific community must take steps to protect scientific integrity, and in doing so protect public health and maintain public trust. Yet, most universities and research institutes have few if any guidelines on these complex issues.</p>
<p>What might guardrails look like in the academic research sector? A possible model may be found in the existing practice of the Institutional Review Board (IRB).</p>
<p>Academic freedom has never meant that academics could do just anything they wanted, particularly if it involved human subjects. In the United States, this idea was formalized in 1974, under the National Research Act of 1974, which stipulated that <a href="https://www.fda.gov/about-fda/cder-offices-and-divisions/institutional-review-boards-irbs-and-protection-human-subjects-clinical-trials">any scientific work involving human subjects must be subject to internal institutional review</a> for potential risks to those subjects. Using this model, we could imagine a form of “extended IRB,” in which funding is reviewed for all projects—not just those involving human subjects—for conflicts of interest and potential for bias (and maybe also associations with convicted felons). Sponsored research would undergo review not just for protection of human subjects, but also for protection of scientific integrity.</p>
<p>Some scientists may object that constraints on funding sources are a violation of academic freedom, or balk at introducing yet another layer of bureaucracy. Others may be leery of inviting inappropriate external interference in the research enterprise (especially in the current political moment). Thus, it is important that any proposed constraints be developed and implemented by faculty and understood as a form of internal university self-governance.</p>
<p>Faculty governance is generally understood to cover hiring, promotion, and curricula, but the concept could be expanded to include faculty oversight of external funding. Any review committee should be structured to reflect the interests of the university as a whole—not just the interests of the individual investigator, department, or division—and perhaps include community members to ensure that the public interest is not neglected (particularly at public institutions, but not only, since private institutions also rely heavily on public funds for research).</p>
<p>Extended IRB would not remedy the problems of undue private influence on the academic research agenda; for that one would need an additional mechanism. But again, we have models. Some universities have a policy of “<a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2013/10/25/us/if-you-build-it-on-public-university-campuses-1-percent-must-be-public-art.html">1% for public art</a>,” in which a small percentage of the budget for construction projects goes to fund campus art. One percent would scarcely be enough to redress research imbalances, but 10% might, particularly if it were put into a fund to which all faculty could apply. Going further, universities could insist that patrons may not hand-pick the faculty they want to fund, but must contribute their donations to a pool of money for which all faculty could compete on a level playing field.</p>
<p>These are preliminary ideas, but scientists are smart, and—with the help of colleagues in the art, humanities, social sciences and perhaps law and medicine—we should be able to develop workable mechanisms to protect scientific integrity and maintain public trust.</p>
