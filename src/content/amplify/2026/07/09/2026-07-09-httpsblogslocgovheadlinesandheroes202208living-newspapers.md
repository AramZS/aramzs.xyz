<h3>How do newspapers relate to plays? When the play IS a newspaper!</h3>
<p>The <a href="https://www.loc.gov/exhibits/federal-theatre-project/?loclr=blogser">Federal Theatre Project</a> (FTP), part of the Works Progress Administration’s efforts to provide work for unemployed people during the Great Depression, produced over 2,700 stage productions across the nation. Separate divisions within the FTP focused on different types of performances. One of them was the Living Newspaper unit which created plays with scenes that dramatized newspaper articles.</p>
<p>In collaboration with the American Newspaper Guild, the Living Newspaper headquarters in New York City wrote plays to send out to be performed by the regional FTP groups. Current events were transformed from the page to the stage.</p>

<p>In the April 1936 issue of the “<a href="https://lccn.loc.gov/50036535?loclr=blogser">Federal Theater</a>,” the monthly bulletin of the FTP, an article (with no attributed author) describes the Living Newspaper as a story “told with the objectiveness of an Associated Press news report and with the vividness of an acted play” (16).</p>
<p>The process of editing – not writing – the next edition (the next play) was not simple. The article goes on to describe the workflow:</p>
<blockquote><p>The theme is chosen by the managing producer and the Federal Theatre director for New York City, in consultation with the national director. It then goes to round-table discussion, much as a board of editors would discuss the layout of a national news story for tomorrow’s edition.</p> <p>The managing editor, who is also the head dramatist, then breaks down a subject into scenes, indicating as specifically as possible the treatment he has in mind for each scene, and passes this on to the city editor in charge of the research and editing staff.</p> <p>This scenario is then parceled out as assignments to the research men with a dead-line of two or three days to get the material required (16).</p></blockquote>
<p>The headlines would emerge and a story line placed in order. It was then translated into stage terms for exits and entrances, and the ten rewrite men on staff were in charge of putting the facts into dialogue.</p>
<p>All of this was done even before the script got to set and costume designers, light and sound technicians, stage managers and business managers. And in a turn-around time of 4 weeks!</p>
<figure><a href="https://chroniclingamerica.loc.gov/lccn/sn83045462/1938-05-16/ed-1/seq-36/?loclr=blogser"><img alt="Image of a newspaper article titled 'Local Drama Groups. There's a fact for every word in the living newspaper.'" sizes="auto, (max-width: 352px) 100vw, 352px" src="https://blogs.loc.gov/headlinesandheroes/files/2022/08/facts-in-living-nps-snip.jpg" srcset="https://blogs.loc.gov/headlinesandheroes/files/2022/08/facts-in-living-nps-snip-300x110.jpg 300w, https://blogs.loc.gov/headlinesandheroes/files/2022/08/facts-in-living-nps-snip.jpg 666w"/></a><figcaption>“Local Drama Groups,” Evening Star (Washington, D.C.), May 16, 1938.</figcaption></figure>
<p>Accordingly, the subject matter of the Living Newspapers was nonfiction – realistic, current, relevant. The topics had to be widely applicable to the vast majority of the nation. Topics selected were the farming crisis, slum housing issues, public health, and the public and private ownership of utilities. If these topics sound too controversial, check out the plays that were written but not allowed to be produced – “Ethiopia” on Mussolini’s <a href="https://chroniclingamerica.loc.gov/lccn/sn83045462/1935-07-14/ed-1/seq-57/?loclr=blogser">invasion of Ethiopia</a> and “Liberty Deferred” on racial inequality in America.</p>
<p>The topic was always recent and was often traced back through history for scope and context. “<a href="https://www.loc.gov/resource/musftpplaybills.200220862.0/?sp=1&amp;r=-0.542,0.204,1.86,1.125,0?loclr=blogser">Injunction Granted</a>,” a Living Newspaper about worker rights and labor unions, spans 200 years of history. Everything was up-to-the-minute accurate, with some scripts updated hours before the performance! They cited their sources, too, in footnoted scripts and <a href="https://www.loc.gov/resource/musftpplaybills.200221274.0/?sp=2&amp;r=-0.108,-0.036,1.437,0.604,0?loclr=blogser">playbill bibliographies</a>. (More indexed terms and sources can be found in the Card File of the FTP in the <a href="https://findingaids.loc.gov/db/search/xq/searchMfer02.xq?_id=loc.music.eadmus.mu995001&amp;_faSection=overview&amp;_faSubsection=did&amp;_dmdid=?loclr=blogser">Music Division FTP Collection</a>.) Often the playbills were even formatted to look like newspapers, with each scene listed in the playbills corresponding with a news item.</p>
<figure><a href="https://www.loc.gov/item/musftpplaybills.200220862/?loclr=blogser"><img alt="Top half of the front page of the playbill for 'Injunction Granted,' with a photograph of a scene with one person on a platform and a crowd of people looking up at him." sizes="auto, (max-width: 556px) 100vw, 556px" src="https://blogs.loc.gov/headlinesandheroes/files/2022/08/injunction-granted-playbill.jpg" srcset="https://blogs.loc.gov/headlinesandheroes/files/2022/08/injunction-granted-playbill-300x218.jpg 300w, https://blogs.loc.gov/headlinesandheroes/files/2022/08/injunction-granted-playbill.jpg 519w"/></a><figcaption>Front page of the playbill for the July 24, 1936 production of “Injunction Granted.” Music Division.</figcaption></figure>
<p>The Living Newspapers could be considered a time capsule of the 1930s and an avenue to approach the daily news of the time. What headlines were considered important enough to perform on a nation-wide stage? What issues were impactful enough to be relatable to a nation-wide audience? How did the headlines compare across the states? Not only are there reviews and reactions to the performances in newspapers, but also the original headlines.</p>
<figure><a href="https://chroniclingamerica.loc.gov/lccn/sn82006551/1933-06-29/ed-1/seq-1/#date1=1932&amp;sort=date&amp;rows=20&amp;words=act+adjustment+agricultural+agriculture&amp;searchType=basic&amp;sequence=1&amp;index=8&amp;state=&amp;date2=1933&amp;proxtext=Agricultural+adjustment+act&amp;y=0&amp;x=0&amp;dateFilterType=yearRange&amp;page=1&amp;loclr=blogser"><img alt="Newspaper article headline 'Wheat Plan brings cash to farmers for growers this year who take part in adjustment program. First payment made in september.'t " src="https://blogs.loc.gov/headlinesandheroes/files/2022/08/aaa-headline.jpg"/></a><figcaption>“Wheat Plan Brings Cash to Farmers,” The Western News (Libby, MT) June 29, 1933.</figcaption></figure>
<p>“Triple-A Plowed Under,” (sometimes reported with the variant spellings of “Triple-A Ploughed Under” or “AAA Plowed Under”) was the first Living Newspaper that opened in New York on March 14, 1936. This paper showed scenes on the implementation of the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1933 and the farming crisis.</p>
<p>The mixed results that followed the 1933 act came to a point in <a href="https://chroniclingamerica.loc.gov/lccn/sn92070146/1936-01-06/ed-1/seq-1/?loclr=blogser">this January 6, 1936 headline</a>. The opening of the show was just as eventful as the events documented on stage with “one arrested, two evicted” as reported in the Evening Star (D.C.).</p>
<figure><a href="https://chroniclingamerica.loc.gov/lccn/sn83045462/1936-03-15/ed-1/seq-29/?loclr=blogser"><img alt='Two newspaper photos of scenes from the play "Triple-A Plowed Under".' sizes="auto, (max-width: 419px) 100vw, 419px" src="https://blogs.loc.gov/headlinesandheroes/files/2022/08/march-1936-triple-a-pictures.jpg" srcset="https://blogs.loc.gov/headlinesandheroes/files/2022/08/march-1936-triple-a-pictures-300x255.jpg 300w, https://blogs.loc.gov/headlinesandheroes/files/2022/08/march-1936-triple-a-pictures-768x654.jpg 768w, https://blogs.loc.gov/headlinesandheroes/files/2022/08/march-1936-triple-a-pictures.jpg 826w"/></a><figcaption>Scenes from “Triple-A Plowed Under.” “W.P.A. News theater Strikes Broadway,” Evening Star (Washington, D.C.) March 15, 1936.</figcaption></figure>
<p>The Living Newspaper titled “Events of 1935” (also simply titled “1935”) opened on May 6, 1936 and documented a survey of national events. Each scene serves as a headline, featuring the <a href="https://chroniclingamerica.loc.gov/lccn/sn85042243/1936-03-31/ed-1/seq-3/?loclr=blogser">Hauptmann Case</a> (which had just reached a verdict in the spring of 1936) and whether or not the USA would participate in the <a href="https://chroniclingamerica.loc.gov/lccn/sn86063730/1935-12-09/ed-2/seq-5/#date1=1934&amp;index=13&amp;rows=20&amp;words=1935+Olympic&amp;searchType=basic&amp;sequence=0&amp;state=&amp;date2=1937&amp;proxtext=1935+olympics&amp;y=0&amp;x=0&amp;dateFilterType=yearRange&amp;page=1&amp;loclr=blogser">1936 Berlin Olympics.</a></p>

<p>While the Living Newspaper Headquarters in New York wrote the plays for distribution to all of the regional Federal Theatre groups, local FTPs also wrote their own Living Newspapers. One of the playbills included in the <a href="https://www.loc.gov/collections/federal-theatre-project-1935-to-1939/about-this-collection/?loclr=blogser">FTP digital collection</a> is for a play performed in Cleveland, Ohio and is simply titled “The Living Newspaper: a dramatization of world news events.”</p>
<figure><a href="https://www.loc.gov/resource/musftpplaybills.200221027.0?st=gallery?loclr=blogser"><img alt="Front page of a playbill that reads 'Cleveland Repository Theatre presents the Living Newspaper a dramatization of world news events.'" sizes="auto, (max-width: 286px) 100vw, 286px" src="https://blogs.loc.gov/headlinesandheroes/files/2022/08/ohio-living-newspaper.jpg" srcset="https://blogs.loc.gov/headlinesandheroes/files/2022/08/ohio-living-newspaper-206x300.jpg 206w, https://blogs.loc.gov/headlinesandheroes/files/2022/08/ohio-living-newspaper.jpg 375w"/></a><figcaption>Cover of playbill from Cleveland, Ohio. Music Division.</figcaption></figure>
<p>The scene list in the playbill shows the headlines that were most relevant to the local audience: “<a href="https://chroniclingamerica.loc.gov/lccn/sn82015313/1936-01-20/ed-1/seq-9/?loclr=blogser">Silicosis at Gauley Bridge</a>,” “<a href="https://chroniclingamerica.loc.gov/lccn/sn90059182/1936-02-06/ed-1/seq-2/#date1=1933&amp;index=0&amp;rows=20&amp;words=Al+Indictment+Smith&amp;searchType=basic&amp;sequence=0&amp;state=Minnesota&amp;date2=1963&amp;proxtext=al+smith%27s+indictment&amp;y=0&amp;x=0&amp;dateFilterType=yearRange&amp;page=1&amp;loclr=blogser">Al Smith-Senator Robinson Speeches</a>,” and “<a href="https://chroniclingamerica.loc.gov/lccn/sn87062014/1936-02-06/ed-1/seq-1/#date1=1927&amp;index=3&amp;rows=20&amp;words=Cooper+COOPER+Hewitt+HEWITT&amp;searchType=basic&amp;sequence=0&amp;state=&amp;date2=1937&amp;proxtext=cooper+hewitt&amp;y=0&amp;x=0&amp;dateFilterType=yearRange&amp;page=1&amp;loclr=blogser">Cooper Hewitt Sterilization Case</a>.” These headlines may not make into the history classes, but they were important enough at the time to bring a community together for both entertainment and a sense of civic unity.</p>
<p>Accusations of censorship, propaganda, and un-American activities always followed the Living Newspaper productions, and it is debated whether or not the Living Newspaper unit specifically played a role in ending the entire FTP in 1939. “Thus,” <a href="https://chroniclingamerica.loc.gov/lccn/sn82015313/1936-03-30/ed-1/seq-13/#date1=1936&amp;index=0&amp;rows=20&amp;words=censorship+Censorship+denied+Denied&amp;searchType=basic&amp;sequence=0&amp;state=Indiana&amp;date2=1936&amp;proxtext=censorship+is+denied&amp;y=0&amp;x=0&amp;dateFilterType=yearRange&amp;page=1&amp;loclr=blogser">an article in the Indianapolis Times</a> says, “the Living Newspaper has traveled the same rough road as the printed one.” Funding for the project was not renewed in the 1940 relief bill.</p>

<p>The Living Newspapers were an interesting facet of the Federal Theatre Project and hold interest for scholars of history, politics, and theatre.</p>
