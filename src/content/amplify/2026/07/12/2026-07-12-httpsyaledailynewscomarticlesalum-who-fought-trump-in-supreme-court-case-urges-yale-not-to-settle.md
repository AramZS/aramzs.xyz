<h2>A former commissioner of the Federal Trade Commission took her case against the Trump administration to the Supreme Court. Amid settlement talks between University leaders and the federal government, she said Yale has a responsibility to fight back.</h2>
<figure><picture><figure><img src="https://yaledailynews.com/_next/image?url=https%3A%2F%2Fydn-cdn.yaledailynews.com%2Fmedia%2FIMG_4897.jpeg%3F2026-07-11T02%253A08%253A38.021Z&amp;w=1920&amp;q=75"/><figcaption>Courtesy of Rebecca Slaughter</figcaption></figure></picture></figure>
<p>Rebecca Slaughter ’03 LAW ’08 went up against the Trump administration in court — and lost. She thinks Yale should fight back in its ongoing settlement talks with the government.</p>
<p>Slaughter served as a commissioner for the Federal Trade Commission from 2018 until 2025, when she was <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2025/03/18/technology/trump-ftc-fires-democrats.html">fired</a> by President Donald Trump without cause. On June 29, the Supreme Court <a href="https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/25-332_qn12.pdf">ruled</a> 6-3 in Trump v. Slaughter to uphold her firing, overturning 91 years of precedent in an expansion of executive power. FTC commissioners were previously protected by a 1935 Supreme Court <a href="https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep295/usrep295602/usrep295602.pdf">ruling</a> that restricted presidents from firing leaders of <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/06/29/us/politics/independent-agencies-trump.html">independent agencies</a> without direct cause.</p>
<p>Amid <a href="https://yaledailynews.com/articles/blumenthal-among-alumni-urging-yale-to-resist-amid-reported-trump-talks">escalating pushback</a> to a Yale deal with the Trump administration, Slaughter stressed the University’s responsibility to defend democratic principles and highlighted the long-term consequences of settling.</p>
<p>“I have personal experience of challenging actions of the administration to accrue power for themselves at the expense of Congress, of the people, and I have experienced challenging that all the way up to the highest court in the land,” Slaughter said in a phone interview with the News.</p>
<p>“And my immediate reaction, on reading the story that Yale was considering settling, is that it cannot be that I, Becca, normal human, had the wherewithal to challenge something that was wrong and an abuse of power, and Yale — with its $44 billion endowment — does not,” she continued.</p>
<p>Late last month, the New York Times <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/06/26/us/politics/yale-trump-administration-admissions-race.html">reported</a> that Yale had entered talks with the Trump administration, which had <a href="https://yaledailynews.com/articles/doj-says-school-of-medicine-discriminates-against-asian-white-applicants">accused</a> the Yale School of Medicine of violating a 2023 Supreme Court ruling that deemed race-conscious admissions illegal in May. The allegations followed a Department of Justice investigation that had widened to include Yale College and Yale Law School admissions, the New York Times reported.</p>
<p>On Friday, Sen. Richard Blumenthal said at a press conference on campus that an initial settlement offer from the University <a href="https://yaledailynews.com/articles/yale-offers-trump-administration-second-proposal-after-first-is-rejected">had been rejected by the Trump administration</a>, and that Yale had made a second offer. He added that both settlement offers had been made without any demand from the Trump administration.</p>
<p>Since the news broke regarding a potential settlement, faculty, alumni and students have mounted campaigns calling for Yale to reject a deal. After the New York Times reported on Tuesday that Law School Dean Cristina Rodríguez ’95 LAW ’00 and several Law School faculty members have lobbied Yale leaders to fight Trump, law students publicly expressed <a href="https://yaledailynews.com/articles/in-private-talks-and-an-open-letter-yale-law-affiliates-fight-trump-deal">their opposition to a potential settlement</a>.</p>
<p>Slaughter believes it is critical for Yale to defend its principles. Having been in court with the Trump administration, she said that she understands the challenges but also the importance of pushing back.</p>
<p>“It’s not fun to push back — it’s much nicer to sort of think about how to walk away or make it go away in the short term — but it’s so much better in the long term to stand on principle,” she said. “And especially as an academic institution, I think Yale has an incredible obligation to do that, and that obligation is to its students, to its alumni, to its faculty, to its employees, to its partners.”</p>
<p>A lawyer by training, Slaughter said she has experience on both the government and private sector sides of investigations.</p>
<p>“Settlement is always a risk calculus question because litigation can be expensive, and there’s reputational costs even if you win, and I get that as a general matter, speaking broadly,” she said. “But I think it is a very different calculus when your entire institutional reputation is staked around integrity, principle, independence — literally, truth and light. That is the motto of the University. It is not what’s the most economically expedient.”</p>
<p>According to reporting in the New York Times, University leaders have said that a deal might be necessary for Yale to continue receiving federal funding.</p>
<p>“Being a university administrator is hard,” Slaughter acknowledged. “I really respect that they have the weight of not only all of the students and all of the faculty and all of the employees and the institutional history. It’s a lot to carry.”</p>
<p>But, she said, it’s that “enormous amount of responsibility for everyone who relies on you” that makes it even more important to adhere to guiding institutional principles rather than “short-term political or economic expedients.”</p>
<p>Slaughter also said that a deal with the Trump administration would not only have long-lasting impacts on the University but might also be ineffective in the short term.</p>
<p>She cited a memo sent to Yale President Maurie McInnis and Yale General Counsel Alexander Dreier LAW ’95, written by two Yale Law School alumni and law professors at the University of Pennsylvania, which analyzed past deals and warned against a settlement. In the memo, Serena Mayeri LAW ’01 GRD ’06 and Amanda Shanor ’03 LAW ’09 GRD ’22 analyzed previous deals made between universities and the Trump administration.</p>
<p>The pair noted instances in which universities like Penn have made agreements with the federal government but continue to be targeted. They outlined the implications of a deal that might be felt on and beyond campus, highlighting that many institutions “that have pushed back against similar pressure from the administration rather than signing agreements have been successful in court.”</p>
<p>Slaughter said that for universities that have made deals with the government, settling “hasn’t solved problems — it’s created more problems and burdened them with some really challenging terms.”</p>
<p>Alumni and current students have expressed their own worries about the dangers of a deal. At an online press briefing on Thursday, Yale College Council Alex William Chen ’28 expressed fear that consequences of a settlement will be felt by many constituencies, and “unduly borne by students.” Novelist Susan Choi ’90 said that a settlement will make the “erosions to campus freedoms” worse.</p>
<p>The impacts of a deal between Yale and the Trump administration, Slaughter believes, “will be felt for a very, very long time.” She repeatedly pointed to the reputational cost of settling, which she said is “difficult to quantify” but “can be measured in alumni sentiment, in student recruitment, in faculty and researcher recruitment and retention, in business partnerships.”</p>
<p>In an email she sent to McInnis expressing her concerns, Slaughter said she emphasized that “whatever Yale does here, whichever way it goes, is not going to be forgotten by myself, or my scores of fellow alumni, when appeals for donations come around, and it won’t be forgotten by prospective students and professors who are considering to which institution they will devote their tuition dollars and their talent.”</p>
<p>“I understand the short term pressure, but the long term consequences for the reputation and the integrity of the institution are very profound,” she said.</p>
<p>After contesting her March 2025 firing by President Donald Trump, it took over a year of legal battles for Slaughter to finally receive a decision from the highest court in the United States.</p>
<p>“I just lost a Supreme Court case,” Slaughter said. “So I understand what it feels like to lose — and it doesn’t feel good. But it feels much, much better to be on the right side of history than it does to be on the winning side of any particular case. And that is not a close call.”</p>
<p>Supreme Court Justice Sonia Sotomayor LAW ’79, joined by Justices Ketanji Brown Jackson and Elena Kagan, dissented to the majority ruling in Trump v. Slaughter.</p>
