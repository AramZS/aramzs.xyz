<p>It’s a new kind of war. In the <br/>
 “kill zone” of Ukraine, surveillance and attack drones swarm the skies, hunting anything that moves.</p>
<figure><img alt="Rueda animada" src="https://www.reuters.com/graphics/UKRAINE-CRISIS/KILL-ZONE/znpnojmknvl/cdn/images/drone.png"/></figure>
<p>There’s no more massing of troops and artillery. Instead, soldiers on both sides hide for months at a time in tiny, isolated foxholes to survive.</p>
<p>Soldiers move in small groups to dodge detection, often on foot or motorbike. Even then, the drones hunt them mercilessly. Casualties are staggering.</p>
<p>Our immersive reconstruction will take you deep into the kill zone, a strip of land about 30 km (20 miles) wide in some places that straddles the line of contact. In this hellscape, buildings lie in ruins and trees are shredded.</p>

<h1>Inside Ukraine’s Kill Zone</h1>
<aside>By <a href="https://www.reuters.com/authors/mariano-zafra/">Mariano Zafra</a>, <a href="https://www.reuters.com/authors/mike-collett-white/">Mike Collett-White</a>, <a href="https://www.reuters.com/authors/tom-balmforth/">Tom Balmforth</a> and <a href="https://www.reuters.com/authors/max-hunder/">Max Hunder</a> <br/> Published July 23, 2026 01:00 AM EDT </aside>
<p>A terrifying game of hide-and-seek is playing out in this deadly corridor that snakes along the 1,200 km-long (750-mile) front line in Ukraine.</p>
<p>The <a href="https://www.reuters.com/world/ukraine-russia-war/">war</a>, well into its fifth year, has become the first major conflict to be dominated by unmanned aerial vehicles, with thousands of the cheap but deadly machines filling the skies.</p>
<p>Ukraine and invader Russia are constantly advancing the new technology, which is transforming modern warfare. Both sides have had to abandon age-old tactics of gathering troops, vehicles and artillery in a relatively compressed area along the front lines. Such visible targets are easy prey for drones.</p>
<p>Instead, teams as small as two to three soldiers hide in tiny dugouts hollowed under the ground, scattered across a huge kill zone extending many kilometres on either side of the line of contact between the two sides. Infantrymen often act as lookouts, avoiding direct engagement that could give away their position, instead calling in drones to do the killing, said an officer in leading Ukrainian drone brigade Madyar’s Birds, who goes by the callsign Silhouette.</p>
<p>Drones are guided by pilots hiding in the kill zone or further away who fly them using video game-style controls. Many are first-person view (FPV) craft, allowing the pilot to see the scene from the drone’s perspective in real time. They are controlled either through radio signals or via long, ultra-fine fibre optic cables stretching behind the drones.</p>

<p>A 30-kilometre-wide “kill zone” along the battlefield illustrates how pervasive drone surveillance can make nearly any movement visible and potentially lethal.</p>
<p>The illustration isn’t a recreation of any particular stretch of the front but features a concentration of scenes from multiple sectors that show how the armies operate and engage. It is based on dozens of first-hand accounts from Ukrainian soldiers, drone pilots and commanders, as well as interviews with military experts and hundreds of battlefield videos posted online by units on both sides.</p>
<p>Reuters was unable to interview members of Russia’s military for this article because of tight restrictions in Russia on reporting the conflict. The Russian defence ministry did not respond to questions about the changing nature of the battlefield.</p>
<p>The Russian-held side of the fly-over focuses more on attacks and Ukraine’s attempts to thwart them, reflecting the dominant dynamic of the battlefield over the last two years. The Ukrainian side focuses on supply lines, rotations and evacuations.</p>
<p>Gaining ground is perilous, often involving huge risk for minimal territory, helping explain how Ukraine has been able to slow Russian advances significantly over the past year despite being outnumbered and outgunned by conventional weapons.</p>
<h2> Russian-held territory</h2>
<p>Ukraine's manpower shortages mean frontline positions are widely scattered. Rotating in and out carries huge risk and infantrymen rarely emerge from hiding. Serhii Krynitskyi, a Ukrainian soldier, <a href="https://www.reuters.com/world/ukraine-russia-war/year-foxhole-how-one-ukrainian-soldier-survived-beneath-front-lines-2026-07-22/">told Reuters how he spent more than 300 days in a single position.</a></p>
<p>The furthermost Russian assault troops often seek to avoid contact with enemy forces. They attempt to gain defendable positions inside Ukrainian-held territory and wait for comrades to join them.</p>
<p>Russian troops who make it this far probe Ukrainian defences in tiny groups of typically one to three soldiers.</p>
<p>A soldier spotted by a drone has little chance of survival. Even abandoned trenches offer little cover.</p>
<p>What armour there is remains heavily camouflaged. This tank, which would fire only sparingly to avoid detection, has a "Mad Max"-style cage to protect it from drones.</p>
<p>Burned-out tanks litter roads and tracks in the kill zone, relics of battles earlier in the war. The dominance of drones has made traditional armoured vehicles almost obsolete this close to the line of contact.</p>
<p>The closer to the enemy you are, the greater the saturation of drones. Nowhere is safe.</p>
<p>Vehicles delivering supplies to troops are at grave risk. They speed to their task and speed away. Electronic jammers are used to try to disrupt incoming drones' link to their remote pilots, rendering them useless.</p>
<p>The skies above buzz with various types of attack drones hunting prey. They may drop bombs or fly directly into a target and explode, kamikaze-style.</p>
<p>Russian drone units are dotted across the kill zone. These pilot crews are prime targets and remain underground. They emerge only briefly to launch drones.</p>
<p>Russian positions might be hidden in basements at this distance, while command posts led by more senior officers are further back from the kill zone.</p>
<p>Artillery is dug in and camouflaged. Though used more sparingly than earlier in the war, artillery still serves as a reliable, all‑weather precision tool. Enemy drones hunt these positions.</p>
<p>Closer to enemy-held territory, small Russian assault teams of two to three soldiers foray forward, on foot, motorcycles or quad bikes, often exploiting fog or bad weather which reduces drones' effectiveness.</p>
<p>But the dangers are extreme. Drones outpace vehicles in the kill zone, and thousands of videos posted by units on both sides show soldiers on foot or on motorbikes being hunted and killed.</p>

<p>How far the kill zone extends at any given stretch of the front depends on numerous factors, including the quality of the drone units there and the landscape. The days of heavy mechanised manoeuvres are over; a drone costing a few thousand dollars can disable a tank worth millions. Long trenches open to the skies have become obsolete. Fibre optic cables trailing behind drones settle upon the landscape like spider webs.</p>

<p>Russian forces mainly gain territory by infiltrating Ukraine’s porous defences. Low cloud and heavy fog can act as cover. Ukraine does not have enough soldiers, so its infantry is thinly scattered, leaving wide gaps to sneak through.</p>
<p>If soldiers find an empty building or bunker in Ukrainian-held territory, they may wait there for several days while another Russian tries to join them, followed by another. “Over 10 days, you end up with five Russian soldiers inside. That’s it – they’ve taken a position,” said Roman Polishchuk, deputy commander of Ukraine’s Liut brigade.</p>
<figure><a href="https://www.reuters.com/world/ukraine-russia-war/year-foxhole-how-one-ukrainian-soldier-survived-beneath-front-lines-2026-07-22/"><img alt="A year in a foxhole" src="https://www.reuters.com/graphics/UKRAINE-CRISIS/KILL-ZONE/znpnojmknvl/cdn/images/foxhole.jpg"/><p>Read more</p><h2>A year in a foxhole</h2><p>How one Ukrainian soldier survived the ‘kill zone’</p></a></figure>
<p>Casualties are massive, with the vast majority now caused by drones. The Center for Strategic &amp; International Studies estimates around 2 million soldiers since early 2022 have been killed, wounded or are missing, 1.4 million of them Russian.</p>
<p>A deputy battalion commander in Ukraine’s 59th Assault Brigade, with the callsign Prime, described relentless pursuits: “If a drone spots an infantryman, it won’t let him go.”</p>
<p>In the age of drone warfare, pilots themselves are prized targets, said Danylo Polozhukhno, deputy commander of Ukraine’s Kraken unmanned systems regiment: “We find them and destroy them.”</p>
