<p>Our nation’s blood supply is in some major trouble currently, according to the American Red Cross.</p>
<p>On Monday, the Red Cross declared a national blood crisis for the second time ever in its history. Blood and platelet levels have fallen to a dangerous low, officials say. Eligible Americans are being urged to schedule a donation as quickly as possible, particularly people with type O-positive blood.</p>
<p>“[T]his summer, blood donations simply are not keeping pace with hospital demand, and inventories —especially type O blood—remain under significant strain,” said Chris Hrouda, president of Red Cross Biomedical Services, in a <a href="https://www.redcross.org/about-us/news-and-events/press-release/2026/red-cross-declares-national-blood-supply-crisis-urges-immediate-.html">statement</a> from the American Red Cross.</p>
<h2>Dangerously low</h2>
<p>Even in the best of times, the national blood supply lies on shaky ground. Roughly two-thirds of Americans are <a href="https://www.facebook.com/RedCrossNCGC/posts/roughly-62-of-the-us-population-is-eligible-to-give-blood-but-only-about-3-donat/1067049168782883/">eligible</a> to donate, yet about 3% of eligible people do so annually. And since most blood products have a limited shelf life (only five days for platelets), hospitals constantly need new donations to keep the supply stocked.</p>
<p>In January 2022, the American Red Cross was <a href="https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1wHqbV64uaK0Dk636gaCiKSdpccuvsWpChQWmdc48dzU/edit?pli=1&amp;gid=727825933#gid=727825933">forced</a> to declare its first ever blood supply crisis, likely fueled by the surge of covid-19 cases seen that winter caused by the emergence of the Omicron variant. And though the organization had reportedly taken additional safeguards to prevent such a crisis again, history has now repeated itself.</p>
<p>The Red Cross issued a national blood emergency on July 13, after a 25% decline in the nation’s blood supply the previous month, but the situation has only worsened since. Recent donations have fallen to a four-year summer low, while hospital demand has stayed high. Summer months tend to see a rise in traumatic injuries that could warrant blood transfusions. The organization cites extreme heat, poor air quality, and widespread foodborne illnesses as factors contributing to lower donor turnout this summer.</p>
<p>Type O-positive blood, the most commonly transfused type, is especially precarious, with the current inventory now below one day’s supply. As a result, the Red Cross has been forced to start limiting distributions of type O blood to individual hospitals. Platelet levels aren’t doing so hot either, with the organization experiencing an emergency platelet shortage.</p>
<p>The American Red Cross is the single largest blood supplier in the U.S., accounting for roughly 40% of the national blood supply. But it’s hardly the only place facing trouble this summer. Two weeks ago, the Red Cross, the Advancement of Blood &amp; Biotherapies, and America’s Blood Centers (which covers most of the remaining 60%) <a href="https://www.aabb.org/news-resources/news/article/2026/07/10/joint-statement--blood-donors-needed-to-help-boost-the-nation-s-strained-blood-supply-this-summer">issued</a> a joint statement calling for more donors.</p>
<h2>Donate now if possible</h2>
<p>Without enough blood, hospitals and doctors are forced to triage the limited supply available, which can lead to people not getting the optimal medical care they need, including in life-or-death situations. So if you’re eligible, which you can see for yourself <a href="https://www.redcrossblood.org/donate-blood/how-to-donate/eligibility-requirements.html">here</a>, making time for donation is just about the best gift you can give someone this summer.</p>
<p>“Every donation has the potential to help save lives, and we urgently need everyone who is eligible to make an appointment to give blood as soon as possible,” said Hrouda.</p>
