Tom Haward, director of Richard Haward’s Oysters. Image by Richard Haward’s Oysters, used with permission</figcaption></figure>
<p><strong>Although the tenth anniversary of the referendum passed three weeks ago, it has prompted renewed reflection among business owners who say leaving the European Union continues to affect their livelihoods. For some, it’s less about the political arguments and more about the actual consequences of leaving the EU on their livelihoods, measured in lost customers, mountains of bureaucracy and shrinking profit margins.</strong></p>
<p>From oyster farmers and food producers to engineering firms, businesses in very different sectors tell remarkably similar stories. Exporting has become more complicated, more expensive and, in some cases, no longer commercially viable.</p>
<h2>‘Our trade to Europe stopped overnight’</h2>
<figure><img alt="Haward's oysters" data-pin-no-hover="true" src="https://eastangliabylines.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/haward_oysters_brexit-683x1024.jpg"/><figcaption>Haward’s oysters. Photo by Tom Haward</figcaption></figure>
<p>For Tom Haward, director of the eighth-generation family business Richard Haward’s Oysters, Brexit has been a “disaster” and a “catastrophe” for the shellfish industry.</p>
<p>The Mersea Island company, which farms around one million oysters a year and supplies what it describes as “some of the finest oysters in the world”, estimates it has lost around £200,000 a year since the UK left the EU.</p>
<p>“When Brexit was implemented all our trade to Europe stopped overnight,” he says.</p>
<p>Although the business has worked hard to replace some European customers with UK buyers, exporting live shellfish remains far more complex than it was before Brexit. The additional paperwork, delays and uncertainty have discouraged many former customers from returning, despite continued demand for British oysters.</p>
<p>“I have been in touch with some European customers and they are still too nervous about buying because it’s just not simple and easy enough to do,” he said. “The Europeans love British oysters. I hate Brexit.”</p>
<p>Haward believes Brexit has caused lasting damage to many small businesses, with the impact continuing long after the political arguments have faded.</p>
<p>His assessment echoes concerns he expressed shortly after the Trade and Cooperation Agreement came into force in 2021, when he warned that decades of carefully built European trade had effectively been wiped out overnight.</p>
<h2>Farming under pressure</h2>
<figure><img alt="Portrait of Tom Bradshaw standing in a field on his farm." data-pin-no-hover="true" src="https://eastangliabylines.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Essex_farmer_Tom_Bradshaw.webp"/><figcaption>Tom Bradshaw. Image by Adam Fradgley Exposure Photography, used with permission</figcaption></figure>
<p>The picture is similar in agriculture.</p>
<p>Tom Bradshaw, president of the National Farmers’ Union and owner of Fletchers Farm near Colchester, says his farming business is making a loss for the third consecutive year.</p>
<p>He points to a combination of pressures: rising costs, recent heatwaves, the loss of government funding that followed Brexit, and more difficult access to Britain’s biggest export market.</p>
<p>The EU still buys around 67% of all the UK food exports, he notes, yet export volumes have fallen by more than 30% since 2019.</p>
<p>Demand is the foundation of successful farming, Bradshaw says, and seeing demand from the EU fall so steeply is “not a recipe for getting the best return for the products we are producing”.</p>
<p>Like many farmers, he has increasingly relied on diversification to keep the business viable. Alongside farming, Fletchers Farm now includes equestrian facilities and light industrial units.</p>
<p>“We are absolutely relying heavily on diversifying to cope,” he says. “It’s completely wrong that producing something as fundamental as the country’s food is not profitable.”</p>
<h2>Beyond farming and fishing</h2>
<p>The experience is not confined to food producers.</p>
<figure><img alt="Portrait of Darren Farrell" data-pin-no-hover="true" sizes="(max-width: 740px) 100vw, 740px" src="https://eastangliabylines.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/darren-farrell.jpg" srcset="https://eastangliabylines.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/darren-farrell.jpg 740w, https://eastangliabylines.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/darren-farrell-300x172.jpg 300w"/><figcaption>Darren Farrell</figcaption></figure>
<p>Darren Farrell, managing director of Essex-based DARJAC Engineering, <a href="https://www.thenewworld.co.uk/the-british-businesses-being-strangled-by-brexit-red-tape/">told</a> <em>New World</em> that Brexit effectively brought his company’s European trade to an end.</p>
<p>“Within nine months of the Government signing the Trade and Cooperation Agreement with the EU, seven out of ten EU suppliers said they could no longer support third-country shipping and warranty,” he said.</p>
<p>The practical consequences soon mounted. “We can’t move our customers’ vehicles to race events on the EU mainland without huge cost, including temporary export permits that take many hours.”</p>
<p>Eventually, the additional costs became unsustainable. “Two years on, we could no longer support the losses. Our business is finished.”</p>
<h2>Businesses want barriers reduced</h2>
<p>The concerns raised by individual businesses are echoed by the wider business community.</p>
<p>The Essex Chambers of Commerce has repeatedly called for the Government to reduce post-Brexit trading barriers, arguing that unnecessary bureaucracy continues to damage firms that export to the EU.</p>
<p>Research cited by the British Chambers of Commerce found that more than half of UK exporters believe the Trade and Cooperation Agreement has made exporting to the EU more difficult, while businesses continue to point to customs procedures, VAT complexity, regulatory divergence and restrictions on business travel as significant obstacles.</p>
<p>Rather than reopening the arguments over whether Brexit should have happened, business organisations have focused on practical measures that would make trading with the UK’s largest overseas market easier.</p>
<figure><img alt="Keir Starmer in conversation with Ursula Von der Leyen at the UN General Assembly" data-pin-no-hover="true" sizes="(max-width: 1024px) 100vw, 1024px" src="https://eastangliabylines.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/Starmer-and-van-der-Leyen.jpg" srcset="https://eastangliabylines.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/Starmer-and-van-der-Leyen-1024x684.jpg 1024w, https://eastangliabylines.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/Starmer-and-van-der-Leyen-300x201.jpg 300w, https://eastangliabylines.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/Starmer-and-van-der-Leyen-768x513.jpg 768w, https://eastangliabylines.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/Starmer-and-van-der-Leyen-750x501.jpg 750w, https://eastangliabylines.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/Starmer-and-van-der-Leyen-1140x762.jpg 1140w, https://eastangliabylines.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/Starmer-and-van-der-Leyen.jpg 1200w"/><figcaption>Image by <a href="https://www.flickr.com/photos/number10gov/54022914335/in/photostream/">Number 10</a> via Flickr (CC BY-NC-ND 2.0)</figcaption></figure>
<p>There are signs that the relationship could continue to evolve beyond Keir Starmer’s “reset”.</p>
<p>His outgoing government have been negotiating closer cooperation with the EU in areas including sanitary and phytosanitary (SPS) rules, with the aim of reducing some of the friction affecting food and agricultural exports.</p>
<p>With a change of prime minister now imminent, it’s too early to know whether that process will accelerate, stall or take a different direction altogether. Andy Burnham has long been regarded as one of Labour’s more pro-European voices, but until his government sets out its negotiating priorities, businesses can only speculate about what practical changes might follow.</p>
<p>For Tom Haward, Tom Bradshaw and Darren Farrell, the debate has long since moved beyond the referendum itself. Their businesses are very different, but they describe many of the same problems: more paperwork, higher costs and more difficult access to customers just across the Channel.</p>
<p>Ten years after the country voted to leave the European Union, the political arguments rumble on. And for many businesses, the economic story is still unfolding.</p>
