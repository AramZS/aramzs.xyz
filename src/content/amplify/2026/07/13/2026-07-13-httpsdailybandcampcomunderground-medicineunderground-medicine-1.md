<p>Nothing is unprecedented.</p>
<p>Anyone who values discovering music can use this strangely comforting thought as a lodestar leading them to new sounds, styles, and pockets of interest. Tracking down the roots of a sound that you love, a new band that kicks your ass, whatever it may be, is a journey of discovery, delight, and occasionally disgust. Sometimes you find the best thing you’ve ever heard; sometimes you fall into quicksand, and you gotta pull yourself out ASAP. But that’s fun, too. To some of us freaks, there’s nothing better than hitting play—whether on a turntable, a cassette deck, a CD player, a phone screen, a Bandcamp page—and hearing what comes next. There’s a nagging sensation that drives the music obsessive. It’s an itch, you scratch it, it feels good, and then you can’t wait for that next itch to come. Underground Medicine is for the scratchers. We’re gonna dig some gold out from the nooks and crannies of the vast Bandcamp landscape, a site I’ve been using since I made my first artist account in 2011 for <a data-clickthrough="true" href="https://tokyostormwarning1.bandcamp.com/album/high-times-at-the-black-eye">an old band</a> I was in.</p>
<p>I guess I should introduce myself. Hi, I’m Erick Bradshaw. I’ve been in the trenches of the underground music scene since the 1990s, making zines, booking shows, playing in bands, DJing, and more (sound engineering, tour booking, PR hacking). And I’m still doing it. I DJ out and <a href="https://wfmu.org/playlists/EH">on the radio</a>; I <a data-clickthrough="true" href="https://blankaccount.bandcamp.com/album/on-the-qt">play in a band</a>; I make <a data-clickthrough="true" href="https://cyanidetooth.bandcamp.com/">experimental music</a> on <a data-clickthrough="true" href="https://roguechromosome.bandcamp.com/">my own</a>; I <a href="https://daily.bandcamp.com/contributors/erick-bradshaw">write about music</a>. (I’ve mostly retired from booking shows, so it is possible to break old habits.) Ultimately, I just really dig turning people on to music; it might even be my primary function in life.</p>
<p>I’ve got some good stuff planned for Underground Medicine—a mix of new, old, and just-dug-up or just-dug, in general. In addition to the main feature each month, Underground Medicine will have some recurring sections. First up is “Deep Cuts,” which will be akin to flipping through the $3–$8 CD bin at the used record store. Over the course of many years, I spent thousands of dollars on used CDs, soaking up as much music as possible, selling back the ones I didn’t dig to get even more of the little silver discs (and I was also buying the even cheaper vinyl that people were unloading by the truckload during the CD era). Bandcamp can be a similar experience except, unlike at the store, you can try before you buy (can’t trade in those used MP3s though, sorry). There is so much cool music on this site, it’s intimidating. I’m here to help! I like connecting the dots between bands, scenes, sounds, and styles (don’t ignore those hyperlinks!). The underground is a continuum, and this is a conduit. Let it flow forth.</p>
<p>Since this is the inaugural column, I’m gonna throw two killer groups at you: Rust Belt death rock from the early 1980s and a mid-‘90s gem that barely made a ripple outside of its hometown.</p>
<h2>Deep Cuts</h2>
<figure><img data-bind="attr: { 'src': currentTrack().artURL }" src="https://f4.bcbits.com/img/a2992732537_16.jpg"/></figure>
<p>Easter Monkeys came howling out of the deserted streets of post-industrial Cleveland with a hypnotic force, wielding pitch-black humor like a shovel dumping dirt on your final resting place. Getting their start in 1981, Easter Monkeys had close ties with Cleveland’s legendary early punk scene: singer and saxophonist Chris Yarmock fronted the Kneecappers; guitarist Jim Jones played in Mirrors and <a data-clickthrough="true" href="https://electriceels.bandcamp.com/album/spin-age-blasters">Electric Eels</a>; drummer Linda Hudson was the sister of <a data-clickthrough="true" href="https://breakoutrecords77.bandcamp.com/album/br009-pagans-dead-end-america-little-black-egg-7">Pagans</a> singer Mike; the line-up was completed by bassist Charlie Ditteaux. <em>Splendor of Sorrow</em> was a lost classic from its very inception; although recorded in 1983, it didn’t see release until 1990, when a posthumous LP came out, and even in the early 2000s, you could still score a sealed copy in Cleveland.</p>
<p>This expanded 2009 reissue reverts to the original mixes, which are different from the LP versions, and adds six more songs. “Nailed To The Cross” is the Easter Monkeys’ grand statement, a relentlessly chugging dirge with Yarmock’s foghorn sax line stalking the band like a hellhound on their trail, the blasphemy a stick in the eye to the Moral Majority, which was trying to stake their claim on the country’s culture and stomp out any hint of rebelliousness in the 1980s. The reissue of <em>Splendor of Sorrow</em> also contains the essential “Cheap Heroin,” which was originally on the 1982 compilation <a href="https://www.discogs.com/release/900801-Various-Cleveland-Confidential"><em>Cleveland Confidential.</em></a> “Cheap Heroin” prefigures <a data-clickthrough="true" href="https://buttholesurfers.bandcamp.com/">Butthole Surfers</a>’ brand of acid-casualty punk with a bass-heavy groove that puts them in conversation with Flipper, whose debut LP came out the same year. It didn’t appear on the record it’s named after, but on a live version of the album’s title track, Yarmock testifies—”I felt the sorrow/ And I’ve seen the splendor/ They masquerade/ As each other”—and his conviction rings true. After Easter Monkeys ended, Jones played in <a data-clickthrough="true" href="https://homeandgarden.bandcamp.com/album/history-and-geography">Home and Garden</a> and then joined <a data-clickthrough="true" href="https://pereubumusic.bandcamp.com/">Pere Ubu</a>. Yarmock fronted St. Jayne, while Ditteaux played in Knifedance and Short Rabbits. Jim Jones passed away in 2008, but the music lives on.</p>
<figure><img data-bind="attr: { 'src': currentTrack().artURL }" src="https://f4.bcbits.com/img/a0080783321_16.jpg"/></figure>
<p>Formed in Richmond, Virginia, <a data-clickthrough="true" href="https://maotsehelen.bandcamp.com/music">Mao Tse Helen</a> had quite the underground pedigree: singer/guitarist Chris Bopst was <a data-clickthrough="true" href="https://gwar.bandcamp.com/music">Gwar</a>’s first bass player, then fronted SST jazz-punkers <a data-clickthrough="true" href="https://alternatives.bandcamp.com/">Alter Natives</a> in the ‘80s before joining Dischord rockers <a data-clickthrough="true" href="https://holyrollers.bandcamp.com/album/s-t">Holy Rollers</a>; bassist Ron Curry had also played with Gwar and was part of ‘90s SST group Hotel X; drummer <a data-clickthrough="true" href="https://chrisfarmer.bandcamp.com/">Chris Farmer</a> had helped define <a href="https://daily.bandcamp.com/lists/brutalist-riffs-a-guide-to-math-rock">math rock</a> with the short-lived, barn-burning Breadwinner; and the line-up was rounded out by second guitarist Brian Murchison.</p>
<p>Mao Tse Helen came out fully formed on their first single as a tight and spastic mix of the Minutemen’s slinky angularity and the howling, avant-blues shuffle of Captain Beefheart and His Magic Band. Bopst’s cranky yammer is a direct homage to Beefheart’s growl, and the band executed these approaches with the precision of seasoned math rock vets, creating spiky, high-energy rock that was really just party music for the black-framed-glasses set (guilty as charged). There was also a sinister, bass-heavy twang to Mao Tse Helen’s sound, almost like Creedence Clearwater Revival covering Black Sabbath. Mao Tse Helen’s sole proper full-length release was the 1996 CD <em>Maize</em>, and it shows what a raucous unit the quartet was. By this point, Farmer had been replaced by Chris Gallo (also a member of <a data-clickthrough="true" href="https://sistersound.bandcamp.com/album/dragging-the-timepiece-heart-2">Sistersound</a>), who handled the tricky beats with aplomb. On “Sweet Love,” Bopst hollers himself hoarse while the band stumbles around like a drunken bull, settling down at points so that Bopst can talk to himself and play a pretty guitar melody. “I’m So Happy To Be Alive, I Think I’ll Sing Myself A Happy Song” references Nina Simone and unfurls like a tornado touching down in your living room. Frenzied but controlled cuts like “Circus Peanuts” and “Mao Tse Theme” clue you into the kinds of kicks these hepcats were in search of. Trust me, it sure beat the hell out of the swing revival! Mao Tse Helen ended up putting out a second 7-inch and recording a <a data-clickthrough="true" href="https://maotsehelen.bandcamp.com/album/songs-by-the-musical-band-group">follow-up album.</a></p>
<p>Underground Medicine’s other recurring section will be “Short Stack.” Here’s where I need your help. I want to cover actual singles that are available on Bandcamp. That means physical media, and several formats will be considered (digital-only releases do not qualify). These formats are eligible for inclusion in Short Stack: 7-inch vinyl, 45 or 33 RPM; 12-inch vinyl, 45 RPM; lathe cuts of any size that have a run of at least 10 copies; cassette EPs that have a running time of less than 12 minutes. The release in question needs to be from 2026 so please send new stuff. Rules!</p>
