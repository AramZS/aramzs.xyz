<p>I’m certainly not the first to say Vajra Chandrasekera is one of the most exciting and visionary writers working in speculative fiction today—both his debut, <em>The Saint of Bright Doors</em>, and the follow-up <em>Rakesfall</em> took the SFF community by storm. Both are risk-taking novels, subverting reader expectations and challenging the boundaries of genre. Chandrasekera’s curiosity and skill as a writer has produced work capable of defying time and space within a single, impactful narrative.</p>
<p>It is of no surprise that <em>Rakesfall</em> was chosen as this years recipient of the <a href="https://www.ursulakleguin.com/prize24">Ursula K. Le Guin Prize for Fiction</a>, which celebrates work of imaginative fiction that shares the ideals and themes of Le Guin’s work, including “hope, equity, and freedom; non-violence and alternatives to conflict; and a holistic view of humanity’s place in the natural world.” This year’s selection panel, consisting of Matt Bell, Indra Das, Kelly Link, Sequoia Nagamatsu, and Rebecca Roanhorse, said the book “trusts us to follow, across the literary equivalent of light years, a deeply felt and moving story of grief, loss, and ultimately hope to savor in dark times.”</p>
<p>I was thrilled to chat with Chandrasekera to discuss <em>Rakesfall</em>, a sprawling narrative about reincarnation, revolution, and recollection, as well as his approach to writing the fantastic.</p>
<p><strong><em>Christina Orlando:</em> Congratulations on receiving this year’s Ursula K. Le Guin prize! In your acceptance speech, you refer to Le Guin as a “Northern Star”—can you tell me about your relationship with her work? </strong></p>
<p><em><strong>Vajra Chandrasekera:</strong></em> I read the <em>Earthsea </em>books relatively early and loved them, of course; I read <em>The Dispossessed </em>quite late and loved it also, as we all do. But in between them I read the odd, funny little story that I loved most of all, which is “Confusions of Uñi” in <em>Changing Planes</em>, which I read not long after it came out some twenty-odd years ago. There is such a wonderful playfulness to that story, to its puns and confusions, and that’s my favourite of her moods. Thinking back now, I’d say it’s one of the many inspirations for<em> Rakesfall</em>’s own shifts and changes.</p>
<p><strong><em>Christina:</em></strong> <strong><em>Rakesfall</em> is, it seems to me, primarily a story about history, memory, and storytelling. When you first set out to write, how did you envision the scope of the novel? Given that readers are taken into the past, future, and even spaces of unreality, I can imagine that being both daunting and exciting to work through. </strong></p>
<p><strong><em>Vajra:</em></strong> I knew from the fragments that I had at hand—the parts that had already been published as short stories—that there would be a long journey through history to build, but I didn’t want it to be a boring highlights reel of the generally agreed-upon major historical events. This is a hazard for stories that work with time, in that we are all already living within a story about time and its power can be overwhelming. I tried to sabotage any sense of a just-so story about history, any definitive “this is how it went and this is how we got there”, because I find that not only boring but dangerous. Histories are multiple, shifting, and furiously active creatures, often far more so than imagined futures. Whether in the mythic past or the mythic future, I tried to keep a sense that there is always something else going on, there are always other people at work, sometimes at odds with you but also sometimes simply off in their own frame of reference that has nothing to do with you. I find that crowdedness a relief, the sense of a vast spatial and temporal terrain that is thick with narrative and promiscuously mixing genres of reality, because that’s much more like real life. Providing relatable content is my mission statement.</p>
<p><strong><em>Christina:</em></strong> <strong>On that subject, the book also traverses genre boundaries with ease<strong>—</strong>in fact, it’s a book that seems to say “fuck genre” entirely (which I am 10000% on board with personally) (and maybe would bring magical realism into the conversation as well as sci-fi and fantasy). What is your relationship to genre? What interests you about the speculative and the fantastic? </strong></p>
<p><strong><em>Vajra:</em></strong> I love genre! I love it so much I like to rub all of the genres over each other all the time. Which I suppose is less to say fuck genre and more that genre fucks. The way I see it, genre is three sets of formal expectations in a trenchcoat: what you’re selling, what you’re telling, and what you’re hiding for someone to find. Publishing’s system of genre classification has its own, <a href="https://andrewgoldstone.com/research/bh2023ms.pdf">deeply fascinating history</a>. Through allusion and influence, writers work within, or at least with, writerly traditions, which are especially rich and plentiful in speculative fiction. And then there are the particular references, detournements, subversions, and interpretations that the particular writer and the particular reader will produce from a given text.<em> Genre</em> is a way of saying what is everyone bringing to the table—everyone involved with this text in any way, really, from the bookseller who writes a shelf talker to the reader who has watched only<em> Boss Baby</em> and is getting real boss baby vibes from this. It’s a big, messy mashup of many different intersecting and overlapping frameworks, and as is probably obvious, I do love that sort of thing.</p>
<p>What I like most about the speculative is the joy, I think. The exuberance, the joie de lire, the fucking about. It’s such a fun form to work in. You can free yourselves to any degree that you please from constraints that otherwise tend to be invisibly ubiquitous, such as physics, location, and selfhood.</p>
<p><strong><em>Christina:</em></strong> <strong>How did freeing yourself of genre constrictions help tell the story you wanted to tell?</strong></p>
<p><strong><em>Vajra:</em></strong> Genre as simultaneously a set of constrictions <em>and</em> a set of freedoms from constrictions! See, this is exactly the sort of thing that happens here. I wanted to write about the real world, and the really fucked up thing about the real world is how unrealistic it is. The complicated way mythic time bleeds into historical time and has such political valence in the present day. The fucked-up relationships our societies have with our dead, especially the mass dead. All the ludicrous bullshit that is currently happening with transhumanist scum attempting to drown a civilization’s informational space in the gibbering mouth froth of rabid autocomplete. None of this is realistic at all, fire the whole writers’ room. Fiction that digs deep into the weird shit feels both real and strange, much like the world it’s from, and I think has a better grip on the material when it comes to having something to<em> say</em> about the world it knows.</p>
<p><strong><em>Christina:</em></strong> <strong>This is also a novel that is deeply playful with language (the lol j/k moment took me OUT) and form—I had a lot of fun reading parts of it aloud to myself—that feel in defiance of what we consider to be the traditional Western novel structure. For you, what was the most exciting part of deconstructing the novel, or perhaps using changes in form to construct the story you wanted to tell? </strong></p>
<p><strong><em>Vajra:</em></strong> I wanted to tell a reincarnation story that questioned the idea of reincarnation itself. I come to this problem from a different angle than most of the book’s readers, I expect, since I grew up in a reincarnation-normative culture. So here’s a critique of a contemporary orthodox southern Theravada folk understanding of reincarnation which most people who read the book wouldn’t know in the first place, which is one of the many things that are funny about this whole situation. But reincarnation stories are their own world too, containing many traditions. There’s a whole world of isekai and truck-kun, there’s whatever the fuck was going on with Hawkman, there’s <em>The Years of Rice and Salt</em>, <em>Cloud Atlas,</em> and <em>The Star Rover</em>. I wanted to write something in that territory, but of my own matter. And in doing so, I wanted to write a story that was about crossing not only worlds and lives but genre and form, which is also what happens when already published short stories are integrated into a new body of text: they are reborn into a new context where their meaning changes. “Redder,” for instance, or “Heron of Earth,” work pretty well as standalone short stories in their own right, which is how they first appeared in <em>Nightmare</em> and <em>Clarkesworld</em>, but the forms of them that appear in <em>Rakesfall</em> don’t mean quite the same thing any more, because now they have to be read in the context of knowledge of a bigger story that the reader of the original story didn’t have—much like we are supposed to forget, in life, of the greater system of reincarnation within which we live. Similarly, changes in form allow for modalities that are not conventionally accessible in third person limited point of view. For example, there’s a lot of irony and humour that the play format in “The Broken Bough” allows—generates, even—which would be so much more space-consuming in third-person limited.</p>
<p><strong><strong><em>Christina:</em></strong></strong> <strong>You mentioned some of the chapters of this book were initially published as short stories. Can you share more about how they came together as a singular narrative?</strong></p>
<p><strong><em>Vajra:</em></strong> Apart from the stories that are indicated in the book as previously published, “The Broken Bough” was also originally written as a short story, though it was never published as such. Including that one, about 20k of the ~80k words of <em>Rakesfall</em> were originally short stories. The text is 75% original to the novel. This makes it rather lopsided as a fix-up, though that’s how the Science Fiction Encyclopedia describes it and presumably they should know. But the reason the fix-up is an interesting idea to me here is because of the specific historical place of the fix-up in speculative fiction as the visible marker of the transition of economic significance, from the writerly point of view, from short form to long form fiction. It marks the point at which people who had been making some money off short fiction realized they needed to write novels now if they wanted to build a career in the fiction-writing business, and in the process turned that transition into a form. I find that manoeuvre delightful and wanted to participate in it in my own fashion, because I’d been trying to make the same move for the same reason. Belated from the point of view of the history of 20<sup>th</sup> century science fiction, perhaps, but for those of us inhabiting the last decade’s surge of international writing in SFF, especially those of us dealing with the mixed blessings of third-world arbitrage, it’s a surprisingly familiar set of circumstances. Why not a little homage to the classic pulp career path, then? First short stories, then the fix-up, then the novels … It may have worked out slightly out of sequence for me since <em>Saint</em> was finished and published first, but still, I’m happy with it. As is traditional, the writer’s material circumstances have constructed the terrain upon which narrative may take root.</p>
<p><strong><em>Christina:</em></strong> <strong>What is your approach to utilizing speculative elements, folk magic and cultural ritual, and technology (both real and imagined) over the course of the novel? </strong></p>
<p><strong><em>Vajra:</em></strong> I like to put it all in there, but not in a blender. Stirred, not shaken. I try to remember that all our modern cultures are a great deal less rational than they are advertised. Magic and ritual are a huge part of our lives. It’s obvious in some cases—for example, fantasies about death and what comes after it. Reincarnation, heavens, hells. But it can be more subtle, too, in the way that we think about fortune or justice or whether a desert is just or unjust. I think the world as most of us experience it, as most people in the world actually live life, is quite thoroughly fantastical, often nightmarish. It’s that feeling of being an awkward, embodied, weirdly misfitting person in a big, weird, cruel world that I ultimately try to echo: everything else is a device to produce this effect. I think it’s the most speculative element there is.</p>
<p><strong><em>Christina:</em></strong> <strong>You’ve spoken before about the difference between American audiences and Sri Lankan audiences, especially when it comes to speculative fiction. Is there anything about Sri Lankan history or culture that you felt especially passionate about sharing with American audiences, who might have been going in expecting an explicitly science fiction novel? </strong></p>
<p><strong><em>Vajra:</em></strong> Sri Lankan culture is all over the book, really. The funeral rituals, for example, are real practices: I have been to more of those than I can count. I enjoyed <em>Rakesfall</em> letting me actually set some scenes in modern Sri Lanka for once, instead of a veiled fantasy analogue of the same. I’m pleased to see that many readers, American and otherwise, have been handling it just fine. Sometimes people get in touch to share interpretations that would have never occurred to me because they’ve read the book through their own cultural lenses, as is only proper, and I always enjoy that. I don’t know if anybody went in expecting a different kind of science fiction novel than the one they got, and were disappointed. And to them I say, no takebacksies. Eat your third-world vegetables, it’s good for your bones.</p>
<p><strong><em>Christina:</em></strong> <strong>Something in your acceptance speech that really stuck with me was the phrase “there is nothing inherently liberatory in the imagination, but it must be made so.” After having also just read Jordan S. Carroll’s <em>Speculative Whiteness</em>, I’ve been thinking a lot about how readers and fans tend to think of SFF as vital to our culture simply because it allows us to imagine possible futures and other worlds. And of course Le Guin has written quite a bit about this too (“The exercise of the imagination is dangerous to those who profit from the way things are because it has the power to show that the way things are is not permanent, not universal, not necessary”). I’ve been guilty of assuming a sort of inherent moral value in speculative fiction because I love it so much—and assuming that everyone gets that The Empire (<em>Star Wars</em>) and The Capital (<em>Hunger Games</em>) are the fascist bad guys. But the fascists also think they’re Luke Skywalker or Katniss Everdeen. What are your thoughts on the possibilities speculative fiction allows writers when it comes to discussing liberation and futurism? What is the responsibility of the writer when it comes to discussing politics, or imparting a specific viewpoint? </strong></p>
<p><strong><em>Vajra:</em></strong> I’ve also been reading and enjoying <em>Speculative Whiteness</em>! Fascists are generally poor readers and wretched, accursed souls, but being human they have as much access to the imagination as anybody else. I suspect it’s that they find triumphant revenge stories appealing—and a sufficiently dogged bad reader can get revanchism out of anything. Blood-and-soil from a stone. But it’s not always just that they’re particularly bad readers: it’s also that a great deal of imaginative literature has politics that are unconsidered (at best) and nakedly fascist at worst, as <em>The Iron Dream</em> depicted with the most heavy-handed irony imaginable only to find the American Nazi Party putting the book on their recommended reading list. Or to look at real examples of the same,<em> Farnham’s Freehold</em> or<em> Altneuland </em>don’t require much in the way of an incorrigible reading. The ferroneiric tendency sustains to this day, and not just in cheesy space opera and milsf, either.</p>
<p>Exercising the imagination isn’t inherently virtuous because it’s entirely possible, and in fact all too common, for the science-fictional imagination to imagine new worlds underwritten by genocide and dispossession. There are plenty of people who find it pleasurable to imagine a better world specifically for themselves and their own—that it will get worse for everyone else is of no consequence. Silicon Valley’s doing extra great with this lately.</p>
<p>I think writers, like all artists, have a responsibility to act as human cultural workers in an actual society, rather than as free neutrons binging and bonging around a deracinated cultural nucleus and waiting for it to explode. If we can agree that art is political, it should follow that it matters quite deeply <em>what</em> the art’s politics are. And that requires writers to read a lot more and question the worldbuilding. This is difficult work and it takes time. If you’re not even <em>trying</em>, though, then you’ve made of yourself a puppet and without even asking whose hand is up yours.</p>
<p><strong><em>Christina:</em></strong> <strong>How would you describe the two souls we follow through <em>Rakesfall</em>? What is their relationship to each other, and what was the most interesting part of exploring how that changed throughout time?</strong></p>
<p><strong><em>Vajra:</em></strong> They are little fragments of personhood that get quite mixed up with each other. They are much closer than lovers, and yet always frustrated by each other. On some occasions they are the same person. It was an interesting way to talk about personhood itself: the idea that we are not like, a glowing ball of self sitting alone in the dark vault of our skull, but something more like one channel in a river delta. Other channels are both tributary and distributary relative to you. Water comes to you and goes from you. You are still recognizably you, you’re physically rooted in the world, but at the same time you are changed constantly, irrevocably, by your contact with others—your absorption into them, your assimilation of them into yourself. We do this with everyone that becomes important to us, and that doesn’t only mean the people who are physically present in our lives: we do it to our own lost loved ones, we do it with the long-dead authors of texts that we read and become obsessed by, we do it to our imaginary characters. I wanted to talk about this very strange and very ordinary thing using the device that seemed most apt for it.</p>
<p><em><strong>Christina:</strong> </em><strong>Can you talk a bit about the color red in this novel? How did that become a thematic throughline for you?</strong></p>
<p><strong><em>Vajra:</em></strong> Red is the colour of revolution, of course. It’s been a recognizable trope for most of two hundred years now. The red flag, dipped in the blood of martyrs. The short-story-turned-chapter, “Redder” was based on a historical event of what one could call left-on-left violence, a redder-than-thou murder. I was only seven when it happened, growing up in a nominally democratic socialist country reeling under brutal neoliberal reforms as well as multiple militant uprisings that were all at some point, in some fashion, leftist. I am familiar with the problems—both state repression which while overwhelmingly violent and thoroughly fascist is at least straightforward to understand, vs. the many, many corkscrewy ways in which revolutions fail.</p>
<p><strong><em>Christina:</em></strong> <strong>The concepts of history and memory are explored both in a larger cultural way and in a personal/familial way. Can you talk about the importance of grandmothers in <em>Rakesfall</em>?</strong></p>
<p><strong><em>Vajra:</em></strong> The grandmothers in “The War of the Grandmothers” are loosely based on my own. Neither lived to see this century, but they both left an impression. Grandmothers are cool and often very weird. They are also a handy measure of deep time. Say we imagine a generation is such that a century is about one grandmother—this was true of my family on both sides, my grandmothers being born in 1890 and 1900 respectively. A mere hundred grandmothers would take us all the way back to the supposed beginnings of human civilization. You could pack the hundred grandmothers into a couple of big buses.</p>
<p><strong><em>Christina:</em></strong> <strong>One of the considerations for the Le Guin Prize is how the work speaks to themes present in Le Guin’s work, including non-violence or alternatives to conflict. This novel is not without violence, and in fact one of the pivotal moments is a moment of violence. How do you approach a work that by necessity will deal with a certain amount of bloodshed, and balance that with hopefulness and understanding? </strong></p>
<p><strong><em>Vajra:</em></strong> I’m not, generally, writing characters that it would be good or safe to emulate. Violence does seem inherent to the set of problems I write in, or perhaps it’s just that the fantastic is a larger-than-life rendition of the world and a bit of the old grand guignol makes good theatre. Sometimes the movements and expressions of the actors on stage are exaggerated and stylized so that people can see from the back row. Violence is a political question: what action is necessary to make change in a violent world? Resistance to a violent system is conventionally cast as itself initiatory or provocative by the people who have a stake in the violent system, which has become invisible and normalized over time. I feel that non-violence as a shaming tactic requires at least a residual capacity for shame in the oppressor, because there are such things as shameless states unfazed at the thought of inflicting mass death, and moral appeals generally don’t work with fascists. Obviously you can exercise power in ways that at least some people think are non-violent, such as withholding labour—we’re currently in a situation that desperately requires massive coordinated global general strikes to counteract the distributed supply chains and multinationals of the neoliberal era, but the organizational capability to do anything even remotely of the sort has been severely degraded, compromised, or outright destroyed over the last few decades, and any rebuilding will happen under extremely dangerous conditions. But of course <em>Rakesfall</em> is not a book about labour organizing. Instead, it’s a book about personhood (leaky) and solidarity (timeless): I think these are important things to understand, about the world and ourselves, and hopefully they are among the many realizations and perspectives that could help us build ourselves into the kind of people who can weather the information storm of ideology and propaganda with our principles intact. To me, hopefulness comes from principle, and the principle can be neither violence nor non-violence, which are both means, but justice, which is an end.</p>
<p><strong><em>Christina:</em></strong> <strong>What advice would you give your 14-year-old self?</strong></p>
<p><strong><em>Vajra:</em></strong> It would be so difficult not to fall into platitudes. All the things adults say to children: it gets better; hard times will come but you’ll get through them; don’t start smoking, it’s a huge pain in the ass to quit. What else could I say that would run against the grain of the obvious? I could say: don’t worry so much about being bad at school, it won’t matter. But who knows what he would do with that terrible knowledge… No, I’d probably just say: Don’t worry, you’ll be fine. Look, the world will never make sense but we believe you will become something that can survive it, one day.</p>
