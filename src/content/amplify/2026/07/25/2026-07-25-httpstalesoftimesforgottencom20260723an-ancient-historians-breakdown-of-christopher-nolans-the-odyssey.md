<p>Christopher Nolan’s <em>The Odyssey</em> is perhaps the most highly anticipated new film of the 2020s. It has already received near-universal acclaim from film critics and earned more at the box office in its opening weekend than any of Nolan’s previous films, including <em>The Dark Knight Rises</em> (2012) and <em>Oppenheimer </em>(2023). It has only been out for one week and is already fueling a culture-wide resurgence of interest in ancient Greek myth and literature.</p>
<p>Although Nolan’s creative decisions for the film raised controversy even before the film was released, I promised myself early on that I would not weigh in on the film until after I had seen it myself. And I will be honest: I went into Nolan’s <em>Odyssey</em> with the expectation, based on my previous experience with big Hollywood adaptations of Greek myths and the images and previews that I had seen beforehand, that it would be unconscionably bad. As I watched it, I found some things I disliked, but, overall, I was pleasantly surprised. By the time I was about an hour or so into it, I found myself really enjoying it and actually liking some (although certainly not all) of the creative decisions Nolan made.</p>
<p>If I had directed a film adaptation of the<em> Odyssey</em>, I would have done it very differently, but, as I have reminded myself repeatedly, I am not the target audience of this film; this film is for <em>normal people</em> who like movies, spectacle, and mythic fantasy, not a nerd like me who has spent over a decade studying ancient Greek history and literature, can read Ancient Greek and Latin, has a master’s degree in Ancient Greek and Roman Studies, and has been writing an ancient history blog for the past decade. In this post, I will lay out my thoughts on the film itself and some of the criticisms other people have made of it.</p>
<p><strong>No such thing as a “historically accurate” <em>Odyssey</em></strong></p>
<p>Before I get into my own review of the film, I want to briefly address some of the criticisms other people have made of it.</p>
<p>One of the most prominent criticisms of Nolan’s <em>Odyssey</em> has been that the material culture depicted in the film is not “historically accurate.” It is factually true that the film’s costumes, sets, and props do not accurately reflect Greek material culture in any historical period, and most of the costumes especially are purely fantastical with no historical basis. The film does include <em>occasional</em> nods to historical Greek material culture (which I will discuss later), but these are rare.</p>
<p>Nonetheless, the use of this as a criticism of the film reflects a fundamental misunderstanding of the original <em>Odyssey</em>. There is no such thing as a “historically accurate” <em>Odyssey</em>, nor can there ever be, because the original <em>Odyssey</em> itself is not set in a real historical time period; it is set in the Age of Heroes, which is a vague, imaginary, mythical time in the remote past. Although modern readers have tried to equate the Age of Heroes to the historical Late Bronze Age in the Aegean, there is very little in either the <em>Iliad</em> or the <em>Odyssey</em> that is actually characteristic of the real-life Bronze Age.</p>
<p>During the historical Late Bronze Age, Greece was dominated by centralized states with powerful kings and highly developed bureaucracies manned by educated, literate scribes, who kept administrative records on clay tablets using the Linear B syllabary. A large amount of scholarly knowledge of Mycenaean Greece comes from these administrative records. The Linear B tablets include records of sacrifices, which indicate that, during this period, the most prominent deities worshipped in these kingdoms were Zeus, Poseidon, and a goddess called “Potnia,” which means “the Mistress.”</p>
<p>As the name “Bronze Age” might suggest, during this period, bronze was overwhelmingly the dominant metal for both weapons and tools. The Mycenaean kingdoms were highly interconnected with other great empires of the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean, and a significant rival to these kingdoms was the Hittite Empire, which was the dominant power in Asia Minor.</p>

<p>The world of the Homeric epics looks <em>nothing</em> like this. Instead, the <em>Iliad</em> and the <em>Odyssey</em> depict a decentralized world in which petty, insecure “big men” rule a patchwork of small, local kingdoms. The poems never mention any of these kingdoms as having any kind of scribal administrators or a scribal education system.</p>
<p>In fact, there is only one brief, apparent mention of writing at all in either of the Homeric epics; this occurs in the <em>Iliad</em> 6.168–170, in which King Proitos of Argos inscribes “σήματα λυγρά,” which means “baleful signs,” on a folded tablet, gives it to the hero Bellerophon, and orders him to deliver it to the king of Lykia. Unbeknownst to Bellerophon, the signs on the tablet instruct the king of Lykia to execute its bearer. The poem treats writing as a strange, sinister, novel invention.</p>
<p>The Homeric epics sometimes use “Potnia” as an epithet of certain goddesses, including Hera, Athena, and Artemis, but they contain no memory of the time when a major goddess was known primarily by this title. By contrast, Aphrodite (who is never mentioned at all in any sources from the Bronze Age) is a major deity in both the <em>Iliad</em> and the<em> Odyssey</em>, and the poems display no memory of a time when she was not worshipped in the Greek world.</p>
<p>Although most of the <em>weapons </em>in the <em>Iliad</em> and the <em>Odyssey </em>are made of bronze, ordinary tools are invariably made of iron. Even the use of bronze for weapons is probably less a genuine memory of the Bronze Age and more a result of the fact that, in the seventh century BCE, bronze was <em>valued</em> more highly than iron and seen as more prestigious. Neither of the Homeric epics contain any mention or memory whatsoever of Hittite Empire or the Hittites; as far as the epics are concerned, the Hittites simply don’t exist.</p>
<p>But the Homeric epics don’t accurately represent the world of the seventh century BCE either! Instead, they depict a fantasy world full of what the Homerist Peter Gainsford <a href="https://kiwihellenist.blogspot.com/2016/08/the-trojan-war-2-homer.html">calls “false archaism.”</a></p>
<p>To give an example, in the Bronze Age, archers would ride chariots into battle and shoot arrows from their chariots. By the time the <em>Iliad</em> and the <em>Odyssey</em> were composed in the seventh century BCE, however, chariots were no longer used in warfare and were instead only used for sports racing. The poet of the <em>Iliad</em> apparently has some vague sense that, at some point in the past, people had used chariots in warfare, but he doesn’t understand <em>how</em> chariots were used, so he describes heroes as using them sort of like battle golf carts: they ride in their chariots to get to battle, and then they dismount to fight on foot.</p>
<p>The <a href="https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/RuleOfCool">Rule of Cool</a> is also hugely important here. For instance, in <em>Iliad</em> 6.230–236, the Akhaian hero Diomedes and the Trojan hero Glaukos trade their armors as a sign of guest-friendship, and the armor that Glaukos gives to Diomedes is made of solid gold. No one in human history has ever actually gone to battle wearing solid gold armor, because gold is an absurdly impractical material to make armor out of; it is extremely rare and expensive, it’s extremely heavy, so it would make the wearer less mobile, and it’s very soft and malleable, so it wouldn’t provide the wearer very effective protection. But none of that matters, because solid gold armor <em>sounds cool</em>.</p>

<p>In <em>Iliad</em> 16.784–786, Patroklos charges three times at the Trojans and kills nine men in each charge. Is this at all plausible? No, <em>but it sounds cool</em>. In <em>Iliad</em> 19.404–424, Akhilleus’s horse Xanthos prophesies to him his imminent death. Did horses really speak prophecies in Archaic Greece? No, <em>but it sounds cool</em>. In <em>Iliad</em> 21, Akhilleus fights the river Skammandros itself, which is angry at him for polluting it with the bodies of all the men he has killed. Did men really fight rivers in Archaic Greece? No, <em>but it sounds cool</em>!</p>
<p>So far, I’ve been using examples from the <em>Iliad</em> because it is seen as the more realistic of the two poems. The fantastic elements in the <em>Odyssey</em> are even more glaringly obvious. The poem includes an island where people eat a flower that causes them to forget their homeland and want to stay on the island forever; a giant, one-eyed, man-eating Kyklops who lives in a cave; a witch who turns men into pigs; Sirens with the bodies of birds whose singing lures sailors to their deaths; a monster who snatches men off the decks of ships; and so many other elements that everyone knows are fantastical.</p>
<p>The Homeric epics themselves contain one layer of anachronisms, but there’s <em>another </em>layer of anachronisms on top of that, because, whenever the ancient Greeks and Romans depicted scenes from the Homeric epics in art, they always depicted the characters wearing contemporary styles of clothing and armor that would have been anachronistic for the time in which the epics were composed.</p>
<p>Thus, while my own heart may yearn for an adaptation of the <em>Odyssey</em> that looks authentically ancient Greek, I cannot fault Nolan’s <em>Odyssey</em> for not accurately representing the material culture of a given Greek historical period, because Nolan is simply doing the same thing that the original <em>Odyssey</em> itself, and later ancient Greek artists who interpreted it, did. It’s the Rule of Cool all the way down.</p>

<p><strong>Nolan’s departures from the original <em>Odyssey</em></strong></p>
<p>Another prominent criticism of Nolan’s <em>Odyssey</em> has been the claim that it isn’t “faithful” to the original epic. As with the previous criticism, this has <em>some </em>basis. It’s absolutely true that Nolan’s film makes significant changes to the story. It cuts large portions of the original epic, including:</p>
<ul> <li>Telemakhos’s meeting with Nestor at Pylos,</li> <li>Menelaos’s encounter with Proteus in Egypt,</li> <li>the nymph Ino’s rescue of Odysseus from Poseidon’s storm,</li> <li>Odysseus’s tricking of Polyphemos by telling him that his name is “Nobody,”</li> <li>Hermes’s assistance to Odysseus on Kirke’s island,</li> <li>the island of the lotos-eaters,</li> <li>Aiolos’s island and the bag of the winds,</li> <li>Odysseus’s time on Skheria with the Phaiakians,</li> <li>Odysseus’s fight with Iros,</li> <li>Odysseus’s revealing of his identity to Eumaios,</li> <li>and Odysseus’s final reunion with his father Laërtes.</li> </ul>
<p>I think it is absolutely fair to criticize a film when it changes a story in a way that makes the story worse or less appealing. Nonetheless, at the same time, changing the story is <em>not inherently a bad thing in and of itself</em>. Every generation reinvents and reinterprets the stories of generations that came before, and stories only remain alive as long as people continue to reinvent them.</p>
<p>A film made in the twenty-first century needs to appeal to a twenty-first-century audience, and, although ancient and modern audiences share a common, basic humanity, our worldviews and values are radically different. We find different meanings in stories than our ancestors did 2,600 years ago.</p>
<p>The original <em>Odyssey</em> itself radically departs from versions of myths that were standard at the time it was composed. For instance, the <em>Odyssey</em> is the only surviving Greek literary work from the Archaic Period that describes a marriage between Aphrodite and Hephaistos; every other surviving source from this period takes for granted that Aphrodite is unmarried.</p>
<p>The <em>Iliad </em>18.382–383 expressly describes Hephaistos as being married to one of the Kharites (the goddesses of charm and creativity). Hesiod’s <em>Theogony</em> 945–946 describes Hephaistos as being married to Aglaia, the youngest of the Kharites. Neither poem mentions the existence of any previous marriage between Hephaistos and Aphrodite. The<em> Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite </em>describes her as seducing the mortal Trojan prince Ankhises and never mentions her having a husband who might object to such a union.</p>
<p>By contrast, in the <em>Odyssey</em> 8.266–366, the blind bard Demodokos tells a ribald, comic tale in which Aphrodite is married to Hephaistos but having an adulterous affair with Ares. This whole story is, as far as we can tell, purely the <em>Odyssey</em> poet’s invention; it does not exist anywhere else in Archaic Greek literature. The story is humorous precisely because its portrayal of the gods acting disgracefully subverts the poem’s original audience’s expectation that gods would behave morally. It was only much later, due to the <em>Odyssey</em>’s own cultural influence, that Greeks and Romans came to accept this story as standard myth.</p>

<p>The <em>Iliad</em> and the <em>Odyssey</em>‘s flagrantly irreverent portrayals of the gods actually offended many philosophically-inclined ancient Greeks. Some of the earliest surviving references to the poems come from Greek poets and philosophers criticizing them. For instance, the pre-Socratic philosopher Xenophanes of Kolophon (lived c. 570 – c. 478 BCE) says in a fragment (DK 21B11):</p>
<blockquote> <p>πάντα θεοῖς ἀνέθηκαν Ὅμηρός θ’ Ἡσίοδός τε<br/>ὅσσα παρ’ ἀνθρώποισιν ὀνείδεα καὶ ψόγος ἐστίν,<br/>κλέπτειν μοιχεύειν τε καὶ ἀλλήλους ἀπατεύειν.</p> </blockquote>
<blockquote> <p>Homer and Hesiod have attributed to the gods<br/>all things that are shameful and blameworthy among mortals:<br/>theft, adultery, and mutual deception.</p> </blockquote>
<p>The pre-Socratic philosopher Herakleitos of Ephesos (fl. late sixth – early fifth century BCE) went even further (Fr. 42 D., 119 B):</p>
<blockquote> <p>τόν τε Ὅμηρον ἔφασκεν ἄξιον ἐκ τῶν ἀγώνων ἐκβάλλεσθαι καὶ ῥαπίζεσθαι, καὶ Ἀρχίλοχον ὁμοίως.</p> </blockquote>
<blockquote> <p>He used to say that Homer deserved to be expelled from the contests and flogged, and Arkhilokhos likewise.</p> </blockquote>
<p>Many of the Greek myths that are most widely beloved today are inventions of even later poets, who also departed from the traditions that were standard at the time. Take the story of Orpheus and Eurydike, which is probably the most prominent Greek myth in 2020s popular culture (aside from, perhaps, the <em>Odyssey</em> itself).</p>
<p>The ancient Greeks primarily knew Orpheus as the reputed author of theological and cosmogonic poetry and the founder of a mystery religion known as Orphism, whose followers believed in reincarnation, practiced secret initiations, followed strict rules for ritual purity in their daily lives (which included a prohibition on consuming the flesh of animals), could not be buried wearing wool, and were often buried with metal (usually gold) tablets inscribed with instructions for the afterlife.</p>
<p>Early Greek sources about Orpheus contain no mention of him having a wife named Eurydike at all, nor do they describe him as descending to the underworld specifically to bring her back. This whole story appears to be a relatively late, secondary addition to Orpheus’s mythology. The Roman poet Ovid (lived 43 BCE – c. 17 CE), however, tells the story in his <em>Metamorphoses</em> 10.1–85, making it into a powerful, tragic love story, representing the lengths to which people will go for love, the transformative power of music, and the inevitability and irreversibility of death.</p>

<p>After the Roman Empire’s conversion to Christianity over the course of the fourth and fifth centuries CE, the Orphic mysteries stopped mattering to people. People stopped copying the theological and cosmogonic poems attributed to Orpheus, and this body of poetry was mostly lost. But the tragic love story of Orpheus and Eurydike told by Ovid still remained moving and powerful. It appealed to poets and artists during the Renaissance and has continued to appeal to modern storytellers.</p>
<p>Today, this myth is the primary subject of Anaïs Mitchell’s acclaimed musical <em>Hadestown</em> (which was originally performed in 2006 and released as a concept album in 2010 before eventually premiering off-Broadway in 2016 and on Broadway in 2019). It is one of the main storylines in Netflix’s mythological black comedy series <em>Kaos</em> (2024). Multiple critically acclaimed films of the past decade, including Céline Sciamma’s <em>Portrait of a Lady on Fire</em> (2019) and Chloé Zhao’s <em>Hamnet</em> (2025), have included retellings of the story and used it as a motif. Meanwhile, Supergiant Games’ smash hit video game <em>Hades </em>(2020) features Orpheus and Eurydike as secondary characters.</p>
<p>Thus, today, anyone who knows anything about mythology knows Orpheus as the devoted lover of Eurydike, while only scholars remember his older significance as the revealer of theological mysteries. This is how myths endure for thousands of years: through adaptation and change. The same principle applies to every ancient story, including the <em>Odyssey</em>.</p>

<p>Even if we set that aside, practical considerations also mean that no modern film adaptation of the <em>Odyssey</em> can ever be an exact retelling of the original. Nolan’s <em>Odyssey</em> is nearly three hours long, which is virtually the limit of what today’s audiences are willing to sit through in a theater, but a full verbal recitation of the <em>Odyssey</em> would take somewhere between <em>sixteen and eighteen hours</em> (depending how fast one recites). Fitting the whole story into a three-hour movie necessarily requires a director to make certain cuts.</p>
<p>On top of this, the <em>Odyssey</em> is an extraordinarily complex and multivarious work, and it does not fit neatly into any modern genre, since it contains elements of fantasy, comedy, horror, and tragedy. The episode with Polyphemos in Book 9 is hilarious and reads like satire, but the <em>nekyia</em> or raising of the dead in Book 11 is one of the creepiest, most disturbing, most existential scenes in all of world literature. The epic contains all of this and so much else.</p>
<p>Our criterion for assessing whether Nolan’s <em>Odyssey</em> is a good adaptation, then, should not be whether it makes changes to the source material at all, but rather whether the changes it makes are ones that make the story better suited for its medium (a three-hour, big-budget, theatrical film) and target audience (twenty-first-century western movie-goers). I do not expect any modern adaptation to capture <em>everything</em> about the original poem. I only expect it to capture <em>some</em> things.</p>
<p><strong>Nolan’s <em>Odyssey</em>’s language and register</strong></p>
<p>This brings me to my next point. Some people have criticized the fact that (nearly) all the dialogue in Nolan’s <em>Odyssey</em> is in contemporary American English, and the characters all use standard American English accents, but this is neither surprising nor problematic. After all, Nolan himself is a British-American filmmaker, this film was produced by Universal Pictures, which is an American Hollywood-based film production company, and the film’s primary target audience is in the English-speaking world, with the largest part of that audience residing in North America.</p>
<p>Pragmatically speaking, if a filmmaker wants large, primarily English-speaking audiences to come to the theater to see a film, then the dialogue <em>has</em> to be in English. As much as I personally wish that a film adaptation of the <em>Odyssey</em> could be entirely in Homeric Greek, the only people who would show up to see that film would be hardcore classics nerds, and there just aren’t enough of us for a film of this scale to earn a profit off us alone.</p>
<p>If the film has to be in English, then any form of English will be <em>just as anachronistic as any other form of English</em>. Nolan could have used Shakespearean English with British Received Pronunciation accents, but that would have just made the film sound less familiar and wouldn’t have been any closer to Homeric Greek. Instead, it makes sense to use the accent that will seem the most natural and least foreign to the largest share of the film’s primary audience. In this case, that is a standard North American English accent.</p>
<p>There is, however, one scene in the film whose choice of language <em>did</em> strike me as problematic. Early in the film, shortly after Odysseus and his men leave Troy, they raid a town for food and supplies. The film does not say which town this is, but, in the original <em>Odyssey</em>, it is stated to be Ismaros, a town of the Kikones, a Thrakian tribe who were allied with the Trojans.</p>
<p>Nolan’s film portrays Odysseus as interrogating a captured townsman (portrayed by Michael Vlamis, the only actor of Greek heritage in the entire film) who speaks <em>Modern Greek</em>, which is portrayed as a completely foreign language to Odysseus and his crew, who are only able to understand the man through a translator.</p>
<p>I think that Nolan’s intention here was most likely to include a nod to the fact that this film is based on an ancient Greek epic. After all, this scene is the only time in the film when any character speaks a real language other than English. Nonetheless, I found this scene very problematic, because it implies (probably inadvertently) that modern English-speaking North Americans are the true heirs of the ancient Greeks and that modern Greeks are barbarians whom the ancient Greeks would not recognize as their cultural descendants.</p>
<p>As I discuss in <a href="https://talesoftimesforgotten.com/2020/02/08/are-modern-greeks-descended-from-the-ancient-greeks/">this post from February 2020</a> and <a href="https://talesoftimesforgotten.com/2021/01/14/did-the-dorian-invasion-really-happen/">this other post from January 2021</a>, this idea is known as the Fallmerayer thesis, and it has a very dark history. During World War II, when the Axis Powers occupied Greece, the Nazis used the narrative that Germans are the purest living relatives of ancient Dorian Greeks and that modern Greeks are degenerate, racially impure imposters to justify committing atrocities against the modern Greek population. I highly doubt that Nolan is aware of this history, but his portrayal of the “barbarian” Kikones as speaking Modern Greek while all the Greek characters in the film speak American English is an uncomfortable reminder of it.</p>

<p><strong>The overall portrayal of Odysseus</strong></p>
<p>Matt Damon initially seemed to me to be a very strange choice to play Odysseus, and I’m still not convinced that he was the best actor for the part, but my overall opinion after having seen the film is that Damon’s Odysseus is more-or-less ok—not <em>great</em>, but ok.</p>
<p>In the original <em>Odyssey</em>, Odysseus is a charismatic, loquacious, amoral, cunning scoundrel. He unabashedly lies, cheats, steals, manipulates, puts people who trust him in harm’s way, and outright murders to get what he wants, and he’s clever enough that he usually gets away with it. He is constantly inventing elaborate fake identities and backstories for himself. He is also arrogant, bordering on narcissistic. His vengeance, although it is slow and carefully planned, is also terrible and implacable, and he will not stop until he utterly destroys all those who have wronged him.</p>
<p>In Book 22, Odysseus (with the aid of Telemakhos, Eumaios, Philoitios, and Athena) brutally massacres all 108 trapped, initially unarmed suitors of Penelope, including some who desperately beg him for mercy. He even wants to kill the bard Phemios, who performed for the suitors, and only spares him because Telemakhos intercedes, telling Odysseus that Phemios only performed for the suitors unwillingly. When they have finished slaughtering the suitors themselves, Odysseus orders Telemakhos to kill all the enslaved women whom the suitors raped; Telemakhos promptly hangs them all.</p>
<p>Even by the brutal standards of the world of Archaic Greek epic, Odysseus in not in any sense a morally good person or a person whom readers should wish to emulate. By twenty-first-century conceptions, he is far more of an antihero than a hero. Personality-wise, he has a great deal in common with Walter White, the protagonist of the series <em>Breaking Bad</em>, and Saul Goodman, the protagonist of <em>Better Call Saul</em>.</p>
<p>Despite this, Odysseus <em>has</em> his virtues. He seems to genuinely care about his family, including his wife Penelope, his son Telemakhos, his father Laërtes, his mother Antikleia, and his dog Argos, and wants to get back to them. He claims he cares about his men as well and tries (unsuccessfully) to keep them alive. He is highly intelligent, a master of tricks and stratagems, and always multiple steps ahead of his enemies. He has an effortless ability to charm those he encounters, and he can seemingly talk his way out of anything. He is courageous in the face of danger, and skilled in many areas (including archery, boxing, and hand-to-hand combat).</p>
<p>Most shockingly of all, several of the people Odysseus <em>enslaves</em> (Eumaios, Philoitios, and Eurykleia) apparently perceive him as a kind enough master that they have remained steadfastly loyal to him, despite his twenty-year absence, and have been actively hoping and praying for his return. Admittedly, because we only see them from Odysseus’s perspective, it is hard to know their true feelings, and they may only prefer Odysseus as the lesser of two evils compared to the cruel suitors, but, even if that is the case, it is still noteworthy.</p>
<p>The combination of all these traits make Odysseus a fascinating protagonist on his own. The <em>Odyssey</em> heightens this fascination by putting this unconventional hero in situations that we might expect more conventional heroes to face. What happens when you take a Walter White or Saul Goodman-type character and lock him in a cave with a man-eating, one-eyed giant?</p>
<p>The <em>Odyssey</em> is, in many ways, a postmodern epic that just happens to be over twenty-six centuries old. In some ways, it is <em>too</em> postmodern for modern cinema. Modern audiences love an antihero, but film companies are rarely willing to bank on a protagonist who does not, on some level, reflect normative social values. As a result, every recent screen adaptation of the <em>Odyssey</em> has had to tone down Odysseus’s deceitful, manipulative, and violent tendencies, and Nolan’s <em>Odyssey</em> is no exception to this.</p>
<p>Nolan’s film portrays Odysseus as much more honorable and merciful than he is in the original epic. The very first time we see Matt Damon’s face as Odysseus is when he saves the dog Argos from being thrown off a cliff as a puppy. The film invents a recurring motif where Odysseus plucks his bow string once every time before he loads an arrow in order to give his opponent warning so that the fight will be “fair.” The film’s version of the massacre of the suitors not only cuts Odysseus’s order to Telemakhos to kill the enslaved women but also portrays Odysseus as sparing some of the <em>suitors</em> who bow to him. All of this is worlds away from the Odysseus in the original <em>Odyssey</em>.</p>
<p>Whatever else one may say about these changes, though, they certainly make Odysseus more likeable for a modern audience—and they aren’t <em>boring</em> either. The thing about the bowstring adds an intriguing eccentricity to Odysseus’s character that I kind of liked. Meanwhile, there’s just enough of Homer’s trickster Odysseus in the film that I didn’t feel <em>woefully</em> disappointed.</p>
<p>One of my biggest complaints about Uberto Pasolini’s film <em>The Return </em>(2024) that <a href="https://talesoftimesforgotten.com/2025/03/14/everything-i-liked-and-hated-about-uberto-pasolinis-the-return-2024/">I discussed in my review of that film</a> was the fact that it made Odysseus into a laconic, stoic war veteran who refuses to talk about his past. I felt this depiction was both one-dimensional and very alien from who Odysseus is in the original <em>Odyssey</em>. That film also thoroughly butchered the scene in which Odysseus, disguised as a beggar, speaks to Penelope. By contrast, Matt Damon’s Odysseus is more talkative and has some element of the wily commander about him. This may be partly because Nolan’s <em>Odyssey</em> shows him in more diverse situations than <em>The Return</em> does, and, as a result, we see more sides of his character.</p>

<p><strong>The overall portrayal of Penelope, Telemakhos, Eumaios, Antinoös, and Athena</strong></p>
<p>The next most important character in the <em>Odyssey</em> after Odysseus himself is, of course, his wife Penelope. As scholars have long observed, the original <em>Odyssey</em> gives Penelope very little characterization and largely leaves her personal feelings enigmatic. Nolan’s <em>Odyssey</em>, however, fleshes her out. This version of Penelope, whom Anne Hathaway portrays brilliantly, misses her husband, but, at the same time, has grown accustomed to ruling Ithaka in his absence and doesn’t want to surrender her power to one of the suitors or to her and Odysseus’s son Telemakhos.</p>
<p>In the original <em>Odyssey</em>, when Odysseus speaks to Penelope while he is still disguised as a beggar, and he tells Penelope that he has seen her husband, she asks him what Odysseus was wearing, and Odysseus is able to describe his own clothing accurately, which leads Penelope to know the report is true.</p>
<p>Nolan’s <em>Odyssey</em> takes this and adds another layer by having Penelope give Odysseus a highly distinctive pin and tell him before he leaves for Troy to wear it all the time so that, if someone comes to her claiming to have seen him, she will able to verify if they really have based on whether they can describe the pin. It’s a change that demonstrates Penelope’s own intelligence and foresight.</p>

<p>Tom Holland plays Telemakhos. He’s technically too old for the part, since Telemakhos is supposed to be just older than twenty, while Holland has just turned thirty, but I’m willing to forgive this, since he still has a bit of a babyface, and his acting is perfect for the role. He brings the same earnest, somewhat naïve energy that he exhibits in his performances as Spiderman, and it suits the part perfectly.</p>

<p><em>ABOVE: Screenshot of Tom Holland as Telemakhos in Nolan’s </em>Odyssey</p>
<p>John Leguizamo’s Eumaios is also great. In the original poem, Eumaios is Odysseus’s loyal, enslaved swineherd who takes Odysseus in when he is disguised as a beggar. Nolan’s version changes the character significantly by making him be blind, by having Odysseus specifically leave him behind on Ithaka because he is his “most loyal servant,” and by having him act as a tutor and foster father to Telemakhos, but I thought all these changes worked well.</p>
<p>Nolan cut the blind bard Demodokos (who only appears on the island of the Phaiakians, a whole section of the epic that Nolan cut entirely), so it was fitting that he made another secondary character blind so that the film would still include blind representation. The film handles Eumaios’s blindness fairly well, showing it as a genuine disability while at the same time showing that Eumaios is able to do things.</p>
<p>Some people might think that the portrayal of Odysseus as having an enslaved man tutor his son is anachronistic for ancient Greece, but, in historical reality, it was the norm, at least in the Classical Period, for a wealthy Greek man to appoint an enslaved man known as a παιδαγωγός (<em>paidagōgós</em>) to chaperone and educate his son, and Nolan’s <em>Odyssey</em> seems to put Eumaios in this role.</p>
<p>Two things about Eumaios’s mentoring of Telemakhos, however, are historically off. First, Eumaios is apparently still a swineherd in Nolan’s retelling, whereas, generally, a <em>paidagōgós</em> would be an educated enslaved man who would hold a certain level of privilege relative to other enslaved people and would be excused from a dirty task like herding swine. Second, the film portrays Eumaios as teaching Telemakhos how to fight with a sword. In historical reality, though, weapons training was virtually unheard of in ancient Greece; instead, the main subjects that a <em>paidagōgós</em> would tutor his master’s son in were reading and writing, poetry, music, and athletics.</p>

<p>Meanwhile, Robert Pattinson makes a perfect, scenery-chewing Antinoös. Some people who only know him from his early work in <em>Harry Potter </em>and <em>Twilight</em> expressed doubts about his casting, but, when I heard he’d been cast, I immediately thought of his performance as Louis, Duke of Guyenne, in David Michôd’s <em>The King</em> (2019) and knew he was exactly the right actor for this role. I was not disappointed. He makes an outstanding villain you just love to hate, and his delivery of the line, “Would someone get these <em>beggars</em> out of here!?” has already become an internet meme.</p>

<p>Of the main secondary characters, the one I thought Nolan’s <em>Odyssey</em> did a poor job of depicting was Athena, who is played by Zendaya. Zendaya is a highly talented actress, but, in my mind, she didn’t really fit well as Athena in this film. This is partly because she looks too young to play a warrior goddess of wisdom, and it feels strange to have Matt Damon’s middle-aged Odysseus looking for guidance in a woman who looks younger than half his age, especially when the other goddesses in the film (e.g., Samantha Morton’s Kirke and Cherlize Theron’s Kalypso) are both played by older actresses.</p>
<p>Then again, Zendaya is almost thirty, and my impression that she looks too young may be partly because I’m accustomed to seeing her playing teenaged characters like Rue in the HBO series <em>Euphoria</em> and M.J. in the Spiderman films. I would have liked to have seen a slightly older, thirtysomething actress in the role.</p>
<p>I also thought that the film really killed Athena’s aura. In ancient Greek iconography, Athena wears a helmet and a breastplate with Medousa’s head in the center, she carries a spear and a shield, and she is often accompanied by an owl or a snake. By contrast, in Nolan’s film, Zendaya’s Athena wears only a plain white robe. This departure from the iconography makes Athena look less visually interesting and removes any visual language to indicate that Zendaya is even supposed to be Athena at all.</p>
<p>The <em>Iliad</em> and the <em>Odyssey</em> both frequently refer to Athena using the epithet γλαυκῶπις (<em>glaukôpis</em>), which means “gray-eyed,” and Nolan’s film repeatedly invokes this tradition by having Telemakhos describe two different characters—first Mentor and then Odysseus himself—as having “wise eyes, Athena’s eyes.” But for some reason, despite including this repeated line about “Athena’s eyes,” the film didn’t do <em>anything </em>to make Athena’s eyes visually distinctive.</p>
<p>Finally, I wasn’t a fan of how the film handles Mentor. In the original<em> Odyssey</em>, Athena takes Mentor’s form as a disguise when she talks to and travels with Telemakhos. Nolan’s <em>Odyssey</em> initially seems to imply that Mentor is really Athena by having Telemakhos say that he has “Athena’s eyes” and that he could be a god in disguise. But then the film apparently reveals that Mentor <em>isn’t </em>Athena by having the suitors kill him! This made it seem like they were setting up for a big reveal that Mentor is really Athena only to apparently disconfirm the thing they seemed to be setting up, which was disappointing.</p>

<p><strong>The allegedly “woke” casting decisions</strong></p>
<p>As soon as the cast for Nolan’s<em> Odyssey</em> was announced, right-wingers on the internet began to attack it as “woke” and “historically inaccurate” due to the fact that it includes Lupita Nyong’o (who is Black) as Helen of Sparta, Zendaya (who is mixed race) as Athena, and Elliot Page (who is a trans man) as the Greek soldier Sinon. These criticisms are just repetitions of the same tired, racist arguments that surfaced nearly a decade ago when the 2018 BBC One/Netflix miniseries <em>Troy: Fall of a City</em> featured Black actors as Zeus and Akhilleus. (I myself addressed these criticisms <a href="https://talesoftimesforgotten.com/2020/06/22/were-achilles-and-zeus-black-in-greek-mythology/">in a 2020 blog post</a>.)</p>
<p>Contrary to what critics have asserted, there are, in fact, Black people in the original <em>Odyssey</em>, and you don’t have to read very far into the epic to meet them. Near the very beginning of the first book of the epic, the poet describes Poseidon as having gone to visit the “Aithiopians” (<em>Od</em>. 1.22–25):</p>
<blockquote> <p>ἀλλ᾽ ὁ μὲν Αἰθίοπας μετεκίαθε τηλόθ᾽ ἐόντας,<br/>Αἰθίοπας τοὶ διχθὰ δεδαίαται, ἔσχατοι ἀνδρῶν,<br/>οἱ μὲν δυσομένου Ὑπερίονος οἱ δ᾽ ἀνιόντος,<br/>ἀντιόων ταύρων τε καὶ ἀρνειῶν ἑκατόμβης.</p> </blockquote>
<blockquote> <p>But [Poseidon] had gone among the Aithiopians who live far away,<br/>the Aithiopians who are divided in two, most distant of men:<br/>some where the son of Hyperion sets, and others where he rises,<br/>and he was receiving a hecatomb of bulls and rams.</p> </blockquote>
<p>The name “Aithiopian” was traditionally explained in antiquity as a compound of the verb αἴθω (<em>aíthō</em>), meaning “to burn” or “to shine” and the noun ὤψ (<em>ṓps</em>), meaning “face.” According to this interpretation, the name means either “Burnt-Faced” or “Shining-Faced.” The ancient Greeks used this name to refer to Black African peoples generally, and it is the source of the name of the modern country <em>Ethiopia</em>. Early Greek poetry generally characterizes Aithiopians as pious people who are especially close to the gods and are the only people among whom the gods regularly walk undisguised.</p>
<p>Dark-skinned people, however, are not merely mentioned as residents of a distant and foreign land. Odysseus describes the appearance of his own herald Eurybates as follows (<em>Od</em>. 19.246–247):</p>
<blockquote> <p>γυρὸς ἐν ὤμοισιν, μελανόχροος, οὐλοκάρηνος,<br/>Εὐρυβάτης δ᾽ ὄνομ᾽ ἔσκε:</p> </blockquote>
<blockquote> <p>He was round in the shoulders, dark-skinned, and wooly-haired,<br/>and Eurybates was his name.</p> </blockquote>
<p>Interestingly, the poet describes <em>Odysseus’s own </em>physical appearance using very similar language. In <em>Odyssey</em> 16.172–176, Athena transforms Odysseus from his disguise as a beggar back into an idealized version of his normal appearance, and the poet describes the transformation as follows:</p>
<blockquote> <p>ἦ καὶ χρυσείῃ ῥάβδῳ ἐπεμάσσατ᾽ Ἀθήνη.<br/>φᾶρος μέν οἱ πρῶτον ἐϋπλυνὲς ἠδὲ χιτῶνα<br/>θῆκ᾽ ἀμφὶ στήθεσσι, δέμας δ᾽ ὤφελλε καὶ ἥβην.<br/>ἂψ δὲ μελαγχροιὴς γένετο, γναθμοὶ δὲ τάνυσθεν,<br/>κυάνεαι δ᾽ ἐγένοντο γενειάδες ἀμφὶ γένειον.</p> </blockquote>
<blockquote> <p>Athena touched him with her golden wand.<br/>First, she set a wide mantle and a well-washed chiton<br/>on his chest, and she enhanced his stature and his youth.<br/>And he became dark-skinned again, and his jaw stretched,<br/>and the beard on his chin became dark.</p> </blockquote>
<p>In two identical lines at different points in the poem (<em>Od</em>. 6.230–231 and 23.158), when Athena transforms Odysseus into a more beautiful version of himself, the poet says, “. . . οὔλας ἧκε κὄμας, ὑακινθίνῳ ἄνθει ὁμοίας” (“She made his woolly hair like a hyacinth”).</p>
<p>Thus, the original <em>Odyssey</em> itself describes Odysseus as “dark-skinned” with hair that is “dark,” “woolly,” and “like a hyacinth.” This description does not necessarily mean that Odysseus is what we would call Black, since these descriptions could apply to someone with dark olive skin and curly, black hair, such as <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jason_Mantzoukas">the Greek American actor Jason Mantzoukas</a>, but, at the same time, these descriptions are also <em>consistent with the interpretation</em> of Odysseus as Black.</p>
<p>Some ancient Roman wall paintings depict Odysseus with dark brown skin and thick, curly hair. A modern person looking at these paintings could very easily interpret Odysseus as a Black man. This ancient evidence is, I think, more than enough to support casting a Black actor as Odysseus himself in a modern adaptation.</p>

<p>All this outrage and anger over diverse casting in films based on Greek myth feels very distinct to the era in which we have been living since 2016. Hollywood films set in the ancient Greco-Roman world have been featuring Black actors for <em>over sixty years</em>. The main cast of Stanley Kubrick’s 1960 film <em>Spartacus</em> included the Black actor Woody Strode as the Ethiopian gladiator Draba.</p>
<p>As far as I am aware, there were no high-profile objections of “blackwashing” or “historical inaccuracy” back in 2014 when Dwayne “the Rock” Johnson (who is part Black and part Samoan) played the title role in the film <em>Hercules</em>. (And, in case you think that film was “woke,” I’ll point out that its director, Brett Ratner, holds the truly stunning anti-woke credentials of being an avid Trump supporter who has been credibly accused of rape, appears in the Epstein files, and is the director the <em>Melania</em> movie!)</p>
<p>But, for some reason, ever since Donald Trump became president the first time, the existence of diverse casts in adaptations of Greek myths has suddenly become a civilizational crisis.</p>

<p>For better or worse, the most controversial castings in Nolan’s film prove to be parts that, while very <em>symbolically</em> important, don’t actually occupy very much screen time. Zendaya’s Athena is mainly implied to be working to help Odysseus behind the scenes. Elliot Page’s Sinon is literally killed within the first minute of the film after saying only one line. The fact that Page is a trans man also probably wouldn’t have been obvious to most viewers who didn’t know it already. Lupita Nyong’o, whose role as Helen attracted the fiercest ire of all the castings, has, I think, barely five lines in the whole film.</p>
<p>If anything, the way Nolan’s <em>Odyssey</em> reserved Black and trans actors for symbolically important roles that received little screentime felt almost a little exploitative, as though he intentionally cast those actors for those parts because he wanted to provoke controversy and win accolades for being supposedly “progressive” while making a film that focuses the vast majority of its actual runtime on white, cisgender actors.</p>
<p><strong>Nods to historical ancient Greek material culture</strong></p>
<p>Since I’ve covered the main cast of the film, now I will address the costumes, sets, props, and soundtrack. These include a few interesting nods to historical Greek material culture. One example of this occurs in the very first scene, in which a bard (presumably Phemios) is performing the story of the Trojan horse for the suitors in the palace of Odysseus on Ithaka. The bard is shown pounding the floor with a staff while he chants words. This is clearly supposed to be a reference to historical bards in ancient Greece who would tap a staff on the ground while performing to keep rhythm.</p>
<p>Nolan’s <em>Odyssey</em>, however, doesn’t quite get it right. Historical ancient Greek bards most likely simply tapped their staffs on the ground, but the bard in the opening scene of the film is pounding his staff forcefully to create a resounding “thud” each time he brings it down, which probably isn’t historical.</p>
<p>Nolan’s <em>Odyssey</em> also seems to miss the <em>purpose</em> of bards tapping a staff on the ground; historical bards did this to keep track of the rhythm while composing hexameter verse, but the bard in Nolan’s opening is not speaking in hexameter verse. In fact, he’s not even speaking sentences; he’s just saying evocative words.</p>

<p>The film’s background music, composed by the Swedish musician Ludwig Göransson, also includes two authentic ancient Greek instruments. The first of these is the <em>aulos</em>, which is a double-reeded woodwind instrument consisting of two pipes that are held together in the mouth while playing. The aulos is related to the oboe, and it makes a highly distinctive, shrill, dissonant sound that ancient authors describe as exciting.</p>
<p>The other ancient instrument used in the film’s score is the <em>kithara</em>, a kind of lyre that traditionally had seven strings. In the film, every time Odysseus plucks his bow, Göransson’s score plays a single note on the kithara. This is an interesting and creative touch, and it creates a motif that runs throughout the film.</p>

<p>The set design of the great hall in Odysseus’s palace on Ithaka probably comes the closest of anything in Nolan’s <em>Odyssey</em> to resembling a real thing from the historical Aegean Bronze Age. The hall takes the form of a very large, long room that contains a hearth and has two rows of columns along the side walls. This layout resembles a megaron, which is the name archaeologists have given to the great halls in Mycenaean palaces.</p>

Evans’s reconstruction of the Minoan palace at Knossos on the Greek island of Krete. These have squat, unfluted, inverted tapering shafts and bulbous, cushion-like capitals.</p>

<p>Finally, the loom at which Penelope is shown weaving in several scenes is a vertical, warp-weighted loom of the kind that actually existed in the ancient Aegean. I was quite pleased to see this, since, as I noted in <a href="https://talesoftimesforgotten.com/2025/03/14/everything-i-liked-and-hated-about-uberto-pasolinis-the-return-2024/">my previous review</a> of Pasolini’s <em>The Return</em>, that film portrayed Penelope weaving at an anachronistic horizontal handloom of the kind that was used during the Early Modern Period that did not exist at any time in the ancient Aegean.</p>

<p><strong>Anachronisms</strong></p>
<p>Apart from occasional nods like these, Nolan’s <em>Odyssey</em> is completely uninterested in creating any appearance of genuine historicity. There’s nothing wrong with this; as I said above, the original <em>Odyssey</em> wasn’t accurate to any historical period either, and we can hardly fault Nolan’s <em>Odyssey</em> for doing the same thing. Nonetheless, because this is an educational blog, I will point out a few of the anachronisms in the film so that readers will be aware of them.</p>
<p>I’ll begin by addressing the armor that Odysseus himself and his crewmembers wear throughout most of the first part of the film. I was honestly pretty disappointed by how generic and uniform this armor looks. They all wear virtually identical helmets, the design of which is clearly based on the Corinthian helmet that actors portraying Greek characters typically wear in Hollywood films. This helmet departs from the historical Corinthian helmet design in several ways—most notably by making the front more open so that the actors’ faces can be more visible.</p>

<p>In historical fact, the ancient Greeks had a wide array of different kinds of helmets with different designs, there were no uniforms, and soldiers were responsible for supplying their own armor and weapons, so, if this film were historical, we would expect to see significantly greater variety in the kinds of armor and helmets the characters wear.</p>
<p>The ancient Greeks had some interesting and unusual helmet types that never seem to make it into Hollywood films (probably because most directors and costume designers aren’t aware they existed). For instance, the <em>Iliad</em> 10.260–265 describes Meriones as equipping Odysseus in preparation for a night raid on the Trojans’ camp with a helmet made of leather adorned with alternating rows of boars’ tusks:</p>
<blockquote> <p>Μηριόνης δ’ Ὀδυσῆϊ δίδου βιὸν ἠδὲ φαρέτρην<br/>καὶ ξίφος, ἀμφὶ δέ οἱ κυνέην κεφαλῆφιν ἔθηκε<br/>ῥινοῦ ποιητήν: πολέσιν δ’ ἔντοσθεν ἱμᾶσιν<br/>ἐντέτατο στερεῶς: ἔκτοσθε δὲ λευκοὶ ὀδόντες<br/>ἀργιόδοντος ὑὸς θαμέες ἔχον ἔνθα καὶ ἔνθα<br/>εὖ καὶ ἐπισταμένως: μέσσῃ δ’ ἐνὶ πῖλος ἀρήρει.</p> </blockquote>
<blockquote> <p>Meriones gave Odysseus a bow, a quiver,<br/>and a sword, and, on his head, he placed a helmet<br/>made of leather; on the outside white teeth<br/>of a bright-toothed boar ran close-set round and round,<br/>well and skillfully made; and, in the middle, felt was sewn.</p> </blockquote>
<p>This is a description of a real kind of helmet that Mycenaean soldiers wore during the Bronze Age. Actual examples of this helmet have survived and are held in museums. This passage is the only unambiguous and undisputed description of a genuine Bronze Age artifact in either of the Homeric epics.</p>

<p>Alternatively, Odysseus’s standard iconography in Greek and Roman art has him wearing a cone-shaped helmet known as a <em>pileus</em>. This helmet is highly distinctive and characteristic of Odysseus, since he’s the only mythical figure who is usually shown wearing it. If you ever see an ancient vase painting, fresco, or mosaic with a man wearing a cone-shaped helmet, the chances are very high that that’s Odysseus. The unconventional shape of the helmet also, I think, points to (pun intended) Odysseus’s unconventional character.</p>
<p>Either the boar’s tusk helmet that Odysseus is described as wearing in the <em>Iliad</em> or the pileus helmet that he typically wears in Greek and Roman art would have been a better, more visually interesting, and more historically-grounded choice, in my opinion, than the generic, faux-Corinthian helmet he wears in Nolan’s <em>Odyssey</em>.</p>

<p>In Nolan’s film, Agamemnon wears a jet-black helmet and suit of armor that look nothing like anything from ancient Greece in any historical period and instead look like they came straight out of one of Christopher Nolan’s Batman movies. There’s nothing even vaguely historical about this costume, and I’ll be honest that I personally found it hard to take seriously. Still, a lot of people seem to think it looked incredibly cool, and the “Rule of Cool” is more important here than historicity.</p>

<p>Another rather obvious anachronism is the fact that many of the characters, including Odysseus and his men and Telemakhos, are portrayed as wearing pants for significant parts of the film. In historical reality, the Greeks <em>never</em> wore pants during any period of antiquity and regarded pants as a quintessentially barbarian garment.</p>
<p>The Hippokratic author of the late fifth-century BCE medical treatise <em>On Airs, Waters, and Places</em> says (in chapter 22) that the men of the Skythian culture, which inhabited the Pontic-Caspian steppe of what is now Ukraine and southern Russia, are effeminate and impotent because they wear pants, spend most of their time riding on horseback, don’t touch their penises, and live in a cold climate. Thus, according to the best Classical Greek medicine, pants make men effeminate and impotent.</p>

<p>At one point early in the film Eumaios scolds the goatherd Melanthios for wearing his spurs inside the palace on Ithaka. The existence of spurs, however, is anachronistic for both the Aegean Bronze Age and Archaic Greece. The earliest archaeological evidence of spurs comes from Celtic cultures of western Europe in the fifth century BCE, and the earliest mention of spurs in the Greek world occurs in Xenophon’s <em>Hipparkhikos </em>6.1, which dates to around the mid-fourth century BCE.</p>
<p>But that’s not all: even if spurs <em>had </em>been invented, there’s no reason why Melanthios, an enslaved goatherd, would ever wear them. Horses in ancient Greece were rare and expensive animals, and they were not a practical means of transportation, because Greece’s terrain is extremely rough and mountainous, and neither horseshoes nor stirrups were invented yet, meaning horses were virtually useless for any kind of long-distance travel over this terrain.</p>
<p>As a result, horses were mainly used for two purposes: war and sports racing. Owning a horse was the equivalent of owning a BMW or Lamborghini today; it was an ostentatious display of wealth. While it is conceivable that Melanthios could be out joyriding Odysseus’s horses while his master is away, if that were the case, one would think that Eumaios would scold him for<em> that</em>, rather than wearing his spurs indoors.</p>
<p>Lastly, as many others have already pointed out elsewhere, the ships that Odysseus and his men sail in in the film bear little resemblance to ancient Greek ships of any period and instead look more like medieval Norse longboats, which did not exist until over a millennium and a half after the time when the <em>Odyssey</em> was composed.</p>

<p><strong>The non-linear narrative structure and unreliability of Odysseus’s narration</strong></p>
<p>One of the things I really liked about Nolan’s <em>Odyssey</em> is that it preserves the original epic’s non-linear narrative structure, interest in the role of stories in preserving memory and shaping identity, and use of an unreliable narrator for parts of the story.</p>
<p>In the original <em>Odyssey</em>, in Books 9–12, Odysseus himself tells the story of his wanderings after the fall of Troy in the first-person to the Phaiakians at the court of King Alkinoös on the island of Skheria. Although the external narrator confirms <em>some</em> parts of Odysseus’s account, most of the story is never confirmed to be true outside of Odysseus telling it.</p>
<p>Given the fact that, at multiple other points in the epic, Odysseus tells elaborate lies about who he is and what he has done (e.g., <em>Od</em>. 13.256–286, 14.191–359, and 19.166–307), and the fact that he tells the story of his wanderings in the context of persuading the Phaiakians to help him, it is entirely possible that Odysseus is fabricating, exaggerating, or misrepresenting parts of this story.</p>
<p>Although Nolan’s <em>Odyssey</em> cuts the island of the Phaiakians entirely, it retains the original epic’s non-linear narrative structure and use of Odysseus as an unreliable narrator. In Nolan’s version, instead of telling the story of his wanderings to the Phaiakians, Odysseus tells it in pieces to Kalypso while he is recovering his memory after Kalypso has kept him on her island for seven years, drugging him with lotos flowers to make him forget about his past and his family on Ithaka. The film implies that Odysseus’s memory of the events is still imperfect and that he has motivation to lie to make himself look better and reduce his own blameworthiness in the deaths of all his men.</p>

<p><strong>The theme of <em>xenía</em></strong></p>
<p>Nolan’s<em> Odyssey </em>also picks up on one of the core themes of the original <em>Odyssey</em>, which is ξενία (<em>xenía</em>), a core principle of ancient Greek ethics. <em>Xenía</em> held that every person had a sacred and inviolable duty to provide safety, food, shelter, and comfort to any stranger or foreigner who sought hospitality. Guests, in turn, had an equally sacred obligation to honor and respect their hosts and to not take more than they needed.</p>
<p>The <em>Odyssey</em> is full of examples of both good and bad hosts. Scenes in which one character shows another character hospitality follow a standard script, in which the host welcomes the guest into their home, offers them food and drink, and does not ask them any questions about who they are until they have finished eating. The guest eats the food the host offers without complaining, usually stays the night, and eventually leaves without overstaying their welcome. Often the host and the guest exchange gifts as a sign of friendship.</p>
<p>The way the<em> Odyssey</em> shows that a character is evil is by having them violate this relationship. For instance, the Kyklops Polyphemos doesn’t welcome Odysseus and his men, immediately demands to know who they are, and eats <em>them</em> instead of offering them food. When Odysseus offers Polyphemos a gift in the form of a skin of wine, Polyphemos responds by offering Odysseus his own “gift”: a promise that he will eat Odysseus last. It is an obvious mockery of the norms of <em>xenía</em>.</p>
<p>The <em>Odyssey</em> portrays the suitors as both bad guests and bad hosts. They are bad guests because they have stayed in Odysseus’s house for three years eating up all his livestock, harassing his slaves, and conspiring to murder his son. They are also bad hosts, because, when Odysseus comes among them as a beggar, instead of treating him with hospitality, the suitors abuse him and make fun of him. At one point, one of them, Antinoös, even throws a chair at him.</p>
<p>Nolan’s <em>Odyssey </em>astutely picks up on the importance of <em>xenía</em> in the epic and takes it in an interesting, contemporarily relevant direction. Translating <em>xenía</em> as “Zeus’s law,” the film portrays the breakdown of <em>xenía</em> as a cosmic consequence of the sack of Troy that will lead inevitably to the collapse of civilization itself. As the ancient historian Bret Devereaux has <a href="https://bsky.app/profile/bretdevereaux.bsky.social/post/3mqzsv5s5bs2i">pointed out in an excellent threat on Bluesky</a>, this depiction seems to be a direct response to the xenophobia and warmongering of the present-day far-right in the United States and the western world at large. Devereaux writes:</p>
<blockquote> <p>I’d almost describe the Odyssey, thematically, as a <em>conservative</em> critique of the kind of overpowering, violent, grasping masculinity of the chuds and the far right – that revels in cruelty to strangers, in aggression and war crimes and dominance.</p> <p>Absolutely fascinating.</p> <p>Not ‘western civilization doesn’t exist’ nor ‘western civilization is bad’ but rather, “western civilization is real and good, captured in the writing andsong and we are <em>killing</em> it with our violence, cruelty and greed, we have <em>become</em> the barbarians at our own gates.”</p> <p>Conservative in that sense.</p> </blockquote>
<p><strong>The Bronze Age collapse and the Sea Peoples</strong></p>
<p>Although I liked Nolan’s emphasis on the importance of <em>xenía</em>, one aspect of his reframing that I <em>didn’t</em> like is the fact that he tries to connect the events of the <em>Odyssey</em> to the historical Bronze Age collapse and specifically incorporates the Sea Peoples, various marauding, seafaring groups who attacked many of the Bronze Age kingdoms, including Egypt, in the early twelfth century BCE.</p>
<p>Neither the <em>Odyssey</em> itself nor any other work of ancient Greek literature ever mentions the Sea Peoples, and Nolan’s attempt to shoehorn them into the film felt extremely forced. I cringed every time the film mentioned them.</p>
<p>I also cringed when Odysseus says to Penelope during their first meeting when he is disguised as a beggar, “Our age of bronze is collapsing.” As many readers are probably already aware, the term “Bronze Age” is a modern historiographical convention, and no one during the Bronze Age knew they were living in the Bronze Age.</p>
<p>Hesiod’s <em>Works and Days</em>, an early Greek poem that dates to close to the same time as the composition of the <em>Iliad</em> and the <em>Odyssey</em>, does include a Bronze Age among the ages of mankind (alongside a Golden Age, a Silver Age, an Age of Heroes, and an Iron Age), but, in Hesiod’s telling, the era of the Trojan War and Odysseus corresponds to Age of Heroes, not the Bronze Age. Hesiod also wasn’t aware of the Sea Peoples or the event that modern historians call the Bronze Age collapse.</p>
<p>All this being said, the original <em>Odyssey</em> does takes place at the end of the Age of Heroes, and one important piece of context for the poem is the tradition that Zeus intentionally caused the Trojan War in order to annihilate the race of demigods. (I discuss this idea in greater depth in <a href="https://talesoftimesforgotten.com/2022/11/14/zeuss-horrifying-plan-for-cosmic-genocide/">this post I made back in November 2022</a>.)</p>

<p><strong>The Trojan horse</strong></p>
<p>Now that I’ve given my more general thoughts on the film, I want to discuss its handling of a few specific episodes. I won’t cover <em>everything</em>, but I will cover parts that I thought were either especially well done or that I wish had been done differently.</p>
<p>Nolan’s <em>Odyssey</em> includes possibly the best treatment of the Trojan horse story that I have seen in any modern adaptation. In the original <em>Odyssey</em>, the story of the horse is told in Book 8, when the bard Demodokos tells the story in the hall of the Phaiakians. The story prompts Odysseus to cry, which reveals his identity.</p>
<p>In most adaptations, the Trojan horse is reduced to a kind of cliché, but Nolan makes it seem surprisingly fresh. In Nolan’s film, the horse first appears in the opening scene, which depicts the Trojans finding it: an exquisite sculpture of a horse rearing on its hind legs, half-sunken in the tide, guarded by a lone soldier, Sinon. The Trojans kill Sinon, who tells them, in his dying breath, that the horse is “a gift for Athena.”</p>
<p>This horse doesn’t have wheels, it doesn’t look like it was <em>meant</em> for the Trojans to take into their city, and it takes a whole army of men with ropes to move it, but it also makes sense why the Trojans would <em>want</em> to bring it into the city, precisely because it is such a beautiful work of art.</p>
<p>Later, when Telemakhos goes to see Menelaos in Sparta, he initially conceals his identity. He sits beside Menelaos, who tells him the incredibly gritty, visceral story of what it was like inside the horse. Menelaos describes how the men were in the horse for days, hot, starving, cramped, and filthy; how they had to piss and shit on top of each other and live in the stench; how some of the men drowned in the tide on the first day; how they had to keep absolutely silent inside; how, when the Trojans stuck swords into the horse and struck one of the men, they had to cover the man’s mouth to muffle his cry of pain; and how some men suffered seasickness as the horse was hauled on its side and set up inside the city. On hearing this description of what his father endured, Telemakhos cries, revealing his identity.</p>
<p>Nolan’s film cuts the island of the Phaiakians entirely, so I thought this was a really creative way of still incorporating the Trojan horse into the narrative, as well as the trope of the hero revealing his identity by crying when he hears the story. In the narrative, the story actually makes more sense and feels more real coming from Menelaos, who was actually there, rather than from the bard Demodokos, who knows the story only second-hand. The gritty realism of Menelaos’s account in Nolan’s version is also unmatched in any other telling of the story. All in all, I thought this part was handled extremely well.</p>

<p>One creative difference I do have with Nolan’s horse is that it looks like it was made by a skilled, professional, modern sculptor with a well-studied knowledge of horse anatomy. It’s difficult to imagine a bunch of Greek soldiers making a beautiful sculpture like this in secret on a beach under harsh time constraints with limited materials. It is also unlikely that Greek sculptor before the Hellenistic Period would have depicted a horse rearing on its hind legs, which is a very unnatural posture for a horse and one that would have made the statue very difficult to stand up.</p>
<p>We actually have a very clear idea of what Greeks around the time of the composition of the Homeric epics imagined the Trojan horse to look like because there is a very early surviving artistic depiction of it on a <em>pithos</em> (large storage jar) from the Greek island of Mykonos that is dated between c. 700 and c. 650 BCE, around the same time the<em> Iliad</em> and the <em>Odyssey</em> were most likely composed. The horse on the pithos has weird, unnatural proportions, including a tiny head with a super-huge eye, and a super-long, skinny tail, and it is standing on four long, skinny legs.</p>
<p>I would very much have liked to have seen a Trojan horse resembling this one; it’s clearly <em>meant to be </em>a work of art, but it also doesn’t look like the person who made it was an expert at realistically depicting horse anatomy.</p>

<p><strong>The rape of Kassandra and scattering of the Greek forces</strong></p>
<p>In the original <em>Odyssey</em>, the reason why the Greek forces are scattered after leaving Troy is because, during the sack of Troy, one of the Greeks, Aias the Lesser, raped the Trojan princess Kassandra, who had sought refuge inside the Temple of Athena. Kassandra had clung to Athena’s own statue, but Aias had pulled her away. Athena was furious over this, and, to punish all the Greek forces for Aias’s crime, she sent a storm that scattered their ships.</p>
<p>Nolan’s <em>Odyssey</em> portrays the Greeks’ desecration of Athena’s Trojan temple through several flashback sequences, but it omits Athena’s storm. Instead, it portrays Odysseus as just deciding to take a different route from the other Greeks based on an apparent whim, saying, “I’ve been following Agamemnon long enough.”</p>
<p>I thought that this was a real missed opportunity. One of the core themes of both the original epic and Nolan’s adaptation is that violating the gods’ law has consequences. Nolan’s film implies that Odysseus’s suffering is a result of the crimes he and the other Greeks committed during the destruction of Troy, but it could have shown this even more clearly by depicting Athena’s storm as the cause of the scattering of the fleet.</p>

<p><strong>The Kyklops episode</strong></p>
<p>I have mixed feelings about Nolan’s<em> Odyssey</em>’s depiction of the Kyklops Polyphemos. On the one hand, the handling of the overall <em>look</em> of this scene is absolutely impeccable. While most Hollywood directors today would probably film a scene like this using a green screen and CGI, Nolan filmed the whole scene on location in a real cave in Greece with real sheep and a real, giant, mechanical Kyklops puppet. As a result, everything about it looks startlingly, viscerally real.</p>
<p>Nolan’s Polyphemos also somewhat resembles, and may be based on, a late fifth or fourth-century BCE Boiotian terra-cotta figurine that is now held in the Museum of Fine Arts in Boston. The figurine depicts Polyphemos as a bald, beardless, naked man reclining on his left side and drinking from a bowl; he has a very large eye in the middle of his forehead, as well as a large, flat, broad nose and huge ears. This depiction of Polyphemos is rather atypical; most other ancient and modern images portray him with hair and a beard, a normal nose, and generally more normal human features (apart, of course, from his single eye).</p>

<p>Nolan’s version of Polyphemos shares the somewhat atypical features of the Boiotian figurine described above, and, at one point, he actually lies down on his left side in a manner similar to the figurine. Nolan’s version of the Kyklops, however, departs from the figurine and from other ancient depictions of Polyphemos by making his single eye not only located in the middle of his forehead but also rotated ninety degrees from the orientation of a normal human eye, so that it is taller than it is wide, with the two lids opening horizontally rather than vertically. He also slightly rotates Polyphemos’s nose, making its location on the face crooked and off-center. All of this gives Nolan’s Polyphemos a deeply creepy, uncanny appearance.</p>

<p>I really liked the <em>look</em> of Nolan’s Kyklops. Unfortunately, I was deeply disappointed by how much of the core of the Kyklops episode Nolan cut from the film. The Kyklops episode is one of my favorite parts of the original <em>Odyssey</em>, in part because it vividly demonstrates Odysseus’s character.</p>
<p>In the original <em>Odyssey</em>, Polyphemos talks to Odysseus and his men, and Odysseus tricks him by offering him a skin of fine Ismarian wine. Polyphemos, who has never tasted alcohol, drinks the wine and loves it. He asks Odysseus’s name, and Odysseus replies that his name is Οὔτις (<em>Oútis</em>), which means “No One.”</p>
<p>This pseudonym is doubly meaningful. First, it symbolically represents the fact the Odysseus, who is separated from his home and family, is alienated from his old identity. The second half of the epic, in which Odysseus gradually reunites with the members of his household and the symbols of his identity, represents him regaining the identity that he has lost.</p>
<p>Second, the name “No One” represents Odysseus’s signature trait, which is his cunning, because another word for “No One” in Ancient Greek is μήτις (<em>mḗtis</em>), which is homophonous with the word μῆτις (<em>mêtis</em>), meaning “cunning.” Thus, Odysseus has, in effect, told the Kyklops that his name is both “No One” and “Cunning.”</p>
<p>In return for the wine, Polyphemos gives Odysseus a “gift”: a promise that he will eat Odysseus last. Then, he immediately passes out drunk. This provides Odysseus and his men an opportunity. They can’t kill Polyphemos, because Polyphemos is the only one strong enough to move the stone blocking the entrance, so, instead, they take a sharpened beam of olive wood hardened in the flame and use it to gouge out Polyphemos’s only eye, blinding him.</p>
<p>Polyphemos cries out in agony. Some other Kyklopes hear his scream and come to ask him if anyone is hurting him, which leads Polyphemos to reply that “No One” is killing him. The other Kyklopes take this to mean that Polyphemos is alright and leave. Odysseus and his men then escape the cave by strapping themselves to the underbellies of Polyphemos’s sheep so that Polyphemos, who, being now blind, can only tell who or what is leaving the cave through touch, will feel only the sheep’s backs and not notice the soldiers escaping.</p>
<p>Odysseus and his men get out safely, but, as they are sailing away, Odysseus shouts back to taunt Polyphemos on the shore, leading Polyphemos to begin hurling great boulders at the fleeing ships. Odysseus, however, can’t restrain himself and taunts Polyphemos again, this time shouting back his real name, his father’s name, and his homeland of Ithaka. It’s the equivalent of shouting back his full name, address, and Social Security Number.</p>
<p>Polyphemos immediately uses this information to call upon his father Poseidon and ask him to make sure that Odysseus never reaches his home or, if the gods ordain that he must, that he only make it back after many years of wanderings and find his home in turmoil when he arrives. The reason why Polyphemos is able to call upon Poseidon to punish Odysseus is <em>because he knows Odysseus’s real name</em>.</p>

<p>Nolan’s <em>Odyssey</em> cuts all the dialogue between Odysseus and Polyphemos. In this version, Polyphemos never speaks to Odysseus or his men; he simply gobbles up a few men and then falls asleep in the cave on his own (without Odysseus giving him wine), allowing Odysseus and his men to gouge out his eye. Polyphemos <em>does</em> cry out in pain, but no other Kyklopes come. The first time we learn that Polyphemos can talk is when he prays to Poseidon while still in the cave immediately after being injured (without knowing Odysseus’s name), but his words sound like an alien language.</p>
<p>In this version, Odysseus and his men appear to escape from the cave by tying straw to their backs, which, if anything, seems less believable than them escaping underneath the bellies of the sheep. (Wouldn’t Polyphemos be able to tell the difference between the textures of straw and wool? It’s also strange that they would use straw instead of wool when they are literally in a cave full of sheep.) Once they are out of the cave, Odysseus shoots an arrow back at Polyphemos, which antagonizes him, causing him to chase after them and hurl a boulder at their ships as they flee.</p>
<p>Nolan explained his decision to omit Odysseus telling Polyphemos that his name is “No One” in <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DWABbDtyfeA">an interview on <em>The Daily Show</em> with Jon Stewart</a>, saying, “I tried. It was not possible to work it in.” Although Nolan does not elaborate on <em>why</em> this was, my guess is that he probably thought that including Odysseus’s humorous trickery of Polyphemos would detract from the dark, gritty tone he wanted for the film. The omission of Odysseus’s manipulation in this scene, however, really flattens Odysseus’s character and makes his escape from the cave seem to be more a result of luck than successful trickery.</p>
<p>Additionally, as Peter Gainsford explains <a href="https://kiwihellenist.blogspot.com/2023/12/puns.html">in a 2023 post on his blog</a>, puns in Greek literature serve a somewhat different purpose from puns in the present day. Today, people often see puns as the lowest form of humor, but, in early Greek literature, puns are more like word magic. While modern people usually perceive the sounds of words and the verbal similarity of words to other words as coincidental and insignificant, the ancient Greeks saw these things as profoundly meaningful. Odysseus’s use of the μήτις/μῆτις pun isn’t just a joke; it shows his mastery of the arcane art of word manipulation.</p>
<p><strong>Kirke’s island</strong></p>
<p>In contrast to my mixed feelings about Nolan’s adaptation of the Kyklops episode from Book 9, I thought that his adaptation of the Kirke episode from Book 10 was <em>really</em> excellent. It’s very different from the original version, but it works really well.</p>
<p>In the original version of this episode in the <em>Odyssey</em>, Book 10, Odysseus sends his second-in-command Eurylokhos ashore with a group of men to scout. They come to a luxurious palace surrounded by lions and wolves, which are lying down tamely. They hear a beautiful voice singing inside, and Kirke, who appears as a young, beautiful, sexually alluring goddess, welcomes them in. She has a host of beautiful nymphs who act as her maidservants.</p>
<p>All the men except for Eurylokhos himself (who suspects a trap and stays back) go inside. Kirke lays out a delicious meal for them, but she puts a magic drug in the food, so that, as soon as the men eat it, they transform into pigs. Eurylokhos, who sees all this, goes back and tells Odysseus.</p>
<p>When Odysseus goes off on his own, the god Hermes appears to him and gives him a magic plant called <em>moly</em>, which will make him immune to Kirke’s magic. Odysseus goes to Kirke’s hall and eats the food she serves him, but, thanks to the <em>moly</em>, the spell doesn’t work on him. Odysseus holds his sword to her throat, and Kirke tries to seduce him. He makes her promise that she will restore his men. Then, after she swears, he agrees to have sex with her.</p>
<p>Odysseus and his men spend the next year on Kirke’s island, and she and her nymphs provide them with food and comfort. Eventually, Odysseus wants to leave, and Kirke tells him that, to learn his way home, he will have to journey to the far west to consult the spirit of the dead seer Teiresias.</p>
<p>The original <em>Odyssey</em> portrays Kirke as a stereotypical, seductive <em>femme fatale</em>; it never really fleshes out her character, nor does it ever explain <em>why</em> she lures men in and turns them into pigs.</p>

<p>Nolan’s version of this episode plays out very differently and clearly draws just as much inspiration from Madeline Miller’s 2018 novel <em>Circe</em> as it does from the original <em>Odyssey</em>. In Nolan’s version, Odysseus’s men mutiny and refuse to take orders from him due to his failure to save most of the men from the Laistrygonians. Odysseus takes a bow and goes off on his own to hunt, while the other men under Eurylokhos journey inland. Eurylokhos and his men see various wild animals lying down tamely in shelters along the path, and they start to become confused and frightened.</p>
<p>Eurylokhos finds a small hovel on the top of a hill. He goes inside uninvited and finds a caged raven. Circe, who is portrayed brilliantly by Samantha Morton as a middle-aged woman with unkempt hair, screams in terror at the sight of a heavily armed, armored man inside her hovel. Eurylokhos tries to reassure her that he and his men are just looking for food, and Circe agrees to prepare a dinner for them.</p>
<p>The men begin to eat, but then they seem unable to<em> stop </em>eating, and, suddenly, they start snorting. The camera zooms in close for a grueling, visceral, body horror scene lasting what feels like several minutes, in which Circe physically molds the men into pigs, stretching their faces and bodies into inhuman shapes as white hair begins to sprout. It is incredibly well done, and critics have already labeled it the most horrific scene Nolan has made in his long career as a director.</p>
<p>Meanwhile, Odysseus is out hunting on his own. He chases and kills a deer with an arrow, but, when he approaches the carcass, he finds a naked man on the ground. Instantly, he realizes that his men are in danger. He goes straight to Circe’s hovel, where the pigs who were once his men surround him. Circe welcomes him in and questions him. She tells him to leave his weapons outside, and he does. Inside, she notices that he has kept a dagger and makes him give it up. Eventually, she offers him the same meal she just gave to his men.</p>
<p>While the pigs pound furiously on the door to warn their captain, Odysseus suspects there is something wrong with the food and doesn’t eat it. We can see him fitting all the pieces together in real time. Noticing Circe’s strange affection for the caged raven, he seizes the bird and threatens to kill it if Circe doesn’t transform his men back. Circe reveals that the raven is her sister, whom she turned into a bird because “we get on better this way.”</p>
<p>She restores Odysseus’s men, then Odysseus and his men take food (leaving behind the meat Circe has enchanted) and promptly leave. Before they go, Circe, who seems to really just want the men gone, tells Odysseus that, to find the way home, he will have to consult the spirit of Teiresias and gives him directions for where and how to summon him.</p>

<p>I like several things about this scene. First, Nolan (taking clear inspiration from Madeline Miller) makes Circe a more nuanced character than she is in the original epic. This Circe is older-looking, not overtly sexual, and more vulnerable; she lives alone (rather than with a host of nymphs) in a hovel (rather than a luxurious palace). The film implies that male visitors to her island have hurt her before, and she turns Odysseus’s men into pigs out of fear for her own safety.</p>
<p>Another thing I like is that Nolan’s version of this scene emphasizes Odysseus’s intelligence and resourcefulness more than the original <em>Odyssey</em>’s version does. In the original <em>Odyssey</em>, Odysseus already knows not to trust Kirke because Eurylokhos warns him, and he is able to resist Kirke’s magic because Hermes helps him. By contrast, in Nolan’s version, Odysseus figures out what is going on all on his own, without any direct assistance from either Eurylokhos or Hermes. Even after Circe disarms him, he figures out a way to coerce her into restoring his men.</p>
<p><strong>The <em>nekyia</em></strong></p>
<p>In the original <em>Odyssey</em>, in Book 11, Odysseus travels to a desolate land in the far west to consult the spirit of Teiresias. There, he performs a necromantic ritual that Kirke has taught to him to raise the spirits of the dead. This episode is known in Greek as the <em>nekyia</em> or raising of the dead, and it is another of my favorite scenes in the <em>Odyssey</em>.</p>
<p>To perform the ritual, Odysseus digs a pit. Then, he sacrifices two sheep (a ram and an ewe) by slitting their throats and pours their blood into the pit. As he does this, the ghosts of the dead rise out of the ground all around him. Each spirit looks exactly how it did at the moment it died; he sees the ghosts of old people ridden with pain, little children who died and will never grow up, and soldiers who died in battle still bearing grievous wounds and wearing their bloodstained armor, with spears sticking out of them (<em>Od</em>. 11.34–43):</p>
<blockquote> <p>τοὺς δʼ ἐπεὶ εὐχωλῇσι λιτῇσί τε, ἔθνεα νεκρῶν,<br/>ἐλλισάμην, τὰ δὲ μῆλα λαβὼν ἀπεδειροτόμησα<br/>ἐς βόθρον, ῥέε δʼ αἷμα κελαινεφές· αἱ δʼ ἀγέροντο<br/>ψυχαὶ ὑπὲξ Ἐρέβευς νεκύων κατατεθνηώτων.<br/>νύμφαι τʼ ἠίθεοί τε πολύτλητοί τε γέροντες<br/>παρθενικαί τʼ ἀταλαὶ νεοπενθέα θυμὸν ἔχουσαι,<br/>πολλοὶ δʼ οὐτάμενοι χαλκήρεσιν ἐγχείῃσιν,<br/>ἄνδρες ἀρηίφατοι βεβροτωμένα τεύχεʼ ἔχοντες·<br/>οἳ πολλοὶ περὶ βόθρον ἐφοίτων ἄλλοθεν ἄλλος<br/>θεσπεσίῃ ἰαχῇ· ἐμὲ δὲ χλωρὸν δέος ᾕρει.</p> </blockquote>
<blockquote> <p>And, when, with prayers and entreaties, I had prayed<br/>to the nations of the dead, I slit the two sheep’s throats<br/>into the pit, and the dark blood flowed,<br/>and the spirits of the dead below gathered out of Erebos:<br/>brides and unmarried youths, the pain-ridden and old,<br/>and delicate little girls who had hearts new to suffering,<br/>and many wounded with bronze spears,<br/>Ares-slain men holding bloodstained arms and armor.<br/>The masses flitted around the pit from all around<br/>with a god-sounding scream, and terror seized me pale.</p> </blockquote>
<p>Worst of all, the dead are just shadows of their former selves; none of them can speak or remember their earthly identities until they have drunk the blood of the sacrifice.</p>
<p>The first spirit who appears to Odysseus is Elpenor, one of his comrades who, unbeknownst to him, got drunk, fell off a roof, died, and now lies unburied on Kirke’s island. Odysseus promises to return to bury his body. Next, Odysseus sees his own mother Antikleia, who he also did not know was dead. Then Teiresias himself comes forward; he is the only spirit who is able to remember his identity and speak without drinking the blood. He gives Odysseus directions on how to get home and also prophesies to him how he will die.</p>
<p>Once Teiresias has delivered his message, Odysseus speaks to his mother. He learns that she died of grief for him during his long years away from Ithaka. She describes to him the conditions back home, giving him information that will prove useful when he arrives back. Odysseus tries to embrace her, but she is incorporeal, and his arms simply pass through her.</p>
<p>Odysseus also speaks to the spirits of his fallen comrades from the Trojan War. Akhilleus tells him how miserable he is in the underworld and says that he would rather be a hireling to a poor man with no land in the world above than the prince among the dead that he is. Agamemnon tells Odysseus how, after returning home, his own wife Klytaimnestra murdered him; he warns Odysseus not to trust anyone. We get a strong sense that no one in the afterlife is truly happy. Eventually, so many souls surround Odysseus and his men that he fears Persephone might send up the head of the Gorgon, so he and his men flee.</p>
<p>What makes this scene so unsettling for me isn’t just the incredibly chilling imagery, but also the way it harnesses our fear of the inevitability of death and the unknowability of what comes after. We will all die, and no one can escape it. We want to imagine that there is an afterlife, that it is a happy place, and that we will remember who we were, but the <em>nekyia </em>challenges these assumptions.</p>

<p>Many adaptations of the <em>Odyssey</em> change the <em>nekyia</em> by having Odysseus actually go to the underworld itself instead of summoning up spirits through this necromantic blood sacrifice, but Nolan’s depiction of the scene stays remarkably true to the original. He has Odysseus perform the sacrifice and the dead rise out of the ground, just as the <em>Odyssey</em> describes and ancient vase paintings depict. Nolan has to condense the scene due to time constraints, so he only has Odysseus speak to three spirits: Teiresias, Sinon, and Agamemnon.</p>
<p>Teiresias tells Odysseus the journey home and warns him that he must not let his crewmembers eat the cattle of the sun. Sinon seems to stand in the place of Elpenor and Akhilleus; he reproaches Odysseus for lying to him about the Greeks’ plan and for letting him die and gives Odysseus a rod that was used for the drawing of lots to select which young men would go to Troy, revealing that Antinoös paid his family to send him to Troy in Antinoös’s place. He tells Odysseus to avenge him.</p>
<p>Much like in the original version of the scene, Agamemnon tells Odysseus how his wife murdered him upon his arrival home and warns Odysseus not to trust anyone when he arrives back until he has ascertained their true loyalties. The scene finishes with the souls of Odysseus’s dead crewmembers demanding that he join them and chasing him and his living crew back to their ship.</p>
<p>The scene is incredibly creepy and very effective. My only disappointment that Nolan did not include Odysseus’s meeting with his mother, but I realize that the film is already nearly three hours long and that including a meeting with Antikleia would have just made it even longer.</p>

<p><strong>The island of the Sirens</strong></p>
<p>Another iconic scene that appears in Nolan’s <em>Odyssey</em> is Odysseus’s encounter with the Sirens. I have somewhat mixed feelings about this scene, but, on the whole, I thought it was pretty good.</p>
<p>One thing most people don’t know is that the Sirens in Greek myth were never mermaids. Instead, as I discuss in greater depth in <a href="https://talesoftimesforgotten.com/2019/05/01/what-is-the-difference-between-a-siren-and-a-mermaid/">this post I wrote back in 2019</a>, the ancient Greeks imagined and depicted Sirens as having the bodies of <em>birds</em> and heads of women. Western artists did not begin to depict Sirens as having the lower bodies of fish until the Early Modern Period.</p>

<p>Nolan’s <em>Odyssey</em> follows the modern tradition of portraying the Sirens as mermaids, which somewhat disappointed me, because I would have liked to have seen them as bird women, but I suppose I can’t expect too much.</p>
<p>The scene in which the ship passes the island of the Sirens is otherwise extremely well done. As in the original poem, Nolan’s Odysseus has his men plug their ears with beeswax to block out the Sirens’ song, while he has them tie him to the mast of the ship so that he can listen safely. We see Odysseus in apparent agony, struggling to free himself, but the sound is muted, as though we are Odysseus’s crew with our ears blocked with wax. We catch only brief glimpses of the Sirens themselves, and we never hear their actual song, but we hear Odysseus describe it and how it made him feel.</p>
<p><strong>The scene where Odysseus rescues Telemakhos from the suitors’ assassins</strong></p>
<p>One scene Nolan’s <em>Odyssey</em> adds that is not in the original epic is when Telemakhos goes to Pylos to offer prayers at the Temple of Athena. Assassins sent by the suitors impersonate the priests and try to murder Telemakhos and his companions by giving them poison, but Odysseus rescues Telemakhos by showing up disguised as a beggar at just the right moment to stop him from drinking. Mentor, however, drinks the poison and dies. With their cover as priests blown, the suitors’ assassins try to kill Telemakhos with their swords, but Odysseus and Telemakhos kill the assassins inside the temple and hastily set a fire to burn the bodies.</p>
<p>I did not like this scene at all. In the original <em>Odyssey</em>, the suitors do try to murder Telemakhos, but this happens out of the audience’s view, and Telemakhos manages to evade the assassins without Odysseus’s help. Nolan’s interpretation adds more action, but at the expense of virtually everything else in the story.</p>
<p>First, from a practical standpoint, it shouldn’t have been possible for Odysseus, who finds out about the suitors’ plot against Telemakhos from Eumaios on Ithaka after the assassins have already left to set their trap, to sail all the way from Ithaka to Pylos to warn Telemakhos in time. The <em>Odyssey</em> itself portrays the distance from Ithaka to Pylos as a day’s sailing. Odysseus couldn’t just hop over.</p>
<p>Second, in the world of the epics, hurting the priest of a god or committing violence inside a god’s temple are some of the worst religious crimes a person can commit. Nolan has the suitors’ assassins apparently murder the priests of Athena so they can impersonate them and attempt to murder Telemakhos inside the temple. Then, he has Odysseus and Telemakhos actually kill the assassins inside the temple <em>and then set a fire</em>!</p>
<p>Both the suitors and Odysseus act like this is no big deal—but let me remind you that, in the original <em>Odyssey</em>, the whole reason why Athena scattered the Greek forces leaving Troy was because Aias the Lesser violated her temple. If the characters are going to violate the most sacred of all divine laws like this, we should at least expect some setup or explanation to show they know what they’re doing here goes beyond mere casual violence.</p>
<p><strong>The ending</strong></p>
<p>Possibly the most significant change that Nolan’s <em>Odyssey </em>makes is in the way it ends, which is totally different from the original epic. This is not very surprising, since there is a near-universal consensus stretching back to antiquity that the ending of the original <em>Odyssey</em> is deeply unsatisfying.</p>
<p>The way the original epic ends is with the relatives of the suitors coming to Ithaka to seek vengeance for their murdered kin. Odysseus, Telemakhos, and Laërtes arm themselves, the first blows are exchanged, it looks as though there’s about to be another war. Then, suddenly, Athena comes down the sky and tells all the men to stop fighting and make peace. The men comply and agree to accept Odysseus as the rightful king of Ithaka for the rest of his days.</p>
<p>Most people consider this ending unsatisfying because it raises the problem of the suitors’ kinsmen seeking vengeance, but its solution to that problem doesn’t bring any material changes; Odysseus remains the king of Ithaka, and the suitors’ kinsmen simply set aside their desire to see the man who killed their relatives face justice. If this whole section of the epic were omitted, the result would still be the same. As a result, many scholars have suspected this ending is a later addition.</p>
<p>Many adaptations, including Pasolini’s <em>The Return</em>, simply end with Odysseus and Penelope’s reunion and omit the parts that come after that, ignoring the problem of what will happen when the suitors’ relatives find out what Odysseus has done. Nolan’s <em>Odyssey</em> takes a slightly different tactic; it deals with this problem by having Odysseus go into a voluntary exile for his massacre of the suitors in violation of the law of <em>xenía</em>, taking Penelope with him and leaving Telemakhos as ruler of Ithaka.</p>
<p>This ending still feels rather unsatisfactory. It seems entirely out of character for Odysseus, the man who has spent literally the entire movie trying to get home, to decide voluntarily almost immediately after arriving home that he will go into exile and never return. One also imagines that, after all the trauma and loss Odysseus has endured at sea over the course of the film, going out on another long voyage would be the very last thing on his mind. Despite all this, Odysseus going into exile seems like a better solution to the problem of his blood guilt than the original poem’s abortive war with the suitors’ relatives.</p>
<p>I think Nolan’s ending would have been better if the film had preserved some element of the conflict with the suitors’ relatives and portrayed Odysseus’s decision to go into exile as part of a negotiated solution to that conflict. The suitors’ relatives could show up looking for revenge just like they do in the original <em>Odyssey</em>, but then Athena could broker a peace agreement between the two parties, in which the suitors’ relatives agree to make peace and accept Telemakhos as their king, and, in exchange, Odysseus agrees to go into exile and never return to Ithaka. To ensure that both parties’ keep their terms, Athena could act as an enforcer of the treaty.</p>
<p><strong>Conclusion</strong></p>
<p>Overall, Nolan’s <em>Odyssey</em> was much better than I expected. There were many parts of it that I liked a lot, while some parts I didn’t like so much. It’s definitely worth watching. I would give it a rating of three and a half out of five stars, which is the highest rating I’d give to any big-budget Hollywood film set in ancient Greece that has been released so far in the twenty-first century. I liked it slightly better than <em>The Return</em> (2024), much better than <em>Troy</em> (2004) or <em>Alexander</em> (2004), and certainly much better than execrable trash like <em>300</em> (2006), <em>Clash of the Titans</em> (2010), <em>Wrath of the Titans</em> (2012), <em>300: Rise of an Empire</em> (2014), and <em>The Legend of Hercules</em> (2014).</p>
<p>I hope that Nolan’s <em>Odyssey</em> elevates the standard for better, more thoughtful big-budget film adaptations of ancient Greek stories in the future.</p>
