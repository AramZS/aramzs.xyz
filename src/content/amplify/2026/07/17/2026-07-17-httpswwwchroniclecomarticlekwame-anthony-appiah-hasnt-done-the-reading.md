<h1>Kwame Anthony Appiah Hasn’t Done the Reading</h1>
<p>The philosopher’s report on the state of literary studies is a shambles.</p>

<p>Scholars should welcome interdisciplinary contestation and dispute over fundamental questions of truth and method, and so this spring’s arrival of a “ <a href="https://cdn.vanderbilt.edu/vu-wpfsx/wp-content/uploads/sites/51/2026/06/State-of-Scholarship-CommissionReport-final.pdf">Report on the State of Scholarship in the Humanities and the Humanistic Social Sciences</a>” was energizing. Demanding an account of the way our fields construct knowledge is entirely appropriate. And while we can disagree with the premises and conclusions of the report, and I do, the fact that it is mainly written without technical jargon is a great service to the ongoing scholarly conversation. The fact that so many have contested the claims and evidence of the report, which was sponsored by Vanderbilt University and Washington University in St. Louis and chaired by Paul Boghossian, is one testament to its clarity.</p>
<p>That said, clarity is not synonymous with accuracy. John K. Wilson <a href="https://www.insidehighered.com/opinion/columns/debatable-ideas/2026/06/09/academic-authoritarianism-new-attack-humanities-scholars">notes</a> the lack of direct evidence warranting the field-based scrutiny recommended in the report. Agustín Fuentes <a href="https://www.chronicle.com/article/are-leftist-professors-really-destroying-the-academys-credibility">argues</a> that the report’s depiction of his field, anthropology, is misleading. Nicholas Dirks and Brian Leiter <a href="https://www.chronicle.com/article/what-a-new-report-about-the-humanities-gets-right-and-wrong">separately</a> <a href="https://www.chronicle.com/blogs/letters/how-strong-is-the-intellectual-case-against-relativism">note</a> the report’s imprecise definition of “relativism,” despite their sympathies with its conclusions, while both Leiter and Richard Moran <a href="https://www.bostonreview.net/articles/the-wrong-way-to-criticize-the-humanities/">critique</a> the irregularities in the constitution of its committee. Jason Stanley <a href="https://jasonstanleyantifascist.substack.com/p/on-the-philosophical-muddle-that">questions</a> the committee’s breadth of understanding in analytic philosophy, the tiny sliver of the humanities that is the home discipline of nearly half of the committee. To their credit, the writers of the report <a href="https://www.chronicle.com/article/inside-the-report-thats-divided-the-academy">have responded</a> to these and other charges with surprising candor.</p>
<p>Now we have the release of <a href="https://higheredstatementofprinciples.com/state-of-scholarship-report/literary-studies/kwame-anthony-appiah/">the first</a> of the committee’s promised field reports, on my own discipline of literary studies. It’s important to note that “literary studies” is not one field but many, loosely held together by certain shared methods, practices, objects, and concerns. Knowing one of these fields does not give us sufficient grounds to critique “literary studies” as a whole. Strangely, this report is written not by the committee’s single literary studies scholar, the Slavist Gary Saul Morson, but by one of the philosophers, Kwame Anthony Appiah.</p>
<p>Appiah’s report is not a promising beginning for these field-specific internal reports. It does not engage the scholarly literature on politics, method, and truth within even Anglophone literary criticism in any serious way. And, in a more dangerous move that the larger report also echoes, it appears to rely on shoddy mixed-method data analyses published as appendices to the report.</p>
<p>Although Appiah is a former president of the Modern Language Association (MLA), he did not train as a literary scholar, and predictably he misreads internal debates over method, evidence, and political epistemology in the fields he addresses. And, since he is not a qualified digital humanist, nor a practitioner of any quantitative, computational, or statistical field, much less an expert on large language model (LLM) prompting and analysis, his report makes a mess of the data analysis it provides as support.</p>
<p>No one could reasonably expect someone from outside the field to adequately review the last 20 to 30 years of debates over truth and method in literary studies in the brief period of the Vanderbilt commission’s efforts, and indeed Appiah has not. His report misses the method wars that roiled literary studies a decade ago and the contestation of the canon as cultural capital 20 years before that; it appears to be unaware of current attempts to reclaim close reading, literary truth, aesthetic autonomy, and humanistic reason. Appiah therefore lacks the grounds for argument or judgment in the fields he addresses: He just hasn’t done the reading.</p>

<p>Appiah is a very distinguished philosopher, ethicist, and theorist of cosmopolitanism. He has also written an intellectual memoir, several detective novels, and given many lectures on W. E. B. Du Bois and other figures in the Black literary tradition. These are not, however, adequate substitutes for reading the relevant secondary literature on, say, life writing, crime writing, or African American aesthetic theory. Despite, or because of, his demonstrable lack of expertise in 21st-century literary studies, Appiah wants to recall literary studies to its supposed roots in close reading and the autonomy of the aesthetic. This is an odd position for a writer interested in Du Bois, who famously wrote and lived by the dictum “all Art is propaganda and ever must be.” Despite his dozens of books, articles, and hundreds of talks on the intersections of culture, race, and identity, Appiah wants to argue that we should talk less about race, identity, and culture; or at least talk <em>differently</em>, more like Appiah, less like Du Bois.</p>

<p>Appiah’s grasp of literary theory is also insufficient to support the theoretical critique advanced in the report. Though he has clearly followed some of the debates around the “sociological turn” in literary studies, he gives incomplete accounts of the major theorists involved. Pierre Bourdieu’s point was not that literary and aesthetic tastes just correspond with elite status, but that the language of aesthetic autonomy favored by Appiah, for example, actively reinforces elite cultural hierarchies. Bourdieu elaborated on his description of “cultural capital” through an enormous body of other methodological works important to sociology, literary studies, and cultural studies, in accounts influential across many linguistic and disciplinary traditions, including the work by John Guillory that Appiah cites extensively. The fact that Appiah disagrees with Bourdieu’s account of the aesthetic, or particular uses of Bourdieu’s concepts, as many of us do, is not a sufficient ground for wholesale dismissal of intellectual work drawing on this paradigm. Informed, literate debate should be our goal: What Appiah misses, here and elsewhere, is the internal contestation of what he wrongly takes to be characteristic of entire fields.</p>
<p>The same pattern holds in his critique of Caroline Levine’s <em>The Activist Humanist: Form and Method in the Climate Crisis</em>(2023) <em>.</em> Appiah begins by citing Levine’s statement that “anti-instrumentality” underlies much work in the humanities, an opening move in her argument for an “affirmative instrumentality” and a defense of the position of the “activist humanist.” Levine is entirely correct that much work in the humanities is reflexively anti-instrumentalist: The position that Appiah wants to defend is, therefore, simply the status quo in the fields he claims to judge.</p>
<p>Here, as with the broader report’s handling of epistemological debates in anthropology, the authors miss what Cambridge School intellectual historians call the perlocutionary intent and illocutionary force of the arguments they examine within their original context. Levine’s claims unsettle certain widely assumed anti-instrumentalist premises about the humanities. While Appiah is free to disagree with her argument for an “affirmative instrumentality” for the humanities, it is misleading to take her claims as metonymic of the debates and fields in which she intervenes. Indeed, reactions from within the field to Levine’s positions have been heated, to say the least.</p>
<p>Then we get to the report’s more dangerous failures in method: the use of LLM classification, topic modeling, and data analysis. Appiah claims no expertise at all in digital analysis or LLM classifiers, which is a problem for relying on data analysis built on evidence from GPT-5.2, the proprietary OpenAI API model used for the “analysis” in Appendix 1. GPT-5.2 is the very definition of a black-box model, with weights and training data that cannot be checked or provided for review. The description of the method used in Appendix 1 is clear but disqualifying: It makes no reference to the literature on the use of literary and genre classifiers in the humanities and therefore cannot support field-level inferences. The classifications given are not validated through human reading and testing as the note on “Methodological Limitations” admits, but this is a fundamental step in the validation of any machine-learning approach to classification.</p>

<p>As Appendix 1 states, comparison between three models allows us to “measure inter-model reliability (whether the prompted models label entries consistently), not construct validity.” Alas, running the same prompts on other black-box models like Gemini is not an adequate form of methodological validation. Appiah may respond that the data analysis in the appendices was not his work (Yu Guo and Gavin G. Cook are credited in the report). But if Appiah cannot validate either the method or result of a computational argument, it is irresponsible to rely on it as support for his conclusions.</p>
<p>What we are shown about the “classification” of the American Comparative Literature Association and MLA prize data in Appendix 1 is almost a parody of motivated reasoning in data analysis. If you ask a chatbot to determine whether “the primary scholarly orientation” of a book is “identity-group” and/or “social-justice-oriented” rather than “primarily literary-formal, aesthetic, philological, or period-defined,” based solely on the book’s title and publisher description, the chatbot will reproduce the implied premise you already hold: Identity and political orientation must be separated from formal and aesthetic concerns that are “really” literary. But since formal, aesthetic, historical, and also racial concerns are inseparable in the history of African American art and aesthetics, for example, what the classifier will give you back as “data” when run over recent work in African American literary studies is just the bad binary distinction built into the prompt.</p>
<blockquote>
<p>At a time when rates of basic literacy are beginning to fall, and with innumeracy also on the rise, we need to do better on the fundamentals.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Running a similar classifier task on Appiah’s own academic <a href="https://appiah.net/biography/curriculumvitae/">CV</a> helps show the absurdity of this procedure. When I asked an LLM to classify the published titles and book descriptions in Appiah’s CV as <em>either</em> “primarily identity-group” and “social-justice oriented” <em>or</em>“primarily philosophical, epistemological, ethical, or concerned with philosophical analysis,” I found that Appiah would seem to be part of the trend he wants to critique. The same classifier prompts, run on the current high-effort GPT-5.6 model, yield a “YES” for “primarily identity-group or social-justice oriented” on 30 percent of Appiah’s single- or co-authored books, and 70 percent of his edited or co-edited books, with a peak of over 80 percent of all books he’d written and edited from 1995-99. It’s only fair to note that Appiah’s books written after 2015 are more distanced from concerns of identity or social justice, and that the sample size here is too small to be meaningful (not that the methodology would render it such in any case). Two books listed as forthcoming on the CV on his website are given the following provisional classifications by the model: <em>Reading Fanon</em> (YES), and an annotated edition of John Stuart Mill’s <em>On Liberty</em> (NO). This is of course because reading Frantz Fanon is “primarily identity-group oriented,” according to the prompts provided, while work on Mill is purely philosophical.</p>
<p>Let us pass silently over the series of slides presented without validation or replicability that is offered as evidence for the supposed rise and fall of Chinese literature and Shakespeare in “Appendix 2.”</p>

<p>“Appendix 3: The Decline of ‘Theory’” is a more sustained attempt at analysis and conclusion, but it depends on a misguided premise. The analysis attempts to track the “decline of ‘theory’” through word frequency tracked across program texts, then the MLA Job Information List, using the frequency of a single word, “theory.” But the absence of a single word is not proof of anything, much less the disappearance of “theory” (which the Vanderbilt commission elsewhere sees still much at work in the world). The absence of a keyword can point to the unspoken keystone of an entire field: Sessions of the American Psychiatric Association, for example, do not contain the word “Freud” in every paper description, nor do sessions of the American Historical Association contain “the history of” in every session title. In this case as well, what the data is tracking is not the “decline of theory” but its normalization: Every literary scholar is expected to theorize and historicize their work, and yet we don’t need to write “literary theory” in every talk and job listing.</p>
<p>The methodological failings throughout these appendices demonstrate the same essential problem that the report evinces throughout: Appiah hasn’t done the reading, can’t discern good from bad data analysis, and doesn’t know what he doesn’t know about literary studies and internal field debates over both epistemology and method. The result is a form of naïve <em>methodological disembedding,</em> an old problem: the credulous use of social-science or scientistic methods for the concerns and objects of the humanities, without awareness of the internal or field-specific conversation around the use of such tools. As the digital historian Ben Schmidt <a href="https://benschmidt.org/sappingattention/when-you-have-mallet-everything-looks/">wrote</a> of an earlier generation of topic modeling in the humanities, “When you have a MALLET [a topic-modeling software tool], everything looks like a nail.”</p>
<p>This would be trivial if the potential actions recommended by the larger report — administrative receivership for epistemologically suspect disciplines — were not so serious, and the failures in basic academic protocols were not so glaring. The actual epistemological crisis we are entering now is one better exemplified by the Vanderbilt report’s AI-generated analyses: automated data collection, sloppy analysis, and a growing sprawl of LLM-generated pseudo-evidence. Meanwhile, the AI ensloppification of the entire academic publishing enterprise continues apace, mostly <a href="http://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2605754123">outside</a> the humanities, as the authors of the report seem to have failed to notice. The use of such tools adds to long-running concerns about reproducibility across the <a href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-10078-y">social sciences</a> as well as the <a href="https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-023-03817-6">biomedical and physical sciences</a>. LLMs can be powerful tools for textual analysis when they are paired with deep field knowledge and methodological sophistication. When they are used to provide specious data analysis for a series of exercises in motivated reasoning, as here, they are worse than useless.</p>
<p>At a time when rates of basic literacy are beginning to fall, and with innumeracy also on the rise, we need to do better on the fundamentals. As Jonathan Kramnick notes in <a href="https://www.chronicle.com/article/who-gets-to-judge-the-humanities">his response</a> to the original report, expertise in one domain of the humanities “does not transfer automatically to expertise in another.” Disciplinary training and expertise is not everything, but it is a necessary condition for reviewing serious scholarly work. A philosopher of ethics does not therefore have the expertise needed to evaluate a black-box LLM analysis.</p>
<p>Dear critics of literary studies: do the required reading.</p>
