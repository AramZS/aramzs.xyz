<p>Two months after McClatchy Media laid off more than 90 journalists nationwide, executives at the storied local newspaper chain offered a blunt assessment of its future.</p>
<p>The award-winning local journalism that has long powered the 169-year-old company was no longer a viable business model, executives told McClatchy employees during a virtual town hall this month, and its survival depended on a wholesale reinvention — centered entirely around artificial intelligence.</p>
<p>From the rollout of new lifestyle websites that feature AI-generated articles and sponsored content to legal review and finance, executives revealed how AI is now integrated into virtually every part of the company. The demonstration comes after McClatchy, one of the nation’s largest newspaper conglomerates, carried out <a href="https://archive.ph/uY5Fs#selection-1493.82-1493.122">deep staff cuts</a> in September at the Miami Herald, The Kansas City Star, and more than a dozen other local news outlets. An anonymous “person close to the executive decision-making” told The New York Times the layoffs <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/10/business/media/mcclatchy-miami-herald-layoffs.html">were not about AI</a> and were instead about “business performance in some areas.”</p>
<p>“We cannot continue to do the same things because the marketplace has changed,” McClatchy CEO <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/tonywhunter/">Tony Hunter</a> told staff during the off-the-record Nov. 6 meeting, according to audio provided to Nieman Lab. Executives told staff that McClatchy-owned publications have suffered a 17% decline in paid digital subscribers in the last year — and unique visitors have plummeted 39%.</p>
<figure><img alt="" sizes="(max-width: 1160px) 100vw, 1100px" src="https://www.niemanlab.org/images/mcclatchy-town-hall-audience-slide-1.jpg" srcset="https://www.niemanlab.org/images/mcclatchy-town-hall-audience-slide-1.jpg 1668w, https://www.niemanlab.org/images/mcclatchy-town-hall-audience-slide-1-700x389.jpg 700w, https://www.niemanlab.org/images/mcclatchy-town-hall-audience-slide-1-990x550.jpg 990w, https://www.niemanlab.org/images/mcclatchy-town-hall-audience-slide-1-768x426.jpg 768w, https://www.niemanlab.org/images/mcclatchy-town-hall-audience-slide-1-1536x853.jpg 1536w, https://www.niemanlab.org/images/mcclatchy-town-hall-audience-slide-1-480x266.jpg 480w, https://www.niemanlab.org/images/mcclatchy-town-hall-audience-slide-1-600x333.jpg 600w"/><figcaption>Photo of a slide with audience figures shown during the town hall meeting</figcaption></figure>
<p>A companywide blueprint presented during the meeting highlights how McClatchy is systematically outsourcing the infrastructure for local news to AI agents — and rebranding around lifestyle verticals for sports fans, outdoor enthusiasts, and true-crime junkies.</p>
<p>Dubbed “Project Catalyst,” the companywide restructure will create an “AI-native operating model” that takes “repeated tasks off of humans” and embeds AI agents “directly into the daily workflow,” <a href="https://www.mcclatchy.com/about/news/jeffrey-dorsey-appointed-chief-operating-officer-of-mcclatchy">Jeff Dorsey</a>, McClatchy’s chief operating officer, said at the town hall. Dorsey described the initiative as an effort to automate “non-core as well as core functions,” “get rid of things that just don’t offer the same value” they once did, and “provide cash, frankly, to keep the business afloat.”</p>
<p>McClatchy, which operates 29 daily newspapers across 14 states, didn’t respond to multiple requests for comment.</p>
<p>Central to the rebrand is the launch of four new websites: <a href="https://www.crimehunter.com/about-us/">Crime Hunter</a>, a site devoted to “real-life crime and justice”; <a href="https://www.saltwatersouth.com/about-us/">Saltwater South</a>, which “celebrates the places, people and experiences that make the coastal South unlike anywhere else”; <a href="https://www.campcountry.com">Camp &amp; Country</a>, a “national outdoor destination”; and <a href="https://www.studentsection.com">Student Section</a>, billed as “the home of the college sports fan.”</p>
<p>Each of the sites is “powered by Trend Hunter,” a company McClatchy <a href="https://archive.ph/T9a74#selection-1351.0-1351.125">acquired in 2024</a> that uses AI “to glean insights into consumer trends” and <a href="https://x.com/KCStar/status/2100364183778783646">sell consumer goods</a> like $33.91 <a href="https://shop.trendhunter.com/collections/rosemary-hair-oil">rosemary oil</a> “to support a healthy scalp environment,” a $249 <a href="https://archive.ph/o/C1qOV/https://shop.trendhunter.com/collections/hair-caps">red light therapy cap</a> to regrow hair “without the hassle of messy serums” and $34.99 <a href="https://shop.trendhunter.com/products/magic-mushroom-coffee-dark-roast-espresso">“MAGIC Mushroom Coffee”</a> for “clarity, balance and natural energy.”</p>
<p>Little discussed, however, were the company’s local news ambitions. <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/kristinesherred">Kristine Sherred</a>, chair of the <a href="https://x.com/WaNewsGuild">Washington State NewsGuild</a> and the food and dining reporter at <a href="https://www.thenewstribune.com/">The News Tribune</a> in Tacoma, Washington, said that she and other journalists were left to wonder “why we were even invited.”</p>
<p>“Our takeaway was that it wasn’t about news,” Sherred said. “The future McClatchy appears to be building does not seem aligned with the mission of journalism.”</p>
<h3>“Agentify the entire enterprise”</h3>
<p>The meeting this month offered the clearest roadmap to date about McClatchy’s all-in approach to AI as one of the nation’s largest newspaper conglomerates navigates traffic declines, shifting consumer habits, and heavy debt obligations. It also comes at a critical moment for a media industry that seeks to modernize with AI — technology simultaneously blamed for <a href="https://www.cjr.org/analysis/traffic-apocalypse-google-ai-overviews-killing-click-throughs-news-sites.php">gutting web traffic</a> and touted as <a href="https://archive.ph/hVW7g#selection-1407.0-1411.43">a tool to cut costs.</a></p>
<p>“There is no sector that has not been disrupted this year by AI — everyone is going through extraordinary disruption,” Hunter said. Through a laser focus on AI-powered efficiencies, he said, the company can turn around its fortunes. “We can win — not with yesterday’s solutions, though.”</p>
<p>For McClatchy, which has undergone <a href="https://archive.ph/h036z#selection-1347.0-1351.114">aggressive cost-cutting efforts</a> since it was acquired by New Jersey-based hedge fund Chatham Asset Management in 2020, its pivot toward AI-generated content over the last year has been met with internal pushback and, from some readers, ridicule.</p>
<p>In May, journalists at The Sacramento Bee, the Miami Herald, and other McClatchy-owned papers <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/05/01/business/media/mcclatchy-ai-newsroom-byline-strike.html">withheld their names</a> from articles that were produced with its “content scaling agent,” a chatbot that repackages human-written news articles at varying lengths for different audiences — complete with reporters’ names.</p>
<p>Similar resistance has played out at other news outlets. Earlier this month, for example, reporters at the Akron Beacon Journal in Ohio <a href="https://www.axios.com/local/cleveland/2026/11/06/akron-beacon-journal-byline-withdrawal-ai">withheld their bylines</a> for two weeks to protest a tool that generates AI summaries of articles that, the journalists allege, undermines their work and provides readers false information.</p>
<p>Many news organizations prohibit the publication of AI-generated articles. But for McClatchy, they’ve become a core part of its business strategy and, according to Hunter, a key part of its “company-wide effort to agentify the entire enterprise.” During a contentious virtual meeting in May, Hunter pleaded with skeptical journalists to get on board or find another job.</p>
<p>The content scaling agent is now one of a dozen McClatchy tools that leverage AI to detect trending topics, write content, fact-check headlines, and serve articles to readers based on their reading habits. Outside content production, executives revealed during the meeting, McClatchy is using AI tools to detect the subscribers most likely to cancel their subscriptions, forecast future revenue, and identify potential advertisers.</p>
<p>Anyone at McClatchy can now build AI tools to support their workflows, Rajiv Pant, McClatchy’s chief product and engineering officer, said earlier this month.</p>
<p>“In every one of these, humans are at the command center, they aren’t just checking the technology’s work,” Pant said. “They set the direction, make the calls and own the results. The technology lets each one of them do more of the work that only they can do.”</p>
<h3>“People want the news”</h3>
<p>Trend Hunter content — including AI-generated posts — now appears regularly across McClatchy’s local publications and social media feeds, including articles with little connection to the communities those outlets have historically served. Recent examples include <a href="https://www.miamiherald.com/living/article317940364.html">a post</a> about winter “barn jackets” in the Miami Herald and a feature about collegiate football in Texas for the Kansas City Star.</p>
<p>Other posts highlight obscure beauty products and questionable health trends, including how <a href="https://archive.ph/o/uY5Fs/https://www.kansascity.com/living/article317184127.html">nugget ice</a> “has become the craze worth understanding,” how <a href="https://archive.ph/o/uY5Fs/https://www.kansascity.com/living/article317135994.html">saunas</a> have “reshaped social wellness,” and “<a href="https://archive.ph/o/uY5Fs/https://www.kansascity.com/living/article317048549.html%23storylink=cpy">what your poop color</a>, shape and frequency say about your gut health.”</p>
<p>Or, as one Kansas City Star reporter put it, the layoffs have created news websites where it’s “city reporters out, shitty reporters in.”</p>
<p>“They’re trying to kind of go toward these buzzy-type topics instead of local journalism that holds people to account,” said the reporter, who spoke on the condition of anonymity due to retaliation concerns.</p>
<p>Readers, an <a href="https://www.niemanlab.org/2026/11/ai-is-becoming-an-information-gateway-audiences-still-dont-want-it-making-their-news/">overwhelming majority of whom</a> are skeptical of AI use in newsrooms, have also taken notice. In a post on X, one social media user wrote that “people want the news” not “AI generated slop.” Another called into question McClatchy’s decision to <a href="https://x.com/jameslineky/status/2098177217997996137?s=43">publish an article</a> — across numerous local news brands — about why the actress Scarlett Johansson “changed her magnesium supplement routine after a spray left her feet feeling dry.”</p>

Who needs real human reporters when you can have this? https://t.co/Z82Vg67WtH</p> <p>— James (@jameslineky) <a href="https://x.com/jameslineky/status/2098177217997996137?ref_src=twsrc%5Etfw">September 10, 2026</a></p></blockquote>
<p>Given the rapid spread of AI-generated content online, McClatchy executives see the brand credibility of its legacy publications as a differentiator. Over time, Hunter said, the market will reward trust — and McClatchy is well positioned to capitalize on its local news audiences.</p>
<p>“It is important that we have credible, accurate and relevant content,” Hunter said. “We are not running from our standards, but we’re running toward a new model to help customers make better decisions.”</p>
<p>In recent months, <a href="https://archive.ph/C1qOV">as much as 20%</a> of articles published by McClatchy newspapers were AI-generated, according to data from AI-detection company <a href="http://originality.ai">Originality.ai</a>. (AI detection tools like Originality are <a href="https://prodev.illinoisstate.edu/instructional-resources/pedagogy/ai/detectors/">imprecise in distinguishing</a> synthetic writing from human-authored content.) Rather than building new audiences, McClatchy’s use of AI-generated content could erode trust among readers, said Jon Gillham, Originality’s founder and CEO.</p>
<p>Such a reality is already playing out in Washington, said Sherred of the Washington State NewsGuild. Sherred’s union <a href="https://www.kuow.org/stories/wa-mcclatchy-newspaper-reporters-strike-for-higher-pay-better-ai-regulations">went on strike</a> in May to demand “ethical limits” around the company’s use of AI. The guild said in a statement at the time it was negotiating for “freedom to write hard-hitting news stories that take time, and not be punished if we don’t churn out clickbait.”</p>
<p>“We hear this from people in the community all the time: They don’t want short snippets, they don’t want rehashed work and they don’t want AI telling them the news,” Sherred said. “The only way you get high-quality local news is by having high-quality local reporters actually out there making it.”</p>
<h3>“Cultivating passionate fans”</h3>
<p>Less than a week after McClatchy laid off journalists nationwide in September, executives sent the remaining staff <a href="https://archive.ph/C1qOV">an emoji-laced email</a> announcing “fresh talent”: several executives, two reporters and three editors for its “Content Innovation Lab” — the engine behind its new AI-powered lifestyle verticals.</p>
<p>A recent job listing for innovation lab writers notes the “company’s future depends” on an embrace of AI and the innovation lab is its “next-generation approach to content” where emerging technologies are leveraged to “fuel experimentation and productivity.”</p>
<p>“From barbecue to bourbon, water sports to hiking, and golf to college sports, we are going to develop a steady stream of reader-focused, high-quality content for national and regional audiences,” according to the job listing. Producers will use AI to launch new brands “tied to dedicated communities of interest around travel, food, outdoor recreation, sports and even crime.”</p>
<p>The insights into the company’s future editorial direction come on the heels of its steepest staff reduction in years, with layoffs of investigative reporters, government watchdogs, and other journalists focused on local news.</p>
<p>“The city of Tacoma no longer has a city reporter,” Sherred said. “What’s the purpose of a local paper if you have no one covering your local government?”</p>
<p>Hired to lead the Content Innovation Lab is <a href="https://x.com/pstiegman">Patrick Stiegman</a>, a 20-year ESPN digital veteran who, during the meeting this month, turned to sports media lingo to describe the goal behind Saltwater South, Camp &amp; Country, and its other AI-powered lifestyle brands. By “cultivating passionate fans” around obsessive lifestyle niches, he said, the company seeks to leverage reader data “and monetization opportunities” to guide coverage decisions.</p>
<p>Perhaps nobody put McClatchy’s pivot in starker terms than Ariel Storch, recently hired to lead an AI-powered rebrand of its video strategy.</p>
<p>“We make entertainment — not content, entertainment,” Storch said during the Nov. 6 meeting. “Think about a late-night show. Nobody tunes in to be informed, they tune in to laugh. The by-product is you leave knowing about a new movie, a new book, a new product.”</p>
<p>Building new audiences is critical as the company works to claw itself out of debt, said <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/joesettineri/">Joe Settineri</a>, McClatchy’s chief financial officer. Stabilizing McClatchy’s core business is necessary, he said, for the Content Innovation Lab and its other AI initiatives to be successful.</p>
<p>“Today, most of that profitability is already spoken for because, like most companies, we have a sizable amount of debt,” Settineri said. “Like a loan on a house, we have to pay it down or you lose the house.”</p>
