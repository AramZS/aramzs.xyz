<h1>Pentagon delays Patriot missile shipments to Ukraine</h1>
<p>US defence secretary Pete Hegseth has yet to sign off on 60 Patriot interceptors for Kyiv, including from European arsenals.</p>
<img alt="A PAC-3 MSE missile is launched, leaving a fiery trail against a cloudy sky over a landscape of green hills." src="https://images.ft.com/v3/image/raw/ftcms%3Ac1d6d578-0dd3-4a3b-9c80-908f8f20de69?source=next-article&amp;fit=scale-down&amp;quality=highest&amp;width=1440&amp;dpr=1"/>
<p>Need help? About 60 Patriot air defence interceptors the US already agreed to supply to Ukraine, including some in European arsenals, are still awaiting the Pentagon’s approval to be sent, according to three people familiar with the matter.</p>
<p>Delivery of the US-designed and produced PAC-3 and PAC-2 interceptors, which are being paid for by Kyiv’s western allies, has been held up by defence secretary Pete Hegseth, two Ukrainian officials and a third person familiar with the situation told the FT. Because Patriot missiles are American, Washington needs to sign off on their export and re-export, including from European stocks.</p>
<p>“It is very important that America authorise the transfer of missiles for Patriot systems that our partners have already paid for,” Volodymyr Zelenskyy said on Sunday.</p>
<p>In his Monday night address to the nation, he warned that Ukrainian intelligence had indicated that the Russians were preparing new strikes. “It is important that the United States and other countries on which the supply of anti-ballistic interceptors to protect our cities and infrastructure depends also pay attention to these warnings,” he said.</p>
<p>The delay in Patriot interceptors comes as Ukraine faces intensifying Russian air strikes on its cities and critical infrastructure ahead of winter.</p>
<img alt="A partially assembled PAC-3 missile on a support stand in a factory, with tools and workbenches in the background." src="https://images.ft.com/v3/image/raw/https%3A%2F%2Fd1e00ek4ebabms.cloudfront.net%2Fproduction%2F9ccaab74-4354-422a-acff-42b33727ca71.jpg?source=next-article&amp;fit=scale-down&amp;quality=highest&amp;width=700&amp;dpr=1"/>
<p>A further 20 or so PAC-2 interceptors, an older model, have also been allocated to Ukraine but are awaiting delivery approval, according to one of the Ukrainian officials.</p>
<p>The Ukrainian president said in late October that Kyiv had “been waiting for nearly a month for Mr Hegseth to sign off on missiles for Patriots and Nasams” medium-range surface-to-air missile systems co-produced by RTX (formerly Raytheon) and Norway’s Kongsberg.</p>
<p>The Nasams have since been approved, the two Ukrainian officials and the other person with knowledge of the situation confirmed. One of the officials said they were “on the way” to Ukraine.</p>
<p>Reached for comment about the weapons, a Pentagon spokesperson said: “There has been no change to Department of War policy regarding support to Ukraine.” They declined to answer further questions.</p>
<p>A separate process of granting Kyiv approval to produce its own Patriot interceptors is also being held up at the Pentagon, according to Ukrainian officials.</p>
<img alt="Donald Trump with Volodymyr Zelenskyy at a Nato summit in July during which he announced that he would grant permission for Ukraine to produce Patriot interceptors" src="https://images.ft.com/v3/image/raw/https%3A%2F%2Fd1e00ek4ebabms.cloudfront.net%2Fproduction%2Ff0645643-8c70-4646-8af0-b40cc8297331.jpg?source=next-article&amp;fit=scale-down&amp;quality=highest&amp;width=700&amp;dpr=1"/>
<p>Hegseth has signed off on the deal, but it has been referred to the Pentagon’s under-secretary for acquisition, Michael Duffey, for further review, according to a Ukrainian official and a person familiar with the matter.</p>
<p>The reason for the move was not immediately clear.</p>
<p>“The Department of War has pursued multiple parallel lines of effort to accelerate transatlantic industrial co-operation on the Patriot air and missile defence system with European partners and Ukraine, and continues that work to this day,” a Pentagon official said.</p>
<p>Zelenskyy has said the first interceptors would probably only be produced in a few years, but approving them now would send a strong signal of support and allow the lengthy process of setting up production lines to get under way.</p>
<p>Patriot systems are among the few western weapons available to Ukraine that can intercept Russian ballistic missiles. PAC-3s use so-called “hit-to-kill” technology, striking the target directly, while PAC-2s are legacy missiles that utilise a fragmentation warhead that detonates near the target, unleashing a cloud of shrapnel.</p>
<p>The US delay comes amid a sharp deterioration in the relationship between Zelenskyy and Trump, with the US president in recent weeks blaming the Ukrainian leader for not wanting to end the war and suggesting a “new president” was needed in Kyiv.</p>
<p>Trump has also scolded Zelenskyy for allegedly being ungrateful for US support and for striking Russian oil refineries, which he blamed for the rise in global oil prices.</p>
<img alt="Kryvyi Rih after Russian ballistic missiles hit Zelenskyy’s hometown at the weekend" src="https://images.ft.com/v3/image/raw/https%3A%2F%2Fd1e00ek4ebabms.cloudfront.net%2Fproduction%2F3ab087e8-be9f-4479-8cae-a67fb11cedcd.jpg?source=next-article&amp;fit=scale-down&amp;quality=highest&amp;width=700&amp;dpr=1"/>
<p>Zelenskyy said last month that Ukraine had received an average of 12 Patriot interceptors per month from the US this year, while Russia is firing about 150 Russian ballistic missiles every month.</p>
<p>The Ukrainian leader has asked Trump for a “winter package” of 300 Patriot interceptors, but his American counterpart has declined, citing low global stocks of the weapon.</p>
<p>The US war in Iran has led to a severe shortage of the Patriot interceptors worldwide.</p>
<p>The hold-up in recent months in the delivery of Patriot interceptors to Ukraine has deepened concerns in Kyiv that the Trump administration is using military assistance as leverage in its efforts to secure a quick deal before a new Democratic-led Congress takes office in January.</p>
<p>Ukrainian officials have interpreted that action, along with recent speed restrictions imposed on Starshield — the Pentagon-funded military version of Elon Musk’s Starlink satellite network — as part of a co-ordinated US effort to leave Kyiv vulnerable to Russian attacks and weaken its position, potentially making it more willing to accept territorial and other concessions to Russia.</p>
<p>Zelenskyy and other Ukrainian officials have said that Putin only understands strength, and they have called on Washington to stand up to the Russian leader, rather than placate him.</p>
<p>“Only a combination of diplomatic skill and real strength on the battlefield can produce the outcome we all want: a just peace,” Kostiantyn Yelisieiev, Ukraine’s former ambassador to the EU and foreign policy adviser to ex-president Petro Poroshenko, wrote on X.</p>
<p>“Diplomacy without strength,” he added, “is like Patriot without interceptors.”</p>
