<p>Let me be clear: you should not threaten your employees with physical harm or death. You should not do that to anyone you work with. Seriously. No matter how tempting it may be.</p>
<p>Why, you might ask, am I stating what should be obvious to every CEO and manager?</p>
<p>Because Friday's talker from <a data-track-click='{"click_type":"other","element_name":"body_link","event":"outbound_click"}' href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/20/technology/jensen-huang-nvidia-culture.html">The New York Times</a> about Huang's notorious, uh, management style reportedly included two settled lawsuits from former employees who complained about the verbal tirades.</p>
<p>This was not simply a rude boss — according to the Times' reporting:</p>
<blockquote>He told one executive that his mother should have aborted him, according to one witness. He said he would rip both arms off a subordinate and beat the person over the head to death with them. And he told another employee he would rip off the top of their head and "pee on your brain."</blockquote>
<p>I know what you're thinking: maybe that employee <em>did</em> deserve to have their brain peed on. I know that some days my brain functions at such a low level that a splash of urine could only be an improvement.</p>
<p>But <em>do not</em>, I implore you, <em>do not</em> threaten to rip your employees' arms off or the tip of their heads off. No threats to rip any parts off, in fact. And especially, crucially, do not threaten to pee on their brains or any other organs —internal or external— under almost all circumstances (I'm still working through possibilities where this might be acceptable but haven't come up with one yet. Let me know if you do).</p>
<p>A spokesperson for Huang told the NYT that specific claims from a lawsuit from a former employee were "false." The report says the spokesperson didn't answer other questions about Huang's behavior.</p>
<p>Until <a data-track-click='{"element_name":"body_link","event":"tout_click","index":"bi_value_unassigned","product_field":"bi_value_unassigned"}' href="https://www.businessinsider.com/jensen-huang-out-as-nvidia-ceo-affair-scandal-2026-11">the events of the last few weeks</a>, Huang was considered a rock star in the tech world, a visionary in business who smartly positioned his company for the AI boom years before it happened. To many people, especially those who would like to see him <a data-track-click='{"element_name":"body_link","event":"tout_click","index":"bi_value_unassigned","product_field":"bi_value_unassigned"}' href="https://www.businessinsider.com/nvidia-board-is-not-considering-reinstating-jensen-huang-2026-11">brought back as CEO</a>, he still is.</p>
<p>Yes, it may be tempting to look at the results — Nvidia's rise to become one of the most valuable companies in the world — and think that his communication style is something to emulate if you want to motivate employees. Huang's management style has its supporters, including Chamath Palihapitiya, who <a data-track-click='{"click_type":"other","element_name":"body_link","event":"outbound_click"}' href="https://x.com/chamath/status/2123819606154547451">said</a>, "This makes me want to work for him MORE."</p>
<p>"Are there other ways of doing it? Yes. But this way is proven…and peeing on someone's brain is just funny," said the "All-In" podcast host.</p>
<blockquote><p>This makes me want to work for him MORE. I’d rather work for someone like this. Someone with ridiculous expectations, impatience and vision and take the lumps that come with that. Guys like this will filter out all the mediocre people that can’t take the heat and won’t take you to greatness. What’s left are a team of killers capable of building the largest, most valuable company in the world. Are there other ways of doing it? Yes. But this way is proven…and peeing on someone’s brain is just funny.</p>— Chamath Palihapitiya (@chamath) <a href="https://x.com/chamath/status/2123819606154547451?ref_src=twsrc%5Etfw">November 20, 2026</a></blockquote>
<p>Again — Chamath, Jensen fans, future business leaders, anyone reading this — I truly must warn you that you really, really should not threaten to pee on your employees. No matter how effective that sounds as a management technique. Trust me, I think there are better ways to <a data-track-click='{"element_name":"body_link","event":"tout_click","index":"bi_value_unassigned","product_field":"bi_value_unassigned"}' href="https://www.businessinsider.com/how-to-give-negative-feedback-to-your-boss-2025-6">deliver tough feedback</a> to deliver to your direct reports (if urine-based threats are all that come to mind, perhaps ask ChatGPT to help you draft it).</p>
<p>Absolutely do <em>not</em> tell them their mother should've aborted them; if nothing else, you typically don't want to discuss hot-button political topics like abortion with employees, even in highly theoretical contexts.</p>
<p>Am I an expert on executive management techniques? No, I am not. I am but a humble reporter, living my life on the IC track with no direct reports. However, having attended several mandatory workplace harassment webinars, I can promise you that bosses should absolutely avoid doing this:</p>
<blockquote>In one instance, Mr. Huang went around a room, telling managers he would kill them, according to one person with knowledge of the incident. When he reached a person in their 70s, Mr. Huang said that the person was really old and everyone hoped they would drop dead.</blockquote>
<p>As a human resources person using ChatGPT might say: <em>you're not just issuing death threats — you're doing age discrimination.</em></p>
<p>A bigger question you might be asking is: are these incredibly funny? Well, yes, generally speaking, it is. However, you might feel quite differently if you were experiencing such a bizarre barrage in person, in front of your peers. I would also be remiss not to mention that these insults, even the "pee on your brain" one, are not totally original. I get it; in the heat of the moment, sometimes you reach for a cliché. I have personally used the threat of tearing someone's arms off and beating them with said arms on loved ones in my personal life (they know what they did, but we're good now).</p>
<p>Bosses who deliver mortal blows with insults are a rich vein in pop culture. Selina Meyer from "Veep" or Ari Gold from "Entourage" had deliciously creative one-liner insults to their underlings. Miranda Priestly and Don Draper had withering cruelty (is there anything harsher than the "<a data-track-click='{"click_type":"other","element_name":"body_link","event":"outbound_click"}' href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LlOSdRMSG_k">I don't think about you at all</a>" line in "Mad Men"?).</p>
<p>We, as a society, love a fictional Mean Boss! And it's partly wish fulfillment: wouldn't it be freeing to be able to just yell at whoever you want at work? To have the power to get away with saying outlandish things to the people who are pissing you off (metaphorically) at that moment, and not face consequences for it?</p>
<p>Let me suggest something: there is a lot of power in <em>not</em> telling someone you'll rip their arms off. There is powerful restraint in being able to control your unpleasant feelings and handle difficult interpersonal conversations without yelling or insulting. Try that!</p>
<p>But do not — please, I beg you — <strong>do not threaten to pee on anyone at work.</strong></p>
