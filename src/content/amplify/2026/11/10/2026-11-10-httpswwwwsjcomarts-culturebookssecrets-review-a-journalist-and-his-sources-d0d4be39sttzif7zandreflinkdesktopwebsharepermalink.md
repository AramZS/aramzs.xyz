<h2>Bob Woodward’s specialty is reporting stories for which he has guaranteed anonymity to those who talked to him. He now violates that promise.</h2>

<p>He should have called the book “Betrayals,” as its overriding purpose is to tell us who his unnamed sources have been over the long and lauded arc of his journalistic life. Co-written with Claire McMullen, a young writer from Australia, Mr. Woodward’s book exposes, hangs out to dry, stabs in the back, snitches on—pick your disdainful phrase—many of the people who had agreed to talk to him on condition that he keep their names secret.</p>
<p>Over the years Mr. Woodward has assembled a vast menagerie of undisclosed sources. His stock in trade—famously, and controversially—is the private or anonymous conversation, conducted on what journalists call “deep background.” In “Secrets,” he explains this phrase. It is, he writes, “newspaper terminology meaning I could use the information but not reveal where it came from.” As part of the deal, he also must blur their institutional affiliation in a way that makes it hard to track them down, even as he discloses that his interlocutors have been at the heart of the White House, say, or the FBI.</p>
<p>Mr. Woodward has built a career of global renown by reporting stories for which he has promised anonymity to many of those who talked to him. This solemn guarantee has been an essential precondition without which people wouldn’t have loosened their tongues. Yet in “Secrets” he violates his promise to a host of people.</p>
<p>“Most of the sources written about in ‘Secrets’ are deceased,” he tells us, a fact of immense convenience to an author who is out to break a covenant. He resorts to casuistry—a form of self-serving and shoddy reasoning—to free himself from his vows of nondisclosure. Many of the sources outed in the book, he writes, “are well-known public figures who have written and talked extensively” and have “published their own memoirs, papers, articles and oral histories.” As a result, he suggests, “some could already be identifiable as my source in public records.” Others, he adds, “gave me permission to name them.”</p>
<p>And so he reveals the names. Among them: Brent Scowcroft—the deputy national security adviser to President Richard Nixon and later the national security adviser to presidents Gerald Ford and George H.W. Bush—who was “a secret source for 35 years”; Richard Darman—the White House staff secretary and deputy Treasury secretary to President Ronald Reagan, and later the director of the first President Bush’s Office of Management and Budget—who was, in Mr. Woodward’s words, a “reporter’s dream source,” meeting or speaking confidentially with the journalist at least 58 times; and Potter Stewart, Lewis Powell, Byron White, Harry Blackmun and William Rehnquist, five justices who spoke to him (and his co-author Scott Armstrong) on condition of anonymity for “The Brethren” (1979), an exposé of the workings of the Supreme Court.</p>
<p>All these men are dead, as is Lindsey Graham, the senator who was, Mr. Woodward tells us, a hush-hush source whom he interviewed 74 times between 2009 and 2025. Also dead is C. Boyden Gray, the White House counsel to the first President Bush. Gray was “one of my lifelong secret sources, first on George H.W. Bush, then as ambassador to the European Union for two years under George W. Bush, and even reading me into key conversations he participated in during Trump’s first presidency.”</p>
<p>Are these men among those who later gave Mr. Woodward their permission to be named or identified? They’re not around to tell us and cannot take issue with the words the author puts in their mouths, the actions he attributes to them, or the interpretations he offers.</p>
<p>We’re supposed to trust Mr. Woodward that permission was obtained. And when it might not have been, we must go along with his decision to disclose names because these are “well-known public figures” who could “already be identifiable” from material in the public domain. But the fact is that they had not, so far, been identified, giving Mr. Woodward’s use of the word “identifiable” a weaselly, Jesuitical tinge.</p>
<p>In the case of the Supreme Court justices, Mr. Woodward appears to dilute the meaning of nondisclosure, suggesting that the version of deep background they were offered was a promise to keep their identities confidential “for their lifetimes.” Startlingly, he adds, since all of those justices “are now deceased, I am lifting the lid on our interviews with them.” (He doesn’t tell us whether his co-author on “The Brethren” was privy to this decision.)</p>
<p>In truth, Mr. Woodward has distorted the very meaning of “off the record” or “deep background.” He has gerrymandered the boundaries of confidentiality to sidestep established ethics. The journalistic generation following him will pay the price. They’ll have to tell their sources: “It’s off the record, non-Woodward rules.”</p>
<p>Here’s Mr. Woodward’s most nakedly specious argument: The sources he discloses, he says, were notable figures in the history of this country. “They are disclosed here to illustrate the extremely important contributions they made during their lifetimes as public servants and as sources—helping to explain what goes on inside our government and bring the public into the room where the most consequential decisions are debated and made.” This is breach of confidence masquerading as a civics lesson, betrayal dressed up as public service.</p>
<p>Laid bare in all of this is Mr. Woodward’s pomposity and his boundless conceit as a journalist who operates—as he sees it—in the eye of History. The public is invited to join, but only as secondary witness, peeping through the keyhole. For his is an Olympian dialogue, not so much between a writer and his sources, as between a lordly oracle and the gods of our inheritance.</p>
