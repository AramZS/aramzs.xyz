<p>I hope you’ll excuse the provocative title: I like starting my “hot takes” as such before getting into the legitimate reasons behind it (and the nuance of the position). In this page specifically I’ll be arguing that some of the values that the IndieWeb is built upon are not currently being upheld very well, and that in contrast <a href="https://paperpilot.dev/garden/atproto">ATProto</a>, the protocol and ecosystem created by Bluesky Social, has proven itself better according to those values. This article is formally addressed as a response to Brennan Brown’s article “<a href="https://brennan.day/the-indieweb-now-has-genai-and-claude-built-in-what-do-we-do/">Major IndieWeb Services Now Have genAI and Claude Built-in. What Do We Do?</a>“ and Aaron Parecki’s response to that article, “<a href="https://aaronparecki.com/2026/10/17/5/rebuilding-my-indieweb-projects">Why I'm rebuilding my IndieWeb projects with Claude</a>“. While reading those articles I simply couldn’t help but think the “<a href="https://indieweb.org/single_point_of_aaron">Single Point of Aaron</a>” issue is a really big problem and indicative of a broader issue of the IndieWeb failing to uphold its own values. I’m passionate about ATProto and love it for many of the same reasons I love the indie web, and felt I could make a strong case for how it succeeds in places the IndieWeb fails.</p>
<blockquote data-v-8509ce84=""><h3 data-v-8509ce84="">On IndieWeb Replies</h3><p>My digital garden only has a couple pages serving as explicit replies to other articles, and at time of writing I haven’t updated my website to mark up specific links as “u-reply-to” links. Just acknowledging it here publicly so it doesn’t come across like I’m just new to the IndieWeb or its specs. <h2><a href="https://paperpilot.dev/garden/atproto-is-a-better-indieweb#atproto-and-the-indieweb">ATProto and The IndieWeb</a></h2>
<p>Let’s start by quickly going over what I’m referring to with regards to ATProto and the IndieWeb. The former is the Authenticated Transfer Protocol, created by Bluesky Social, and I recommend reading “<a href="https://overreacted.io/a-social-filesystem/">A Social Filesystem</a>“ which goes over how it works on a technical level, but if not then we can continue with the following high-level overview:</p>
<p>On AT Protocol, user data is stored on Personal Data Servers (PDSes), a service that’s relatively easy to self-host and already has many instances across the network users can choose from. The user data is stored as JSON or blobs of data that follow specified formats defined in “Lexicons”. “AppViews” are the actual apps users then use - like Bluesky. Lexicons can be created by anyone, and when AppViews support the same lexicons then users can freely switch between AppViews with their data effectively coming with them. Different AppViews can then have their own moderation, features, presentation, and even be completely different “types” of apps (like Instagram vs Twitch vs Letterboxd etc) while maintaining a coherent identity across them all.</p>
<blockquote data-v-8509ce84=""><h3 data-v-8509ce84="">On Private Data</h3><p>All the user data is (currently) public, including every post, reply, like, follow, and (controversially) block. Private data is <a href="https://atproto.com/blog/atproto-spaces-alpha">currently in alpha</a>, and a popular library for creating AppViews called <a href="https://happyview.dev/">HappyView</a> already supports it, but this is a rather new development so moving forward there will be increasingly more AppViews keeping appropriate data non-public. Some AppViews have historically supported private data by keeping them “off-protocol”, which means they do <em>not</em> come with you when switching AppViews - for example, Bluesky’s DMs.</p></blockquote>
<p>The IndieWeb is defined by <a href="https://indieweb.org/">its wiki</a> as “a community of independent and personal websites based on the principles of: owning your domain and using it as your primary online identity, publishing on your own site first (optionally elsewhere), and owning your content.” Personal websites have existed long before, but the IndieWeb is explicitly about websites as one’s canonical online identity and being the source of truth for their content. It does this by defining various <a href="https://spec.indieweb.org/">specs</a> (such as webmentions and microformats) and recommended practices that can be implemented to connect personal websites to each other, forming a decentralized social media network. They also sell <a href="https://indieweb.org/swag">merch</a>, host <a href="https://events.indieweb.org/">events</a>, and take <a href="https://opencollective.com/indieweb">donations</a>.</p>
<h2><a href="https://paperpilot.dev/garden/atproto-is-a-better-indieweb#the-indiewebs-values">The IndieWeb’s Values</a></h2>
<p>If you look across the “<a href="https://indieweb.org/why">Why IndieWeb</a>” page and the “<a href="https://indieweb.org/principles">Principles</a>” page, I think you could group the points into a handful of specific values (in no particular order):</p>
<ol><li><strong>Permanence</strong>. The wiki calls social media sites “ephemeral” and recommends using stable permalinks for all content. Your content cannot be deleted without your consent. We should build things to last. Use PESOS and POSSE so your content can outlive the silos.</li><li><strong>Pluralism</strong>. All of the IndieWeb’s specs and services are suggestions, not requirements. They want a diversity of ideas, sites, and software.</li><li><strong>Interoperability</strong>. Different sites should be able to talk with each other.</li><li><strong>Sovereignty</strong>. Having a domain specific to one person or org creates a stronger association between the content and author than a mere handle on a large social media site. The domain becomes a canonical identity.</li><li><strong>Agency</strong>. You should be able to control what you and visitors to your site see and are able to do. You should be able to freely change what tools you use to participate in the web.</li></ol>
<blockquote data-v-8509ce84=""><h3 data-v-8509ce84="">On “Owning Your Data”</h3><p>I think that term, despite being oft repeated, doesn’t really work as a value on its own. Does it mean you own the hardware with the data on it? That you have control over which servers have it? Ability to view and export your data? Control over how your data gets used? Depending on your definition, the indie web is not particularly good about data ownership, so breaking it down into the above values makes it easier to dissect.</p></blockquote>
<p>Let’s go over each and how both the indie web and AT Protocol are doing across each value.</p>
<h3><a href="https://paperpilot.dev/garden/atproto-is-a-better-indieweb#permanence">Permanence</a></h3>
<p>Alright, I’m actually starting with a pretty weird value, in that its one I <em><a href="https://paperpilot.dev/garden/ephemeral-identities">disagree with</a></em>. So I <em>do</em> think ATProto (and social media in general) is better at being permanent than the indie web, but I actually like that a personal website is more likely to go away once a user loses interest in it.</p>
<p>When a traditional social media site gets shut down, like Vine, Google Plus, Cohost, or Pillowfort, it can feel really impactful due to the number of people who lose data, and that it wasn’t consensual. However, personal websites going down accounts for far more peoples’ data going offline. Personal websites can go down for many reasons beyond deliberate deletion, from the owner dying, hardware failures, or just running out of money to pay for hosting and a domain. These sites, by being decentralized and having generally less traffic than traditional social media are also less likely to have been scraped by archival sites like the wayback machine. The “digital dark ages” happened <em>before</em> social media consolidated into a handful of silos. In practice, the largest social media sites like FB, TikTok, Youtube, or X are so established they’re unlikely to ever be fully shut down, and have far more backup redundancy than any smaller service.</p>
<p>It then follows that using a large PDS like the official ones ran by Bluesky are similarly more likely to outlive any specific personal website as well. Users can also <a href="https://pdsmoover.com/">migrate between PDSes</a> near-seamlessly (preserving identity and social graph) thanks to the separation of identity, data, and apps. Being able to have a similar level of data portability on the IndieWeb is considered <a href="https://neatnik.net/hardcore-indieweb/">hardcore</a>.</p>
<p>Most prominent creators on the indie web have some sort of mirroring setup for their content: Some through PESOS, others POSSE. The website itself can then display things like the number of reactions on other platforms, sometimes even including avatars or names of people who have reacted or replied to a post on another platform (including in cases where the other platform doesn’t usually make the avatars and display names that prominent, but I’ll get into that in the agency section). Adding new platforms can be wildly varied in difficulty, depending on what data can be easily and reliably retrieved from the other platforms, and what types of data a site owner has already implemented support for.</p>
<p>In comparison, on ATProto divorces your content from apps and AppViews, so your data is already highly portable and in standardized well-defined structures likely to be supported by future software. And public data is retrievable and verifiable trivially by external platforms and users. While syndicating will likely always require some amount of work, this design caps how bad it can be.</p>
<h3><a href="https://paperpilot.dev/garden/atproto-is-a-better-indieweb#pluralism">Pluralism</a></h3>
<p>Despite the IndieWeb naming this value <a href="https://indieweb.org/pluralism">explicitly</a>, in practice the last 15 years have not created the flourishing software ecosystem. Diversity isn’t something that can be forced by a protocol or through governance alone. It’s a culture problem, and despite having a lot of programmers within the IndieWeb, there are bafflingly few alternatives to any software made for the indie web. I speculate its due to how personalized the indie web is - there aren’t many generalized solutions, and few developers who want to take their fun hobby and add all the features and assurances and documentation required to make something usable broadly. To be clear: this means <em>personal sites</em> have a lot of diversity (they’re great!) but the IndieWeb ecosystem for interoperability through micropub, microformats, and web mentions does not.</p>
<p>I also find it humorous that their page on pluralism contrasts it explicitly with homogeneity. Aaron, in his article about using Claude Code, argued that IndieWeb does not prescribe tools and thus using LLMs and vibe-coded software is perfectly fine. However, I’ve argued that those are exactly the kinds of things where LLMs <em>introduce</em> <a href="https://paperpilot.dev/garden/social-media-biodiversity">homogeneity</a>. Suffice to say, I’m skeptical that vibe coding software will actually lead to a diverse array of software. In his article, Aaron celebrated the creation of an alternative to webmentions.io created in response to his use of AI for coding. I think that would have been “damning with faint praise” if it weren’t so earnest.</p>
<p>To contrast, AT Protocol has been around for a much shorter amount of time but already has a plethora of servers and software across the entire stack, that really demonstrate developers experimenting and iterating and improving rapidly. For Twitter-likes alone there’s Bluesky, Blacksky, Mu, Witchsky, Anisota, and other more niche AppViews. There’s hundreds of different PDSes and an ecosystem of services for livestreams, videos, blogs, reviews, and so much more!</p>
<p>In my personal experience as a developer, using existing lexicons or creating new ones feels a lot better than the using existing microformats or creating new ones. In my experience as a user, I think its amazing to be able to try experimental social apps and carry my social graph with me. In contrast, on the IndieWeb every new platform or post type requires development work to integrate into the website and setup syndication for just to be able to claim ownership over the new data. I can explore fundamentally new ideas of what social software can be without trapping my identity and data in a silo. And ofc on ATProto you can still add new record types to your website as well, but either way you have ownership over your data.</p>
<blockquote data-v-8509ce84=""><p>I’m a big fan of <a href="https://semble.so/">Semble</a> and what it might turn into as it grows more popular, so I’m going to highlight it as a use of ATProto that is nothing like Bluesky. I’m a big skeptic of <a href="https://paperpilot.dev/garden/social-media">Social Media</a> and <a href="https://paperpilot.dev/garden/feeds">Feeds</a> in particular (regardless of algorithm). Semble is built by <a href="https://ronentk.me/">Ronen</a> and inspired by <a href="https://ronentk.me/post/stig_data/">applying stigmergy to collective sensemaking</a> - think ants coordinating through pheromone trails, but for social link sharing. Semble let’s you collect and connect your links and see what other people have connected it to, in a way reminiscent of ye olde web surfing.</p></blockquote>
<blockquote><p>I strongly believe in his vision, and by building it on ATProto he has ensured <a href="https://semble.so/profile/semble.so/collections/3mrbbmxuv3m2f">easy integration</a> into other services. In some of his <a href="https://ronentk.me/publication/users-to-makers-2022/">earlier talks</a> he suggests building on <a href="https://indieweb.org/Solid">Solid</a>, but I think switching to ATProto, which has the same promise of interoperability but with a much more diverse ecosystem, was the right call.</p></blockquote>
<h3><a href="https://paperpilot.dev/garden/atproto-is-a-better-indieweb#interoperability">Interoperability</a></h3>
<p>Interestingly enough, interoperability can sometimes be in tension with pluralism: having many ways of doing things, and allowing for a diverse set of implementations can make it harder for things to talk to each other on a protocol level. On the IndieWeb, web mentions and mf2 are <em>the</em> way to talk between sites. Perhaps the IndieWeb could more aptly be considered a singular “network”, like each of the silos are, rather than the meta-network “above” the other networks IndieWeb frames itself as being. Interoperability via mf2 and web mentions in this case is an aspect of the indie web that, in my experience, has been paradoxically held up as one of its defining features, yet downplayed as optional because its technical complexity otherwise harms onboarding.</p>
<p>In contrast, ATProto uses lexicons. Every service in the network uses well-defined structured data that anyone else, without the consent of existing services, can read or write to (still requiring the user’s consent, of course). Something I create in one AppView may be useful in another. Or I can switch AppViews if one makes changes I dislike. Or new AppViews can come around and change how I interact with my data, without needing permission from whatever AppView I originally created it within. This gives the enshittification-resistance promised by interoperability built into the entire network, rather than something optional that every user must understand and setup in order to take advantage of.</p>
<blockquote data-v-8509ce84=""><p>To use Semble as an example again, your Semble collections can appear while <a href="https://bsky.app/profile/did:plc:qy5pluw2bsuq2x6albsgkvx3/post/3muduru7x3c2x">writing in Anypub</a>, and Margin collections can appear and be followed from <a href="https://blog.cosmik.network/3mfryjkrqss25">within Semble</a>.</p></blockquote>
<p>I believe this value is actually a really important part of what makes the IndieWeb the IndieWeb. Personal websites themselves have existed for a long time, and interoperability is what turns those websites into a form of social media - so what the IndieWeb <em>does</em> is add interoperability to personal websites, thus creating a social media network away from silos. Prior to the IndieWeb there was really just webrings and good old linking to each other - both of which are great! With that in mind I think calling mf2 and web mentions optional kinda serves to artificially inflate the size of the IndieWeb (and conflating it with the indie web). It may help with onboarding, but as we’ll get to I think AT Protocol beats the IndieWeb on onboard-ability as well.</p>
<h3><a href="https://paperpilot.dev/garden/atproto-is-a-better-indieweb#sovereignty">Sovereignty</a></h3>
<p>Sovereignty on the IndieWeb more or less focuses on owning a domain. This at minimum means adding dependencies on domain registrars, DNS, and an SSL CA, and often other dependencies such as a hosting service. Staking your place on the web always costs becoming dependent on commons infrastructure and sometimes actual money as well. Free domains in particular typically end up as subdomains, which compromises on the merits of having a domain over a handle on a silo.</p>
<p>In comparison, AT Proto lets you use a verifiably owned domain as your handle without relying on SSL or even actually hosting anything at the domain. Your DID is typically a record on the centralized PLC directory which at time of writing is owned by Bluesky social and soon to be stewarded by <a href="https://blog.plcred.org/3mwlphq42d227">plcred.org</a>, but if that’s too much you can use did:web and host your identity more thoroughly on your domain.</p>
<p>I do not believe having a domain as a canonical identity is something that really benefits the average user, and the insistence that it does (and the barrier to entry involved in obtaining a domain) contributes to the IndieWeb’s nichedom. ATProto’s modular sovereignty between did:plc vs did:web, domain handles, and self-hosting your PDS allows users to incrementally increase their sovereignty with no up-front costs and being able to change domains later (e.g. from <code>foo.neocities.org</code> to <code>foo.com</code>) seamlessly (unless you choose to use did:web, but that’s your choice).</p>
<h3><a href="https://paperpilot.dev/garden/atproto-is-a-better-indieweb#agency">Agency</a></h3>
<p>At the risk of sounding cliche: agency <em>for whom</em>? The IndieWeb focuses a lot on the agency you have as a publisher of content. You control the css, the url, where the content gets syndicated, whether to have comments, a guest book, accessibility features, etc. It’s a double edged sword, requiring more up front work, particularly in learning the “right” ways to do a lot of web development, but makes every page so diverse!</p>
<p>On the other side, readers can use their pick of software to read any (properly marked up) post from their RSS reader, microsub reader, or plain old browser. There can sometimes be things a site bullder does that limits accessibility somewhat, but overall it does well.</p>
<p>However, there’s one area here where I think IndieWeb falls short and ATProto handles properly, and that’s data ownership when it comes to interactions across sites. Websites inconsistently support replies and reactions in various forms, such as web mentions, and almost never can someone have guarantees that the interaction they send will later be editable, retractable, or un-impersonatable. Once the interaction is sent, it is under the other person’s control, and the best you can do is display the retraction or edit on your site and hope they at least linked to it. The culture is also currently such that sending a web mention is granting full permission to put your name, face, and website on their page forever. In the most extreme example, someone implementing PESOS on a forums would be copying over an archive of entire threads’ contents, including potentially outdated usernames and avatars, without the consent of those having their posts copied nor even giving them the knowledge the act took place.</p>
<p>AT Protocol, in comparison, places all those interactions in control of the person doing the interaction, and they cannot be spoofed by other users. They can then be fetched live in-browser and will respect any edits or retractions. This allows you to engage with other’s content more freely.</p>
<p>That said, ATProto does not guarantee publishers have moderation powers over the replies and reactions they receive (although app views can make those guarantees), and I think that’s a genuine flaw. Although, to a degree people can always share their responses to another’s post, even if indirectly (like web mentions: a reply can be distributed even if the web mention recipient decided to not show the reply on their site).</p>
<h2><a href="https://paperpilot.dev/garden/atproto-is-a-better-indieweb#decentralization-and-bluesky">Decentralization and Bluesky</a></h2>
<p>Bluesky (reasonably) has tainted the opinions of many on ATProto itself. The IndieWeb wiki page for <a href="https://indieweb.org/AT_Protocol">ATProto</a> is quite critical, such as referencing old theories that pieces of the stack wouldn’t actually see alternatives get built and hosted, which as discussed earlier very much <em>did</em> happen to such an extent that the IndieWeb’s own lack of software diversity sticks out quite a bit (I think one could even argue Aaron has been given more influence over the direction of the IndieWeb than Bluesky has over ATProto). Even the centralized PLC can be avoided by using did:web for those sufficiently motivated (and there are proposals for other DID methods that may improve upon this aspect even further). In any case, I believe ATProto upholds the aforementioned values despite any remaining centralization concerns. The state of the ecosystem is in a place where I feel confident Bluesky cannot meaningfully enshittify or otherwise threaten it in the future.</p>
<p>Weirdly enough that page also contains a lot of Bluesky-specific criticism despite the wiki having a separate page for <a href="https://indieweb.org/Bluesky">Bluesky</a>. And it doesn’t even show off how to do syndications of blog posts via Standard.site’s lexicons, despite that being something highly practical for IndieWeb readers and would actually belong on a page about AT Protocol beyond Bluesky!</p>
<p>So yes: Bluesky Social sucks, but AT Protocol is actually pretty good and has reasonable resistance to Bluesky fucking it up. I like the Bluesky-critical posts by Coyote, most notably “<a href="https://osteophage.neocities.org/writing/bluesky-offloadable-moderation">If you've signed up for Bluesky, you've signed up for offloadable moderation.</a>” (and they’ve discussed potentially writing about their qualms with ATProto as well, which I await eagerly). In any case, I do not need to argue (nor do I believe) Bluesky and AT Proto are without their faults to make my point they serve the values of the IndieWeb better than the indie web does.</p>
<h2><a href="https://paperpilot.dev/garden/atproto-is-a-better-indieweb#onboarding-and-conclusion">Onboarding and Conclusion</a></h2>
<p>It’s not a value itself per se, but ease of onboarding is something I’ve been alluding to quite a bit and is, in my opinion, part of where the IndieWeb falls short of its goals the most. It even goes as far as to compromise on its other values (like interoperability or sovereignty) for the sake of onboarding, a tension noticed by Coyote in “<a href="https://osteophage.neocities.org/essays/indie-web-priorities">Which Part of the Indie Web Ethos is the Bigger Priority?</a>” and by the darth mall in “<a href="https://darthmall.net/2024/indieweb-is-for-devs/">The IndieWeb is for Developers</a>“.</p>
<p>But even with that in mind joining the IndieWeb still requires:</p>
<ul><li>Owning a domain</li><li>Finding or buying hosting for a website</li><li>Creating the “scaffolding” of their website</li></ul>
<p>All <em>before</em> the user can actually start participating in the network. These typically involve some amount of money and often quite a bit of research and learning. And after all those steps you’ve just created a personal website - not anything that actually makes it part of the IndieWeb yet! If there’s one take away from this article, it should be that IndieWeb asks every participant to work very hard to achieve the values of the IndieWeb, and ATProto makes these values accessible.</p>
<p>With that in mind, I offer one closing comparison to AT Prototocol: a network where participating requires as little as making a website on any AppView in the network, which immediately gives users many of the benefits of these values, with the ability to “go further” at their own pace by making their own site, making a domain their canonical identity, self-hosting their PDS, etc.</p>
<p>If you have your own website today, I encourage you to explore making records to make your website legible as a publication on ATProto (optionally on your own PDS) and setup your website as your handle. You can make similar records for your other content on your website as well and I guarantee you’ll love to see what ATProto-based software that unlocks for you. You can even get started with one of <a href="https://brennan.day/publishing-my-eleventy-blog-to-the-atmosphere-with-standard-site/">Brennan’s own articles</a>!</p>
