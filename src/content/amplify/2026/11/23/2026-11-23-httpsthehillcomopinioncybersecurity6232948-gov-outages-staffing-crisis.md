<p>This month has seen a deluge of high-profile and high-stakes technical failures across the federal government. A <a href="https://apnews.com/article/faa-ground-stop-air-traffic-control-023aa7d681da357310274f750da7ad4e">router glitch at the Federal Aviation Administration</a> resulted in the longest ground stoppage of flights since Sept. 11, 2001. Just days later, a classified internal network at the National Security Agency <a href="https://www.cnn.com/2026/11/17/politics/highly-classified-national-security-communications-network-goes-down">went down</a>. Alongside other private-sector disruptions that same week were outages at <a href="https://apnews.com/article/microsoft-outage-outlook-email-365-status-down-d92547a821930290db2964388016979a">Microsoft</a>, <a href="https://apnews.com/article/bank-outage-chase-wells-fargo-truist-50fee2693c22f597fa6ca51a97ef2cda">JPMorgan Chase</a> and <a href="https://apnews.com/article/apple-marriott-technology-outages-ff097d2a0bec767baf38a1bd8eff5829">Apple</a>. Even The Associated Press is asking: <a href="https://apnews.com/article/flights-grounded-microsoft-ai-chaos-ea5f7a1f95a38f65dd57e58cfc17672b">“could everything be broken?”</a></p>
<p>And that’s not even the worst news — we’ve been pushing out all the people who would know how to fix it.</p>
<p>We don’t need to turn to conspiracy theories about coordinated cyberattacks or foreign adversaries to make sense of the emergencies at the FAA and NSA; it comes as no surprise that government technologies and infrastructures are old and aging, and that perhaps most federal agencies have been <a href="https://fedscoop.com/the-gao-flagged-10-critical-legacy-it-systems-years-later-most-havent-been-modernized/">behind on much-needed tech upgrades</a> for decades. Yet this classic American tale of antiquated government systems is only part of today’s story. Over the past two years, we have seen an unprecedented exodus of tech talent from the federal government due to the actions of the Trump administration, leaving the nation even further on its back foot.</p>
<p>The government reports that the federal workforce has shrunk by <a href="https://data.opm.gov/explore-data/analytics/workforce-changes">more than 250,000 employees</a> since <a href="https://thehill.com/people/donald-trump/">President Trump </a>re-took office in January 2025 — that’s more than <a href="https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/03/13/federal-workforce-shrank-10-in-trumps-first-year-back-in-office/">one in 10</a> federal employees. Data from the Office of Personnel Management show the FAA alone shrank by almost 1,700 employees that year, or about 3.6 percent of its workforce.</p>
<p>The implications of these civil service reductions have not spared technical workers. While <a href="https://apnews.com/article/faa-firings-trump-doge-safety-airlines-27390c6a7aac58063652302df5a243d3">“safety-critical” jobs</a> at agencies like the FAA were perhaps protected from mass layoffs, <a href="https://www.nextgov.com/people/2026/02/agencies-lost-around-20000-tech-workers-last-year-and-now-trump-admin-hiring/411222/?oref=ng-author-river">almost 20,000</a> of the 325,000 federal workers who left in 2025 were in technology, data or telecommunications, including <a href="https://www.science.org/content/article/u-s-government-has-lost-more-10-000-stem-ph-d-s-trump-took-office">10,000 with Ph.D.s in STEM</a> fields. Entire tech programs and offices were halted, cancelled or closed.</p>
<p>Consider two innovative government programs that bring fresh tech talent directly from tech companies and master’s degrees into federal agencies: the United States Digital Corps and the Presidential Innovation Fellowship. Their <a href="https://fedscoop.com/gsa-looks-to-terminate-probationary-employees/">2024 cohorts were almost entirely fired</a> in early 2025, as the new arrivals <a href="https://www.politico.com/news/2025/02/26/early-career-federal-workers-fired-00206248">had been in government for less than a year</a> and were included in probationary terminations — part of the <a href="https://www.nationalreview.com/corner/obviously-doge-did-not-save-money">failed efforts</a> to cut federal spending by the Department of Government Efficiency.</p>
<p>Or consider the small-but-mighty government agency <a data-id="https://fedscoop.com/gsa-shutters-18f-possibly-leaving-agencies-in-the-lurch/" data-type="link" href="https://fedscoop.com/gsa-shutters-18f-possibly-leaving-agencies-in-the-lurch/">18F</a>, whose entire mission was to fix technology problems and modernize tech systems within the government. The 18F agency and its 90 employees were <a href="https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2025/mar/15/elon-musk-18f-x-false-claims">“deleted” by Elon Musk and DOGE</a> in early 2025 — not to mention DOGE itself, which <a href="https://fedscoop.com/usds-workers-quit-doge-tech-elon-musk/">took over</a> the in-house tech talent hub formerly known as the United States Digital Service before <a href="https://thehill.com/homenews/administration/5955468-doge-shuts-down-operations/">sunsetting</a> in July 2026.</p>
<p>It didn’t take long for federal agencies to realize the serious consequences of the massive reduction in force. A new <a href="https://thehill.com/opinion/technology/5680054-gutted-government-tech-programs/">Tech Force initiative</a> to hire 1,000 early-career tech workers was announced in December 2025, and by March 2026, the White House Office of Personnel Management had launched a broader <a href="https://federalnewsnetwork.com/hiring-retention/2026/03/trump-administration-expands-push-to-hire-early-career-federal-employees/">program to attract those workers</a> (back) into federal positions across the government. But, of course, <a href="https://www.nextgov.com/people/2026/05/tech-force-set-out-hire-1000-technologists-last-year-its-onboarded-10-so-far/413833/">federal hiring is slow</a>, especially for tech hires.</p>
<p>And in the meantime, <a href="https://www.nextgov.com/cybersecurity/2025/07/government-layoffs-are-making-us-less-safe-cyberspace-experts-fear/407074/">cyber threats</a> and <a href="https://apnews.com/article/social-security-benefits-retirement-disability-ac309ca60a4726329bbc5afc29d3b7fa#:~:text=Updated%2012%3A49%20PM%20PST%2C%20April%201%2C%202025.,that%20said%20they%20were%20%E2%80%9Cnot%20receiving%20benefits.%E2%80%9D">infrastructural</a> <a href="https://fedscoop.com/irs-paper-returns-processing-gao-report/">breakdowns</a> <a href="https://fedscoop.com/dca-crash-final-report-ntsb-faa-dod/">haven’t</a> <a href="https://www.cnn.com/2026/08/12/weather/nws-warning-glitch-outage">stopped</a>.</p>
<p>Whatever the root cause for any particular outage turns out to be, one thing is clear: by eschewing tech workers in the government, we’ve made the country’s systems harder to protect and maintain.</p>
<p>To prevent future tech-based disruptions — not only from old tech needing replacing, but also from new tech like generative AI that needs careful deployment and oversight — we must not only re-hire our government tech workforce, but also properly value them.</p>
<p>Initiatives like the Tech Force and the Office of Personnel Management’s Early Career Talent Network are steps in the right direction. But prospective federal workers might ask, understandably, why they would forgo sky-high industry salaries to take a government job where they risk being <a href="https://www.npr.org/2026/06/03/nx-s1-5742806/trump-federal-employees-civil-service-job-protections-schedule-f">fired at will</a> or, at the very least, <a href="https://www.pbs.org/newshour/politics/watch-trump-says-many-of-fired-federal-workers-didnt-come-to-work-and-are-being-weeded-out">publicly derided</a> by a hostile administration.</p>
<p>Future legislation, executive orders and hiring initiatives must encourage, foster and celebrate technologists who choose to work in civil service and public-sector roles. In today’s dual moment of corroding copper wire and moonshot AI tools, our national security depends on it.</p>
