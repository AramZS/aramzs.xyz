title: They Loved Their Proximity to Trump. They Won’t Like What Comes Next.
---
<figure><img alt="A person in a dark suit walks through an illuminated doorway and waves a hand. The backdrop features glowing red, white and blue designs." src="https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/11/23/multimedia/23shachtman-vhzt/23shachtman-vhzt-articleLarge-v2.jpg"/><figcaption> Kevin Dietsch/Getty Images</figcaption></figure><p>For a while there, it was great to be a Trump administration kid, or at least to be in business with one. The sons of the president and some of his top officials saw their net worth multiply. The mere mention of a Trump family member’s involvement could send a company’s stock price soaring. And when these princelings announced a summit at Mar-a-Lago, many of the world’s top financiers beat a path to the reception desk.</p><p>Come January, however, being a Trump-era princeling, or even just being in business with one, is going to be a lot more complicated. When Democrats take control of Congress, they’re almost certain to start investigating the novel arrangements that over the past two years have <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/30/opinion/trump-america-capitalism-rich-politics.html">fused public office with private gain</a>.</p><p>Some of the companies that profited so handsomely off their proximity to the Trump White House are already downplaying or even severing those lucrative connections. If they’re hoping legislators — or the general public — will forget what these companies did to get ahead, they will be sorely disappointed. They placed a high-risk, high-reward bet on the Trump administration’s way of doing business. They experienced the upside; now comes the down.</p><p>Companies that <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/30/opinion/trump-america-capitalism-rich-politics.html">did business with the princelings</a> or funded the White House ballroom are <a href="https://www.politico.com/news/2026/11/05/democrats-oversight-investigations-lawyers">rushing</a> to sign on with lawyers who specialize in congressional investigations. The federally backed Export-Import Bank of the United States, which had previously signed a letter of interest to put $900 million into a Trump-backed tungsten mining project, says it is no longer looking to fund the operation. The prediction market Kalshi <a href="https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-12/donald-trump-jr-out-as-kalshi-adviser-following-midterms?srnd=undefined">announced</a> it was parting ways with Donald Trump Jr., who had served as an adviser. Shares of Dominari Holdings, the Trump Tower-based financial organization at the center of some of Don Jr. and Eric Trump’s more esoteric deals, have <a href="https://finance.yahoo.com/quote/DOMH/">lost</a> more than 50 percent of their value in recent weeks. That’s all before the House and the Senate officially change hands.</p><p>The prospect of Congress actually performing its long-neglected oversight duties “immediately changes the dynamic in this pay-to-play environment,” says Donald Sherman, a former Senate investigator who now heads the Citizens for Responsibility and Ethics in Washington, a left-of-center anticorruption organization. “If you have to worry about your C.E.O. being pulled into a transcribed interview or deposition,” he adds, it “significantly raises the cost of doing business.”</p><p>The Trump White House is now going so far as to make the up-is-down, black-is-white <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/11/us/politics/trump-democrats-congress-oversight-committee.html">claim</a> that aggressive congressional oversight could itself be a crime.</p><p>There are almost too many investigative targets to choose from. ICE. Epstein. Artificial intelligence. The Iran war. <a href="https://www.wsj.com/politics/policy/top-democrats-target-trump-administrations-nvidia-china-records-0c212746?mod=mhp">Nvidia</a>. Price gouging. <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/11/us/politics/jim-himes-cia-house-intelligence-oversight.html">C.I.A. covert action</a>. One progressive advocacy group put together a <a href="https://www.politico.com/f/?id=000001a1-b1df-d2f6-adb3-b7ff7ad40000">dossier of corporate targets</a>, complete with proposed hearings and witness lists, and it ran to 182 pages.<strong> </strong>This month, Representative Jared Huffman, the ranking member of the Natural Resources Committee, <a href="https://democrats-naturalresources.house.gov/imo/media/doc/2026-11-09_jh_document_preservation_letters.pdf">wrote to the heads of 27 different companies</a>, including Boeing and UnitedHealth, telling them to preserve records in advance of congressional probes.</p><p>Right at the top of the incoming Congress’s priorities is corruption, starting with business transactions involving President Trump’s children or those of his advisers.</p><p>Democrats don’t need to persuade Americans to care about corruption; according to a recent Gallup poll, a stunning <a href="https://news.gallup.com/poll/713933/record-high-say-government-corruption-widespread.aspx">89 percent</a> already think it’s widespread. I spoke with a dozen congressional staffers, open government advocates and lawyers for companies expecting to be targeted, and almost all of them said lawmakers’ goal will instead be to link all these shady deals to the other big theme of the midterm election: affordability. They’ll try to show that the princelings’ profits come at everyone else’s expense.</p><p>“They’re going to try to find every possible way to connect corruption to concrete cost issues,” Dylan Hedtler-Gaudette, of the watchdog group Project on Government Oversight, said of House and Senate investigators. “Crypto is an example.”</p><p>Democrats have already got a head start there. Senator Richard Blumenthal, the ranking member of the Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations, has been looking into the Trump family meme coins since <a href="https://www.facebook.com/reel/1654851948491221">May of 2025</a>. In July of this year, he released the stories of <a href="https://www.hsgac.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026_07_27-Trump-Coin-Testimonials.pdf">three investors</a> who sank tens of thousands of dollars into those coins, only to get wiped out. (In all, about a million investors are estimated to have <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/07/04/us/politics/trump-coin-crypto-investors-loss.html">lost nearly $3.8 billion</a>.)</p><p>To dig deeper, Congress’s standard investigative playbook probably won’t work. As they did in the previous Trump term, members of the administration will no doubt invoke executive privilege to try to fight subpoenas, withhold records and drag out the process. Those strategies would be far less likely to succeed, however, for those officials’ children or their companies, or the broader ecosystem of businesses that has grown around them.</p><p>Zach Witkoff, the son of the president’s top envoy, helped form World Liberty Financial, a cryptocurrency company that’s all but certain to be a star attraction at congressional hearings next year. Don Jr. and Eric Trump are two of the other co-founders. The president himself is one of the company’s direct beneficiaries, having reaped <a href="https://www.forbes.com/sites/danalexander/article/the-definitive-networth-of-donaldtrump/">more than $300 million</a>, according to Forbes. So are investment vehicles controlled by the United Arab Emirates’ security chief, which bought 49 percent of the company. Shortly thereafter, the United States ended longstanding limits on the export of advanced A.I. chips to Abu Dhabi.</p><p>The Emiratis “basically figured out that the way to change foreign policy is to invest in the president’s family’s crypto business,” Representative Ro Khanna told me this year. No wonder President Trump had <a href="https://youtu.be/m2aC8lAOQbg?si=Z0NWofixoZa7Dbmx&amp;t=1957">no real answer</a> when Ross Douthat asked him about the deal on “60 Minutes.” There’s no good answer to give.</p><p>Congressional investigators are likely to point out that though World Liberty Financial deals worked out great for the Trumps — one transaction alone added <a href="https://www.cnbc.com/2026/06/10/trump-crypto-alt-5-sigma-ai-financial.html">an estimated $500 million</a> to their coffers — they left many ordinary investors in the dust.</p><p>The companies backed by 1789 Capital, the venture capital firm that counts Don Jr. as a partner, present a different set of concerns. Take Vulcan Elements, a North Carolina start-up that makes rare-earth magnets. According to reporting by ProPublica, in the months after the company received funding from 1789, White House senior counselor Peter Navarro requested that the Pentagon get involved. By the end of the year, Vulcan <a href="https://www.propublica.org/article/donald-trump-jr-vulcan-deal-white-house">got a $620 million loan</a>.</p><p>Vulcan’s chief executive, John Maslin, insists political favoritism had nothing to do with it. In a late 2025 interview, Mr. Maslin said <a href="https://subscriber.politicopro.com/article/eenews/2025/12/11/never-met-donald-trump-jr-mineral-startup-denies-political-favoritism-00686633">he had never even met Don Jr</a>. (And unlike, say, Jared Kushner, Don Jr. isn’t working for the administration while he conducts his business.) 1789, for its part, says it takes only minority, nonvoting positions in its companies and that its partners have never directly intervened with the government on behalf of any of the businesses in its portfolio.</p><p>“If I was making these kinds of calls and trying to do the things that, you know, we’re alleged to be doing every day, I think that would last for about two seconds before it was out there very publicly,” Don Jr. <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vcUlkZsbM1g">told a recent forum</a>. “So, the notion in and of itself is ridiculous,” he added.</p><p>Regardless, the attention and money from the Trump administration have been an enormous boon to companies like Vulcan. Its valuation grew from $250 million to <a href="https://www.axios.com/pro/climate-deals/2026/03/17/vulcan-elements-washington-harbour-rare-earth-magnet">$2 billion</a> between August 2025 and March. Representative Huffman’s committee had <a href="https://www.cnbc.com/2026/03/25/donald-trump-jr-subpoena.html">tried to subpoena Don Jr. over the Vulcan deal</a>; the effort was rebuffed at the time by Republican members. That will change when the new Congress is seated.</p><p>So how far will it all go? A single investigation into corporate cronyism can produce millions of documents and dozens of witnesses, and the Democrats want lots of them. The congressional oversight committees are understaffed, and on the clock; any probes have to be wrapped up by the 2028 elections. Meanwhile, Attorney General Todd Blanche has made it clear he considers it his job to protect the president and his top officials, so don’t count on the Justice Department to act on any criminal referrals that might result from all these hearings and investigations.</p><p>That means the primary risk to princeling-connected chief executives is reputational: a thrashing in Congress, on live TV and in a thousand YouTube clips. That’s better for them than a criminal or civil trial, but still, no executive wants to be the punchline in Senator Jon Ossoff’s next viral video. No company wants to be shorthand for corruption in a presidential debate.</p><p>The princelings, like many investors of the past half-decade, poured money into industries that rely on government intervention: defense, aerospace, advanced manufacturing. For a while, it must have seemed like a pretty safe strategy. Now when one of those companies gets a federal contract, it’s likely to be eyed with suspicion. That change of perspective is bad for them. For everyone else, not so much.</p><p>Noah Shachtman is a contributing Opinion writer.
