<p>A federal judge on Monday extended the <a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/sports/2026/11/14/judge-blocks-trump-administration-closing-east-potomac-park/">temporary restraining order</a> that bars the Trump administration from closing East Potomac Park and the area’s busiest public golf course.</p>
<p>U.S. District Judge Ana C. Reyes pushed the order’s expiration from Nov. 28 to Dec. 12. She found that the original grounds for the order still exist and wrote that, with Thanksgiving coming, “it is impossible to brief, hold a hearing, and have the Court rule” on a preliminary injunction before the temporary restraining order would have run out.</p>
<p>The administration had planned to close the park on Nov. 15 in advance of an extensive overhaul. President Donald Trump wants to replace the three existing courses with a single championship course “fully capable of holding Major Events.” The government cited safety concerns and the lack of an operator, which Reyes found to be pretextual.</p>
<p>The <a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/sports/2026/11/15/trump-vows-appeal-east-potomac-ruling-golf-operations-remain-unclear/">government is appealing</a> the temporary restraining order, which Trump called “highly destructive and political.”</p>
<p>Reyes set a hearing for Dec. 8 to consider a preliminary injunction that would keep the property open longer. The hearing could settle a key question that has left the course in limbo. Reyes’s original order kept the park open but did not require the government to run the golf operation. Last week she asked the parties to address whether she can order the government to operate the course to preserve the status quo.</p>
<p>In the meantime, <a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/sports/2026/11/20/trump-closed-beloved-dc-golf-course-dcs-playing-it-anyway/">golfers have kept playing</a> — for free, with no staff on-site. Over the weekend, the park was busy, with golfers on all three courses, runners and cyclists along Ohio Drive and families at the miniature golf course. Last week golfers brought leaf blowers to clear the greens and picked up trash. Flags and flagsticks had been donated by other nearby golf properties to ensure play could continue close to normal.</p>
<p>The course has been <a href="https://washingtonpost.com/sports/2026/11/16/after-one-last-sunday-beloved-dc-golf-course-is-closed-sort-now/?itid=ap_rick-maese_article-list_1_4">without an operator since Nov. 15</a>, when the National Park Service ended National Links Trust’s role running the course. No replacement had been lined up, and Reyes called the government’s decision to end the nonprofit’s management of the course “an entirely self-inflicted and self-serving injury.”</p>
<p>Reyes on Monday denied, for now, the plaintiffs’ request to subpoena National Links Trust. The plaintiffs had wanted to probe the circumstances of NLT’s “ouster.”</p>

<p>Nearly a third of Black adults said they couldn’t afford food for themselves or their families at some point in the past year, double the rate of White adults, according to a new Gallup poll.</p>
<p>The <a href="https://news.gallup.com/poll/716921/food-unaffordability-experienced-black-adults.aspx">study</a>, conducted through Gallup’s <a href="https://news.gallup.com/poll/246200/gallup-national-health-index-work.aspx">National Health and Well-Being Index</a>, found that 30 percent of Black adults struggled to put food on the table, triple the rate among Asian adults (10 percent). Among Hispanic adults, the rate was 24 percent.</p>
<p>Gallup found the highest rates in parts of the South, where 24 percent of adults reported trouble affording food in the past year. That’s more than double the rate in the Mountain West and above the national rate of 18 percent.</p>
<p>The findings come from a web survey of 5,065 U.S. adults conducted May 28 to June 5 in which Gallup asked: “Have there been times in the past 12 months when you did not have enough money to buy food that you or your family needed?”</p>
<p>Gallup released its report three days before Thanksgiving — and more than a year after the Trump administration <a href="https://www.reuters.com/world/us/trump-administration-cancels-usdas-annual-food-insecurity-survey-wsj-reports-2025-09-20/">ended</a> the Agriculture Department’s decades-old annual food insecurity survey.</p>

<p>This is not the week to test the <a href="https://www.instagram.com/reels/DHd1R0mxzNV/">Airport Theory</a>, the viral trend that glorifies the mad dash to the boarding gate.</p>
<p>“Thanksgiving is peak of peak for travel,” said Gary Leff, founder of View from the Wing, a travel blog. “That means more people in line to check bags and at security. It also can mean a bit more time getting through the airport.”</p>
<p><a href="https://www.airlines.org/news-update/u-s-airlines-prepare-for-busy-thanksgiving-travel-period-amid-record-high-jet-fuel-costs/">Airlines for America</a>, a trade association, has projected 29 million air travelers over the Thanksgiving holiday (Nov. 20-30), including 3.3 million people on the coming Sunday. The <a href="https://x.com/TSA/status/2123822429197770763?s=20">Transportation Security Administration</a> said it is anticipating nearly 17.6 million passengers between Tuesday and next Monday. Last year, the agency screened a record 3.13 million passengers on the Sunday after the holiday.</p>
<p>To ensure a smooth travel day, work backward. Start with the ride to the airport. Factor in potential traffic snarls, such as roadwork or airport construction, or public transportation service delays. For car shares, avoid long wait times and dropped rides by preordering your pickup. If you are driving, reserve an airport parking spot. Many parking lots require reservations at least 12 hours in advance.</p>
<p>Airlines and airports typically recommend arriving at least two hours ahead for domestic travel and three hours early for foreign flights. For checked bags, be aware of the carrier’s minimum drop-off time, generally 45 minutes before departure. To circumvent the queue at the counter, Leff recommends using a skycap.</p>
<p>“Some airlines charge for the service, but the fees are nominal and worth it when it’s the difference of making your flight,” Leff said.</p>
<p>Security checkpoints could be slower because of the higher volume of passengers and bags stuffed with holiday goodies. Familiarize yourself with <a href="https://www.tsa.gov/travel/security-screening/whatcanibring/all">TSA’s carry-on rules</a>. For instance, pumpkin pie is allowed, but the filling alone is not. TSA PreCheck might not move as quickly as it did, say, last Wednesday. Leff recommends signing up for TSA Touchless. While 50 million people are signed up for PreCheck, only a fraction are enrolled in Touchless.</p>
<p>“Those lines which use facial recognition are usually shorter because not as many people are signed up yet,” he said.</p>

<p>President Donald Trump has tapped Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton to serve as his acting national cyber director, after a spate of tech outages that affected airlines, banks and widely used software platforms.</p>
<p>The White House said Monday that Clayton would assume the role pending Senate confirmation of Trump’s choice for the post, Kirsten Davies, who is the Pentagon’s chief information officer.</p>
<p>Clayton, who as DNI coordinates 18 U.S. intelligence agencies, also chairs a White House task force on artificial intelligence. His appointment as acting cyber director was reported earlier by Axios.</p>
<p>Americans were rattled this month by a string of electronic glitches that, although they do not appear to be cyberattacks, affected vital systems. They included the Federal Aviation Administration’s air traffic control communications and Microsoft Outlook.</p>
<p>Trump last week <a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/politics/2026/11/19/white-house-replaces-top-cybersecurity-official/">fired</a> national cyber director Sean Cairncross and announced he was appointing Davies to the post in an acting capacity. She previously held cyber-related roles at major corporations, including the Estée Lauder Cos., Hewlett Packard Enterprise and Siemens.</p>
<p>Trump has now decided to nominate Davies as the full-time national cyber director, the White House said, adding that she will serve as a senior adviser to Clayton pending her confirmation by the Senate.</p>

<p>Thanksgiving is special — and not just because of the family and the food. It’s also the biggest running day of the year. More than a million people will participate in a <a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/wellness/2025/10/22/turkey-trot/">turkey trot</a> on Thursday. These races, almost always 5-kilometer (3.1-mile) events, welcome serious and first-time runners, fast-paced and ambling walkers, and 5-year-olds wearing tutus.</p>
<p>Doing your first or 50th turkey trot this week? These four tips could help make this year’s event especially fun, memorable and no sweat.</p>
<ol><li><b>Dress up. </b>Most entrants, including the speediest, don turkey hats, tail feathers or, the preferred option, a tutu. It gets you in the right — meaning irreverent — turkey trot mood. </li><li><b>Pick up your race packet early.</b> Having your packet, with your runner number and instructions, the evening before makes race day less hectic than fetching it just before the start.</li><li><b>You’re welcome to walk</b>. More people stroll than sprint (<a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/wellness/2026/02/22/jeffing-walk-run-technique/">though you can always do both</a>) at most turkey trots, running’s most low-key, welcoming event. Average finishing times at many trots are close to an hour. </li><li><b>Invite your opinionated relatives.</b> People who walk together often find common ground, developing “increased positive rapport, empathy, and prosociality,” according to a famous Columbia University <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28481583/">study</a>. So, let your firebrand niece accompany your older uncle and preemptively defuse disagreements before the dinner buffet. </li></ol>

<p>Senate Democrats are calling for an investigation into why Immigration and Customs Enforcement is <a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/immigration/2026/09/21/immigrants-with-final-deportation-orders-disappear-ice-detainee-locator/">erasing immigrant detainees from an online database</a> designed to help attorneys and family members track them while they’re in custody.</p>
<p>Sen. Elizabeth Warren (D-Massachusetts) and 20 other senators urged the Department of Homeland Security’s chief watchdog in a letter Sunday to probe reports that ICE is deleting detainees with a final removal order.</p>
<p>ICE created the detainee locator after years of concerns about human rights abuses in detention so that families, attorneys and the public may track immigrants in deportation proceedings.</p>
<p>In the letter, the senators said that deleting immigrants from the locator interferes with attorneys who must file lawsuits in the proper court district and with relatives who are trying to check on their loved ones.</p>
<p>“Erasing detained individuals from a public searchable system eliminates transparency,” the senators wrote in the letter to Inspector General Joseph V. Cuffari. “It can put lives at risk while making it easier for ICE to conceal any harm against people in immigration detention.”</p>
<p>DHS did not provide details about changes to the database, saying in a statement that “ICE does not ‘erase’ people” and that detainees may communicate with relatives and attorneys.</p>
<p>The DHS inspector general did not immediately respond to a request for comment.</p>
<p>ICE has held record numbers of detainees since President Donald Trump returned to the White House, including thousands with final removal orders, the letter said.</p>
