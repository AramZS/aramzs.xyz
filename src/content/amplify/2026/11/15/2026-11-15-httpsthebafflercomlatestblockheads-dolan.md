<p>Underlying any good puzzle is a key tension: How stupid is the solver willing to feel? Make the puzzle too difficult, and the whole thing feels impossible. But make the solution too easy, and what’s even the point? If the puzzle is simple enough to clock at a glance, you might as well be mindlessly swiping colorful fruits on your mom’s iPad. The sweet spot is somewhere amorphously in the middle: the fleeting moment between feeling immovably stuck and—<em>aha</em>! —the answer that had been there all along finally clicking into place.</p>
<p><em>Order of the Sinking Star</em>, the latest video game from the cult-favorite indie development studio Thekla Inc, is the sort of puzzler that leans into the inscrutable. <em>Order </em>is a riff on the Sokoban style of game design. The original <em>Sokoban</em> (a Japanese word which loosely translates to “warehouse keeper”) was a primitive PC game that about pushing boxes onto pre-set targets. That game’s title eventually came to encapsulate an entire style of game design. Sokoban games play out on grids, each level usually no larger than what can fit on a single screen. The moment-to-moment action should be familiar to anyone who has seen the shovelware mobile games advertised between YouTube Shorts: Push one block two spaces to the left, another block three spaces to the right, and your little avatar can freely pass to the awaiting princess. Things don’t get much more basic. Yet Sokoban-style games can be devilishy hard, a grueling of test of spatial reasoning and move sequencing. What makes Sokobans appealing to puzzle nuts is the same aspect that scares off others: There is rarely a conceptual gap between the solver’s understanding of the puzzle and its solution; no prompt to come back with another item or fourth-wall break (“<em>Raiden, turn the game console off right now!</em>”) to indicate that the player is supposed to be thinking outside the box. Rather, a Sokoban’s solution is never any more complicated than moving the right boxes to the right spaces in the right order. It’s video game puzzling boiled down to its barest bones.</p>

<p>Thekla pitched <em>Order </em>as the biggest, most innovative puzzle game ever made, one which even the most rabid puzzle-hounds will need hundreds of hours to fully complete. As a design-level mission statement, this is telling. Scale and scalability are the <em>Order’s</em> key themes. The game opens by dropping the player at the confluence of four magical islands, each extending out in one of the cardinal directions. Depending which direction you explore, a unique set of puzzles appear, mostly single-screen affairs that your player character descends into. Each of the four paths available from <em>Order’s </em>start features its own riff on Sokoban conventions (in the north you control multiple characters at once, the west has screens that repeat themselves recursively, like a Moebius strip, etc.), and you can ping pong between each at will. During <em>Order’s </em>earliest hours, each of these sequences functions as its own self-contained game. Progression is marked by an expanding playable area: Completing tricky puzzle sets will kick your character back out to the overworld screen, with new routes opening to deeper reaches of the four islands.</p>
<p>In this respect, <em>Order </em>is legible enough as a traditional puzzle game, albeit with unusually ornate graphics for a block pusher. But there’s a twist to <em>Order’s </em>design which sets it apart from similar titles: Each of these distinct directional paths is actually a remake of an existing Sokoban game, the original’s systems and puzzle designs ported into a new, shared visual shell. North is a medley of ideas from the <a href="https://sites.math.washington.edu/~ostroff/puzzles/Heroes_of_Sokoban.html"><em>Heroes of Sokoban</em></a> series, west is from the <a href="https://silverspaceship.com/promesst/"><em>PROMESST</em></a> games, east is <a href="https://alan.draknek.org/games/puzzlescript/mirrors.php"><em>Mirror Isles</em></a>, and south is <a href="https://alan.draknek.org/games/puzzlescript/skipping-stones.php"><em>Skipping Stones to Lonely Homes</em></a>. Each of the original games <em>Order </em>pulls from were modest, 8-bit-styled projects published and distributed for free online. Those games built a respectable following within the <a href="https://www.draknek.org/">internet’s niche puzzle- and indie-game obsessed communities</a>. But their creators likely had few illusions about their projects—or any Sokoban game, for that matter—having a particularly wide appeal.</p>
<p>Yet Thekla seems to have interpreted the supposed ceiling of Sokoban games’ crossover appeal as a challenge. The studio built off its lo-fi predecessors directly, licensing each of the smaller games (crucially, paying their original creators) and lifting their mechanics and levels directly into <em>Order</em>. Rather than coming up with arbitrarily unique Sokoban mechanics, and designing bespoke tutorial sequences to teach new players these rules, Thekla could port large swaths of this content, making small tweaks only as needed to tune difficulty and legibility. And so the design of <em>Order’s</em> opening hours was, functionally, outsourced. The sequence at the beginning of <em>Order’s </em>northward path, for example, is pulled almost tile-for-tile from opening to the <em>Heroes of Sokoban </em>web game. To a certain extent, this was a canny move: By borrowing extensively from the systems and level design of proven, if underappreciated, older games, <em>Order </em>could enter production with a good chunk of its nuts-and-bolts fundamentals already designed, skipping straight to the good stuff while still honoring the Sokoban “canon” in the process.</p>
<p>It also speaks to <em>Order’s </em>real focus: The way each of these subgames interact with each other. Moreso than any one-to-one remake of an older web game’s puzzles, <em>Order’s </em>novel contribution to the puzzle genre is the moments where these games intermix. Represented on the overworld map as golden rooms where each of its cardinal directional paths begin to fold back onto each other, these are also where <em>Order’s</em> meatiest puzzles appear. The gameplay systems initially siloed within each directional subgame start to intermingle in a way that’s brain busting: What happens, say, when the magic beams from <em>Order’s </em>westward path interact with the mirror mechanic introduced in the east? Or, if you manage to crack that puzzle, can you do it again while controlling the Thief, a character who pulls blocks rather than push? The effect of these unforeseen interactions is, in its way, exhilarating.</p>
<p><em>Order </em>spends the first dozen or so hours of its runtime carefully meting out new mechanics and teaching you strict rules, just to totally upend said rules once you begin to feel comfortable with them. It’s like being dropped in a hockey rink, handed a spatula, and told to score. The sheer combinatorics of what Thekla has integrated into <em>Order</em>—four sub-games worth of ideas, plus late-game pivots to entirely new puzzle paradigms, such as overworld puzzles, the presence of “enemies,” and even (Sokoban purists cover your ears) 3D—means there are a dizzying number of potential mechanical interactions. This daunting scale is being flaunted as <em>Order’s </em>selling point: over one thousand discrete, hand-crafted puzzles. The result, as they tell it, is the biggest puzzle game ever made.</p>
<p>It’s a lofty goal, I suppose. But to paraphrase Jeff Goldblum, it seems like Thekla was too concerned about whether they <em>could </em>pull such a feat off to worry about whether they <em>should</em>. Because while <em>Order</em>’s game-combining schtick is interesting on paper, the finished product frequently feels like less than the sum of its parts. The puzzles are unique, plentiful, and irritatingly clever. And yet as an actual game, <em>Order</em> comes off as cold, even clinical. It’s as if the game spends its entire runtime trying to prove how smart it is rather than focusing on pesky considerations like, say, “Is it enjoyable to play?”</p>
<p>Ironically, a lot of this has to do with a key constraint of the chosen system itself: the Sokoban style. The best puzzle games evoke a feeling analogous to discovery. Puzzlers as distinct as <em>Portal</em> and <em>Tetris</em> share a decidedly tactile design philosophy of teaching-by-doing, of letting players tool around with gameplay systems to learn something, even if they aren’t technically solving any puzzles. There’s a sense of progression to these games that’s a degree removed from actual achievements: Merely by existing in their worlds and engaging with their mechanics, players develop a constantly recontextualized sense of what is or isn’t possible with the tools at hand. The texture of the gameplay systems, and the puzzles themselves, teach players what is needed to solve them.</p>
<p>And yet because of their lack of conceptual depth, Sokobans lack this meta-level of puzzle design. Since the solution to any Sokoban is just a sequence of ups, downs, lefts, and rights, there is little wonder or whimsy to be gleamed by experimenting with its systems. The whole game can feel fiddly and unforgiving. Even in <em>Order’s </em>earliest hours—which Thekla has taken care to sequence in a way that introduces new concepts gradually—there were puzzles that I was stuck on for close to an hour, only to finally solve seemingly arbitrarily after brute-forcing every sequence of moves available. There was no <em>aha! </em>moment or opportunity to demonstrate that I mastered some gameplay concept. I merely floundered around for enough time, in enough of the available directions, that I bumped up against the puzzle’s solution. It might as well have been an accident. Sure, this is less the fault of <em>Order </em>in particular than an inherent weakness to the entire Sokoban school of game design. But it also begs the question: Why did Thekla spend the better part of ten years developing the “biggest puzzle game ever made,” just for it not to be particularly fun?</p>
<p>This hubris probably has a lot to do with Jonathan Blow, Thekla Inc.’s founder, lead puzzle designer and public-facing talking head. After a decade working as a contract programmer on triple-A games like <em>Deus Ex: Invisible War</em> and <a href="http://number-none.com/product/index.html">moonlighting as a magazine columnist</a>, Blow’s breakout was the release of 2008’s <em>Braid</em>. <em>Braid </em>was a puzzle-platformer that revolved around a time-rewinding mechanic. Its puzzles and plot were designed entirely by Blow (though he did hire artists for the sprite work) and, in something of a right place/right time situation, its release ended up a watershed moment for the nascent indie game scene. Due to the emergence of affordable home PC development tools like Unity and digital storefronts like Steam, the late aughts was a time when many solo developers and small studios realized that making video games outside the corporate mainstream was not only possible but viable.</p>
<p>Indie games could be smaller, smarter, weirder, and still end up even more successful than their bloated-budgeted triple-A cousins. <em>Braid </em>was a massive critical and commercial success. Blow netted millions of dollars in royalties and carved out a niche for himself as something like gaming’s resident public intellectual. In the wake of <em>Braid</em>’s release, he appeared in the era-defining documentary <em>Indie Game: The Movie</em> and became outspoken about what he saw as fundamental flaws in contemporary gaming. Blow’s pet topics were wide ranging: both aesthetic (<em>Braid’s </em>one time-traveling mechanic—in which you could hold a button to make the game rewind a la <em>Super Mario-</em>meets-<em>Tenet</em>—was a simple idea iterated on for an entire game’s worth of puzzles, exemplifying Blow’s philosophy of less-is-more, design-forward game development) and unapologetically technical (he is an outspoken critic of triple-A gaming’s management workflows and how their choice of specific technologies like game engines and programming languages have downstream effects on game efficiency and performance).</p>
<p>With funds from <em>Braid</em>, Blow founded Thekla and hired a small staff for his next game. This project, 2016’s <em>The Witness</em>, was a 3D puzzle-exploration game in the tradition of 1990s point-and-click cult favorites like <em>Myst</em> and <em>Riven</em>. It is also a masterclass in narrative and mechanical game design. Like <em>Braid’</em>s time rewinding and <em>Order’</em>s block pushing, <em>The Witness </em>also takes a single, provocatively simple mechanic—tracing a line through a maze—and iterates and expands on it repeatedly, until the end result is a totally recontextualized meta-commentary on the puzzle genre as a whole. Though <em>The Witness </em>is a far more elaborate production than <em>Braid</em>, it made a greater commercial splash and comparable critical reviews.</p>
<p>Thekla and Blow restuffed their coffers and solidified their reputation as some of the most forward-thinking developers in gaming. Magazines like <a href="https://r.search.yahoo.com/_ylt=AwrNaBEWc7BqOwIAg0JXNyoA;_ylu=Y29sbwNiZjEEcG9zAzEEdnRpZAMEc2VjA3Ny/RV=2/RE=1791158295/RO=10/RU=https%3a%2f%2fwww.newyorker.com%2ftech%2fannals-of-technology%2fthe-prickly-genius-of-jonathan-blow/RK=2/RS=OcvgBNmTCs9.ZhiwAVkv5UieufM-"><em>The New Yorker</em></a> and <a href="https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/archive/2012/05/the-most-dangerous-gamer/308928/"><em>The Atlantic</em></a> described Blow as an enigmatic genius, cranky but brilliant. Sure, he was as likely to put his foot in his mouth in an offensive tweet as he was to become <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gWqnz-7iQbY&amp;xstg=CAMSEBUJ_b-oH-PhF0yjBgavkzY%3D">embroiled in a faux, internet-breaking beef with Soulja Boy</a>, but that was part of his charm. This was the cultural zenith of techno-optimism. Silicon Valley nerds were celebrated for their ability to move fast and break things, for how they centered efficient engineering above all else. Both Blow’s games and his public-facing schtick were perfect fits for the cultural moment. His provocations functioned, if not exactly as selling points for his work, at least as stakes in the ground that proved his iconoclasm.</p>
<p>Yet in the ten years between the release of <em>The Witness </em>and <em>Order of the Sinking Star </em>something has changed. During Covid, Blow’s commentary—once mainly jabs directed at gaming industry rivals and wonks—took on a MAGA-ish flair. Like Musk, Zuckerberg, and company, Blow has spent the 2020s letting his libertarianism evolve from an intellectual flirtation to full-on philosophy. The specifics are uninteresting, the sort of thing you’d expect to overhear among tech expats living in Austin: vaccine skepticism and diatribes about DEI and a hostility toward California culture as a whole and Gavin Newsom in particular. More so than any of his games industry commentary, <a href="https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-01-22/jonathan-blow-a-controversial-video-game-designer-returns-with-a-new-game">these controversies have kept Blow in the news over the last half decade.</a></p>
<p>He’s also started to alienate his industry peers. At the end of last year, the developers of each of <em>Order’s </em>subgames took strides to distance themselves from Blow and his courting of press both good and bad. “In recent years it has become increasingly clear that Jon’s beliefs/priorities and mine are not aligned,” <a href="https://bsky.app/profile/draknek.bsky.social/post/3m7qyg3lbqk2s">wrote Alan Hazelden</a>, original developer of <em>Mirror Isles </em>and <em>Skipping Stones To Lonely Homes</em>. “He’s adversarial to people talking about privilege and representation, is dismissive of diversity efforts, has dabbled in covid trutherism, and is pro-MAGA.” Yet despite wanting to separate himself from Blow’s abrasive politics, Hazelden has also expressed frustration at how his own work is likely to be overshadowed by Blow’s name, brand, and baggage: “This industry’s obsession with auteurs is not healthy.”</p>
<figure><blockquote><p>Jonathan Blow’s drift rightward tracks with the rest of the tech industry. As do his illusions of grandeur and his projects’ issues with scope creep.</p></blockquote></figure>
<p>Blow’s drift rightward tracks with the rest of the tech industry. As do his illusions of grandeur and his projects’ issues with scope creep. <em>Braid </em>took Blow something like three years of full-time work to compete, working largely solo and financed using his own savings. <em>The Witness, </em>developed alongside a staff paid via <em>Braid</em>’s earnings, took seven. <em>Order of the Sinking Star</em>, despite its pivot back to (mostly) 2D and integration of existing titles in lieu of a traditional early game, took ten. This is likely due to a separate project Blow and Thekla have been working on alongside the game: an entirely new programming language called Jai. The specifics of Jai, and the problems it seeks to address, are unabashedly technical. Having started working on the language in 2014, Blow views the project as a way to put his money where his mouth is and address the specific technical gripes he has about how contemporary games are made. Jai could be thought of as a reimagining of what other existing programming languages (specifically C++) had those systems been designed from the ground up to address developing games. In a 2025 keynote lecture, Blow described the almost-finished product as a “language with ‘manual’ memory management,” designed in a stripped back way that’s directly applicable to the logic programmed into videogames and, more specifically, making them run as efficiently as possible.</p>
<p><em>Order </em>was programmed entirely in Jai, running on a bespoke engine Blow developed with the game’s demands in mind. As such, its release has to pull double duty: both as a standalone product, and as proof-of-concept for the larger suite of software that Blow has been developing in tandem. Like the puzzles found in the deepest corners of Blow’s games, Jai is at once conceptually impressive yet totally inscrutable for 99 percent of the population. It feels more like the vanity project (one that self-consciously echoes reactionary blogger Curtis Yarvin’s similarly “disruptive” <a href="https://www.compactmag.com/article/the-rise-and-fall-of-urbit/">Urbit language</a>) of an independently wealthy programmer than something that is realistically going to change how video games are made.</p>
<p>Blow has been vocal about his intention to make Jai open source once the language is sufficiently polished, no doubt to pad his reputation as gaming’s resident guy-who-isn’t-afraid-to-tackle-problems-head-on. But again, the concept creep—especially for a company who hasn’t released an entirely new commercial product since 2016—throws the whole mission into doubt. Surely there were more efficient uses of Blow’s and Thekla’s energy than rewriting game programming from the ground up and taking a decade to adapt a handful of web games into 3D? Plus, <em>Order </em>isn’t even necessarily a great demonstration of Jai’s capabilities. As efficient at compiling as Blow claims his technology is, the game’s frame rate still hitches on my Steam Deck every time I open the world map.</p>
<p>In an industry like video games—where the vast majority of what’s on offer is off-puttingly corporate, grossly juvenile, or an ugly combination of both—it could be argued that a figure like Blow represents a breath of fresh air. His provocative MAGA-via-Musk, Silicon Valley libertarian talking points are cheesy, but he has an actual aesthetic and logistical alternative vision for video games. Surely the closest thing gaming has to an actual artist, an auteur even, should be given a pass to sound off on whatever he wants so long as the work is good? Plus, he’s using his (comparatively small) fortune to fund offbeat puzzle games. It’s not like he’s hatching an evil plan to run the next Palantir.</p>
<p>Except that’s not exactly true. Blow’s outward facing persona as a disruptor—half Silicon Valley and half outsider artist—obscures the reality of <em>Order’s </em>release. Which is that as much as Thekla touts the game’s complexity and bespoke design as a selling point, the finished product falls into many of the same industry traps Blow has spent his career critiquing. Chief among these is scale, arguably the primary cause of the state of perpetual crisis the gaming industry has existed in since the pandemic. Much ink has been spilled about these problems, from Microsoft’s unsuccessful play at turning its Xbox brand <a href="https://www.reuters.com/business/world-at-work/microsoft-joins-ai-driven-tech-layoff-wave-with-4800-job-cuts-2026-07-06/">into a monopoly</a> (and subsequent layoffs) to Sony having to <a href="https://www.forbes.com/sites/paultassi/2026/08/19/sony-has-another-pending-playstation-live-service-fumble-on-its-hands/">cancel</a> a huge swatch of live-service games (and subsequent layoffs) to what’s probably the biggest gaming story of the decade: <em>Grand Theft Auto 6</em>.</p>
<p>As the series’ first entry in thirteen years, <em>GTA 6 </em>is emblematic of the industry’s unchecked hubris. From its promotional materials, <em>GTA 6 </em>will be the most technically dense, visually striking, and narratively sophisticated game ever made. While past entries in the series leaned into madcap, cartoonish energy, <em>GTA 6 </em>strives for a realism that puts even <em>Red Dead Redemption 2’s </em>horse testes to shame. The production has taken at least eight years, and while developer Rockstar recently denied rumors that the game’s budget had ballooned past $1.5 billion, it is still almost certainly the most expensive video game ever produced. <em>GTA 6 </em>is at once impossibly complex and nauseatingly expensive, yet too big to fail. When <em>GTA 6</em> inevitably nets billions of dollars, it will signal all the wrong things to misguided gaming executives, kickstarting yet another round of throwing good money after bad, bloated projects that are practically guaranteed to fail to recoup.</p>
<p>And so, despite how Blow sneers at big-budget, triple-A gaming, <em>Order of the Sinking Star </em>represents those same misguided management choices trickling down into the indie- and art games space. And unlike <em>Grant Theft Auto</em>, <em>Order </em>overestimates its appeal. Taking a decade to develop your least palatable product yet, accompanied by a software so niche most of your audience doesn’t even understand what it does, isn’t charmingly and combatively indie. It’s bad management, full stop. While laundering his abrasive personality via the trappings of auteurism, Blow has come to resemble both his generational cohort of “founders” and the stodgy execs they supposedly disrupted: convinced of his own brilliance, signing off on bloated budgets, and with no one around to tell him no. Its scale may be smaller, and its aims artier, but <em>Order of the Sinking Star</em> proves that stupidity is a problem in the gaming industry from the top all the way down.</p>
