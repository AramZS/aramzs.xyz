<p>A federal judge will soon take up the question of whether training AI models on copyrighted materials is fair use, in multidistrict litigation that could shape licensing markets and the tech industry’s legal exposure. In September, parties involved in <a href="https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/69879510/in-re-openai-inc-copyright-infringement-litigation/"><em>In re: OpenAI, Inc. Copyright Infringement Litigation</em></a> asked United States District Judge Sidney Stein to resolve whether training on copyrighted works is fair use, and related claims, without a trial.</p>
<p>On Nov. 6, Stein approved the filing of 56 amicus briefs in the much-watched case. The litigation began with separate suits, including two high-profile complaints filed late in 2023.</p>
<ul><li>The Authors Guild, an advocacy organization for writers, <a href="https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/67810584/1/authors-guild-v-openai-inc/">sued</a> OpenAI in September 2023 claiming that the company fed authors’ copyrighted works into its large language models.</li><li>The New York Times <a href="https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/68117049/1/the-new-york-times-company-v-microsoft-corporation/">sued</a> OpenAI and Microsoft over “unlawful use of The Times’s work to create artificial intelligence products” in December 2023.</li></ul>
<p>In April 2025, a dozen similar cases <a href="https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/69879510/1/in-re-openai-inc-copyright-infringement-litigation/">were combined</a> and sent to Stein’s court in Manhattan. The key issue is whether copying books, news articles and other copyrighted material to train models is “fair use.” OpenAI and Microsoft say it is, because the models “learn’ from the works rather than reproducing them. The publishers say it is unlicensed copying that yields products competing with their work.</p>
<p>Briefs backing the plaintiffs were filed by organizations including the <a href="https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/69879510/2373/1/in-re-openai-inc-copyright-infringement-litigation/">News/Media Alliance</a>, the <a href="https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/69879510/2359/1/in-re-openai-inc-copyright-infringement-litigation/">Copyright Alliance</a>, the <a href="https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/69879510/2304/1/in-re-openai-inc-copyright-infringement-litigation/">Association of American Publishers</a>, the <a href="https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/69879510/2393/1/in-re-openai-inc-copyright-infringement-litigation/">RIAA and the National Music Publishers’ Association</a>, <a href="https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/69879510/2332/1/in-re-openai-inc-copyright-infringement-litigation/">SAG-AFTRA and the Writers Guilds of America West and East</a>, <a href="https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/69879510/2373/1/in-re-openai-inc-copyright-infringement-litigation/">ProPublica</a>, <a href="https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/69879510/2413/1/in-re-openai-inc-copyright-infringement-litigation/">PEN America</a>, the <a href="https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/69879510/2377/1/in-re-openai-inc-copyright-infringement-litigation/">Open Markets Institute</a>, <a href="https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/69879510/2448/1/in-re-openai-inc-copyright-infringement-litigation/">America’s Newspapers</a>, <a href="https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/69879510/2456/1/in-re-openai-inc-copyright-infringement-litigation/">WME</a> and <a href="https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/69879510/2325/1/in-re-openai-inc-copyright-infringement-litigation/">Digital Content Next</a>.</p>

<p>“The briefs provide an outstanding resource for understanding the fault lines in what has become perhaps the most closely watched AI lawsuit in the world, and more importantly, a cogent primer on what’s at stake in our AI future,” <a href="https://publishingperspectives.com/2026/11/copyright-infringement-lawsuit-against-openai-draws-more-than-50-very-illuminating-amicus-briefs/">wrote Andrew Albanese</a> in the trade publication Publishing Perspectives.</p>
<p>In order to help readers engage with the key arguments put forward in the amicus briefs, we used Claude Code to produce two resources. The first is a visualization that depicts the most cited precedents in the briefs, giving readers a sense of the universe of relevant rulings that informed the briefs. The second is a compilation of key quotes and a directory of all 56 briefs.</p>
<p>These resources went through multiple rounds of review for accuracy, but they may still contain errors; the data is available for download so you can check our work. Other guides to the briefs have been published by <a href="https://www.techdirt.com/2026/11/02/the-openai-copyright-case-has-drawn-a-mountain-of-amicus-briefs-we-sorted-them-for-you/">Techdirt</a> and the <a href="https://progresschamber.org/resources/openai-mdl-key-amicus-fair-use-issues/">Chamber of Progress</a>, both of which are affiliated with amici supporting the defendants. Their counts differ from the below because they sort briefs by where their arguments land rather than by the side each brief declares. If you spot an error, write to <a href="mailto:contributions@techpolicy.press">contributions@techpolicy.press</a>.</p>

<p><b>How we counted.</b> Case citations were extracted from each brief's table of authorities and its text with the Free Law Project's eyecite parser, parallel citations were merged, and each brief's citations of these cases were checked against a search for the case's name. Cases were matched to CourtListener's citation database where available. The table view also counts references, every time a brief's text cites a case, including short-form citations and "Id.," as matched by eyecite; a sample of "Id." matches was checked against the briefs. <p><b>How this was made.</b> This analysis was conducted with Anthropic's Claude Opus 5.5, an AI model, with prompts from Tech Policy Press editors, who set the rules for categorizing the briefs. Claude wrote and ran the code that downloaded the briefs and extracted their citations, read each brief, and decided each ambiguous citation individually after reading it in context. Those decisions were not reviewed by an editor; each is logged, with its reason, in the downloadable data. A Tech Policy Press editor checked a sample of the quotes in the downloadable data against the briefs and found each one verbatim.</p>
