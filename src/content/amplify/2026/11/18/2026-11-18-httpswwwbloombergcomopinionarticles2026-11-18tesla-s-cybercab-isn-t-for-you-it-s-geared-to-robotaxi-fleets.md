<body><figure><img alt="Who’s it for?" src="https://assets.bwbx.io/images/users/iqjWHBFdfxIU/iIACVeVWhRSw/v1/-1x-1.webp"/><figcaption><p>Who’s it for?</p> / <p>Photographer: Billy H.C. Kwok/Bloomberg </p></figcaption></figure><div><p>Tesla’s cybercab is, in one sense, the ideal product. Here is a nominally autonomous vehicle that works, up to a point, in a bare sliver of the US; it can be rendered inoperable by Tesla Inc. if you do something deemed “improper”; and can’t be used to earn money as a robotaxi without Tesla’s permission. You aren’t allowed to resell your cybercab or modify it, even the gold paint job, and Tesla can collect video footage of whatever you’re doing in the car. Plus, the cybercab lacks a steering wheel and pedals, so it’s Tesla’s way or not the highway, as it were.</p><p>Surely every automaker dreams of persuading people to <a href="https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-16/tesla-takes-cybercab-orders-with-terms-even-musk-says-are-steep" target="_blank">  hand over almost $30,000 upfront</a>, and then $299 a month, in exchange for the privilege of quasi-owning that?</p><p>Tesla never wants for audacity. How else to describe asking people to pay tens of thousands of dollars, plus subscription, for this extremely limited version of autonomy after promising for years that Tesla’s regular vehicles were essentially ready to drive themselves? The cybercab’s invite-only sales event looks geared more toward the stock market than the car market. Still, it helps surface a bigger question with implications not just for Tesla but the entire industry: Who exactly is the cybercab for?</p><p>Vehicle autonomy is generally framed in terms of two broad business models. One is where self-driving fleets make money by ferrying people around as a service: robotaxis. The other concerns selling self-driving vehicles to people for individual ownership.</p><p>The cybercab makes more sense as a fleet robotaxi, albeit with the giant caveat that it only makes sense if Tesla eventually manages to deploy a self-driving system capable of operating safely at scale. The form factor, with just two seats and no controls, is more suited to short urban trips for paying passengers rather than people who require a vehicle to serve multiple roles. Tesla recommends, for example, that you don’t put pre-teen children in one. The lack of regular controls also means lower costs for the manufacturer, critical to reducing robotaxis’ cost per mile. </p><p>Autonomous vehicles are synonymous with robotaxis today, particularly in the US, where dominant fleet operator Waymo LLC has an estimated 4,000 vehicles across cities. Yet falling costs for the technology — the foundational assumption on which this whole edifice rests — means robotaxis cannot be the whole ballgame. Making a return on the gargantuan sums being invested in autonomy necessitates tapping as wide an addressable market as possible, beyond just urban and suburban ride-hailers. </p><p>Alex Roy, an autonomy expert and co-founder of venture firm New Industry Management LLC, has long argued in favor of developing personally owned vehicles <a href="https://www.thedrive.com/news/25928/why-im-joining-argo-ai?utm_source=chatgpt.com" target="_blank">“with a self-driving button</a>,” retaining manual controls but capable of autonomous operation when wanted. Besides the safety benefits of having backup controls <a href="https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-12-21/waymos-froze-blocked-traffic-during-san-francisco-power-outage" target="_blank">when autonomous systems get stuck</a>, Roy argues that personally owned vehicles come with valuable optionality: You can’t store your kids’ car seat and sports paraphernalia in a robotaxi, and sometimes you don’t want to wait for a ride. Plus, vehicles don’t just carry people and stuff but also heavier cargoes of personal aspiration and self-image, especially in the US.</p><p>While robotaxis represent a huge opportunity to anyone who can deliver a safe and profitable service, the bigger opportunity may lie in providing the self-driving brains for millions of personally owned models. “The really interesting thing will be when we get to reliable autonomy, not just these pockets of reliability,” says Mark Wakefield, global automotive market lead at AlixPartners LLP, a consultancy. For him, this would present “an iPhone moment” in the sense of creating a novel platform enabling new market segments, such as sending your privately owned vehicle to collect groceries, thereby competing with the Amazon delivery van.</p><p>We are a long way from there, but the convergence of automakers, technology providers and transportation services companies toward that goal is undeniable. Waymo’s partnership with Toyota Motor Corp., with the latter now poised to <a href="https://waymo.com/blog/2026/10/toyota-bz4x-partnership/" target="_blank">integrate Waymo’s hardware directly into one of its electric models</a>, is an important example. This is partly about providing the robotaxi operator with a new, dedicated vehicle. But it also opens the door to putting more advanced driver-assistance and autonomous systems into a range of vehicles that could be sold to the public.</p><p>Tesla is nominally doing this already, of course, but hasn’t yet cracked the actual, and rather crucial, scalable autonomy bit. Rather than the gimmick of selling cybercabs to fans who happen to live in certain neighborhoods of Texas, or who just want a pricey gilded art installation in their driveway, a surer signal of progress would be some other automaker paying to license Full Self Driving for its own models.
