<p>In August 2013, Starbucks barista Coulson Loptmann was partway through a seven-hour shift and hadn’t eaten all day. He supplemented his income with food stamps, but often didn’t have enough for lunch. That day, a co-worker had marked some breakfast sandwiches as expired and thrown them away, so Loptmann took one from the bin and ate it. A week later, <a href="https://www.thestranger.com/news/starbucks-fires-employee-on-food-stamps-for-eating-a-sandwich-from-the-garbage-17606083/?ref=thepourover.coffee"><u>he told Anna Minard of The Stranger</u></a>, he was fired.</p>
<p>According to Loptmann, his manager told him that the company considered his action to be stealing. In response to Minard’s queries, a Starbucks spokesperson claimed that it was against policy to consume food marked as expired, because “we do not want our partners to consume potentially spoiled products and get sick”. Starbucks said Loptmann <a href="https://www.huffpost.com/entry/coulson-loptmann_n_3829290?ref=thepourover.coffee"><u>was let go after</u></a> a string of performance issues, which the barista denied.</p>
<p>Loptmann was struggling to make ends meet at the time, and turned to food stamps after his hours were cut. He earned $9.94 an hour in Seattle—just above Washington State’s <a href="https://lni.wa.gov/workers-rights/wages/minimum-wage/history-of-washington-states-minimum-wage?ref=thepourover.coffee"><u>then-minimum wage</u></a> of $9.19, but more than $6 below what the Economic Opportunity Institute <a href="https://opportunityinstitute.org/2013/04/washingtons-minimum-wage-falls-far-short-of-a-living-wage/?ref=thepourover.coffee"><u>considered a living wage</u></a> in the state.</p>
<p>In many ways, conditions for the company’s workers have only gotten worse since then. Back in 2013, then-CEO Howard Schultz <a href="https://www.businessinsider.com/mcdonalds-and-starbucks-ceos-make-more-than-9200-an-hour-2013-12?ref=thepourover.coffee"><u>earned 1,096 times</u></a> as much each year as the average Starbucks barista. In 2024, thanks to signing bonuses and stock options for new chief executive Brian Niccol, that <a href="https://www.restaurantdive.com/news/starbucks-mcdonalds-ceo-pay-worker-wages/745064/?ref=thepourover.coffee"><u>ratio skyrocketed</u></a> to 6,666 to one. The Starbucks unionisation movement arose, in part, in response to this inequality.</p>
<p>Recently, <a href="https://www.esmmagazine.com/a-brands/starbucks-sees-annual-profit-above-estimates-as-turnaround-gains-traction-324983?ref=thepourover.coffee"><u>Starbucks announced</u></a> growing sales and a $1 billion stock buyback as its turnaround plan under Niccol begins to show progress. At the same time, many of its baristas are still struggling to make ends meet, with some relying on public assistance programmes like Medicaid and the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP, commonly known as food stamps).</p>
<p>While the company <a href="https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2024-12-12/how-much-does-starbucks-sbux-pay-raises-get-smaller-after-tough-year?ref=thepourover.coffee"><u>has made small</u></a> increases to its pay, they have typically fallen <a href="https://abcnews.com/Business/starbucks-raise-pay-us-retail-workers-3-year/story?id=104655793&amp;ref=thepourover.coffee"><u>below inflation</u></a>. Earlier this year,<a href="https://www.businessinsider.com/starbucks-changing-pay-perks-weekly-checks-barista-bonuses-2026-4?ref=thepourover.coffee"><u> it also trumpeted</u></a> a $1,200 bonus for workers—but only for those at top-performing stores. Meanwhile, the cost of living continues to rise and, as executives and shareholders cash in, many Starbucks baristas are struggling to survive. By displacing the costs of its poverty wages onto the state, Starbucks is embodying the modern “corporate welfare” approach to business.</p>
<h3>Starbucks and Food Stamps</h3>
<p>At the time of Loptmann’s dismissal, Washington actually had one of the better minimum wages in the United States. That meant he earned more than the average Starbucks worker, who made just $8.79 per hour in 2013, <a href="https://web.archive.org/web/20140212205106/http://www.nerdwallet.com/blog/investing/2013/466-hours-overtime-equals-hour-ceo-pay/"><u>according to a report</u></a> by Nerdwallet. Schultz, by comparison, earned the equivalent of $9,637 per hour that year.</p>
<p>Things have not improved since then. Last year, Starbucks’ average CEO-to-worker pay ratio was 1,794 to one. That figure was significantly down from 2024’s comically large ratio, but it was still the most extreme of the 10 large chains <a href="https://www.restaurantdive.com/news/restaurant-chains-starbucks-chipotle-wendys-chilis-ceo-ratio-2025/825581/?ref=thepourover.coffee"><u>surveyed by Restaurant Dive</u></a>. The median Starbucks worker’s pay in 2025 was just $17,279, only slightly above the federal poverty line for an individual, $15,960.</p>
<p>Because of these low wages, workers at Starbucks—as well as many other large American corporations—are often forced to rely on federal assistance. A 2024 report by the U.S. Government Accountability Office <a href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-26-108703.pdf?ref=thepourover.coffee"><u>found that</u></a> millions of SNAP and Medicaid recipients were in work, including at some large companies. Walmart and Amazon workers dominated the list, but employees of Starbucks, McDonald’s, and Dunkin also featured. In Washington, Starbucks’ home state, the report noted an estimated 242 employees received food assistance while working at the company.</p>
<p>While that number represents just a sliver of Starbucks’ <a href="https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2024-11-21/starbucks-shrank-retail-staff-8-despite-hundreds-of-new-stores?ref=thepourover.coffee"><u>roughly 200,000</u></a> U.S. retail employees, it is still stark when you consider that it reflects just one state—and that the company has <a href="https://finance.yahoo.com/quote/SBUX/?ref=thepourover.coffee"><u>a market value of</u></a> $119 billion. (Starbucks did not respond to questions about its wages or the executive-to-barista pay ratio. I could not find—and Starbucks did not respond to a request for—data on exactly how many of its workers rely on SNAP or Medicaid.)</p>
<p>These disparities seemingly undercut the claims Starbucks <a href="https://about.starbucks.com/press/2026/brian-niccol-opens-starbucks-investor-day-with-confidence-in-the-companys-turnaround-and-path-for-long-term-sustainable-growth/?ref=thepourover.coffee"><u>often makes</u></a> <a href="https://about.starbucks.com/press/2026/starbucks-announces-new-incentive-rewards-program-to-create-more-opportunities-for-partners-to-share-in-success/?ref=thepourover.coffee"><u>about</u></a> its “competitive pay” and “industry-leading” benefits package, which <a href="https://about.starbucks.com/press/2025/an-update-on-bargaining-and-our-commitment-to-partners/?ref=thepourover.coffee"><u>it says is</u></a> “worth on average $30 per hour for hourly partners”. To access that package, baristas must work at least 20 hours a week. However, workers <a href="https://fortune.com/2023/03/30/starbucks-barista-doesnt-qualify-for-wonderful-benefits-you-howard-schultz-unions/?ref=thepourover.coffee"><u>regularly complain</u></a> about <a href="https://www.reddit.com/r/starbucks/comments/1f8kj4e/barely_any_hours_but_working_every_day/?ref=thepourover.coffee"><u>how few hours</u></a> they <a href="https://www.buzzfeednews.com/article/venessawong/starbucks-executive-promises-frustrated-baristas-consistent?ref=thepourover.coffee"><u>are offered</u></a>, meaning such benefits are out of reach of many.</p>
<p>Tyla Foxe is a barista at a Starbucks location in Rockford, Illinois. She makes $15.62 per hour, in a state where the <a href="https://labor.illinois.gov/laws-rules/fls/minimum-wage-law.html?ref=thepourover.coffee"><u>minimum wage is $15</u></a>. Despite working around 30–35 hours per week, Foxe tells me that she doesn’t make enough to get by. “You could not live off Starbucks income even if you had a full 40 hours a week”, she says. “You would not qualify for any [apartment] around here”.</p>
<p>Foxe is living with a friend, and says most of her coworkers live with family or have several roommates. While she qualifies for Starbucks-subsidised health insurance, it would be too expensive to include her wife, and she isn’t able to take part in the company’s 401(k) programme. “I can’t even enrol because I can’t take the chance of losing any of the money I get per paycheque—I would not be able to live off that”.</p>
<p>Because of their low income, Foxe’s wife enrolled in SNAP, but the programme has a work requirement. A tough local job market meant they were eventually kicked off. “If you forfeit it you can’t reapply for three years,” Foxe says. “It was nice, but it wasn’t something that we were able to use in the long run”.</p>
<p>Foxe began working at Starbucks in 2025 at a store that had unionised three years earlier. That unionised status was part of its attraction. “That was one of the things I was originally looking for in a job”, she says. “It’s definitely been one of the better things about working there”.</p>
<h3>Welfare At Scale</h3>
<p>Researchers, unions, and activists <a href="https://www.teamstersjc42.com/when-corporations-dont-pay-a-living-wage-taxpayers-pick-up-the-tab/?ref=thepourover.coffee"><u>describe big companies</u></a>’ policy of letting public assistance <a href="https://www.taxpayer.net/budget-appropriations-tax/4-examples-of-corporate-welfare-in-action/?ref=thepourover.coffee"><u>supplement their low wages</u></a> as “corporate welfare”. A <a href="https://ips-dc.org/report-americas-20-largest-low-wage-employers-and-the-affordability-crisis/?ref=thepourover.coffee"><u>recent report</u></a> from the Institute for Policy Studies (IPS) described how the “Low-Wage 20”, a group of the largest U.S. employers—including Starbucks—fuel the affordability crisis by refusing to pay their workers a living wage.</p>
<p>“When corporations can get away with shifting their employees’ basic living costs onto taxpayers, this is a form of corporate welfare”, wrote Sarah Anderson, director of the IPS’ Global Economy Project and the report’s author. “It means taxpayers are subsidizing business models based on poverty wages”.</p>
<p>The IPS report noted that 45% of eligible Starbucks employees had zero balance in their 401(k) plans in 2024, which substantiates Foxe’s account of the struggle to save. Anderson also pointed to the $18 billion the company has spent on stock buybacks between 2019 and 2024, “money that could’ve gone towards worker wages or other productive investment”. That period was also the time when “growing employee discontent”, as Anderson describes it, led to the start of the current unionisation wave.</p>
<p>I have written about share buybacks before as part of an article <a href="https://www.thepourover.coffee/coffee-industry-profits-wealth-capture/"><u>on wealth capture by coffee corporations and executives</u></a>. Buybacks are a way for big companies like Starbucks—as well as Keurig Dr Pepper, Nestlé, and others—to remove stock from the marketplace, thus increasing the value of remaining shares. “This artificially increases the value of their shares and, in turn, the value of the executive’s stock-based pay”, Anderson tells me.</p>
<p>CEOs often receive stock options as part of their compensation packages, which can raise their pay significantly—Schultz <a href="https://web.archive.org/web/20131024000233/https://www.theguardian.com/business/2013/oct/22/best-paid-chief-executives-america"><u>made $191 million</u></a> in this way between 2008 and 2012, for example. Anderson notes that Niccol’s awards in his first two years as Starbucks CEO came to around $110 million. “With continued stock buyback spending, the value could significantly increase for reasons that have nothing to do with his managerial performance”, she says.</p>
<p>Starbucks has also benefited from other subsidies, such as <a href="https://tennesseelookout.com/2026/05/21/state-approves-30m-for-starbucks-nashville-location/?ref=thepourover.coffee"><u>the $30 million grant</u></a> Tennessee gave the company in May for opening a new corporate office in the state. The Good Jobs First <a href="https://subsidytracker.goodjobsfirst.org/parent/starbucks?ref=thepourover.coffee"><u>subsidy tracker also lists</u></a> tax credits, training reimbursements, and grants from states going back to 2002, with a total value of nearly $4 million.</p>
<p>All this shows that Starbucks could absolutely pay its employees a living wage. In 2018, the Roosevelt Institute think tank <a href="https://rooseveltinstitute.org/publications/curbing-stock-buybacks-crucial-step-raising-worker-pay-reduce-inequality/?ref=thepourover.coffee"><u>calculated that</u></a> the money the company spent on buybacks up to that point, reallocated to employee compensation, would have been enough to give every worker a $7,000 raise. Instead, Starbucks chooses to return that money to its shareholders and executives, and rely on the government to subsidise its workers’ wages instead.</p>
<h3>‘Why We’re Fighting’</h3>
<p>Securing higher wages is <a href="https://sbworkersunited.org/what-were-fighting-for-updates-on-contract-bargaining/?ref=thepourover.coffee"><u>one of the main focuses</u></a> of Starbucks Workers United (SBWU) in its <a href="https://www.thepourover.coffee/the-ongoing-fight-for-a-union-contract/"><u>contract negotiations</u></a> with the company. The union is demanding a $17 hourly wage floor; currently, most baristas start at between $15.25–$16 per hour. (This is down from the union’s previous demand of $20 per hour in 2024, which <a href="https://finance.yahoo.com/news/starbucks-union-lowers-proposed-wage-140001238.html?ref=thepourover.coffee"><u>it modified</u></a> earlier this year in an attempt to restart stalled contract negotiations.)</p>
<p>“Here’s the reality of working at Starbucks: stores are understaffed, baristas are struggling to get by, and lack critical on-the-job protections”, SBWU said in an emailed statement. “Many baristas already struggle to get enough hours to pay rent or even qualify for health insurance, leaving some of us to rely on SNAP to feed ourselves and our families and Medicaid because Starbucks’ coverage is out of reach”.</p>
<p>In September, like <a href="https://www.pbs.org/newshour/nation/why-health-insurance-premiums-are-rising-according-to-experts?ref=thepourover.coffee"><u>many other big companies</u></a>, Starbucks <a href="https://www.seattletimes.com/business/starbucks/starbucks-raises-health-insurance-premiums-sparking-worker-tension/?ref=thepourover.coffee"><u>raised its health insurance premiums</u></a>, with costs for some workers doubling. Foxe’s premium went from $100 to $165 per month for the most basic plan, a significant rise for someone living paycheque to paycheque. “This is exactly why we’re fighting for a fair union contract”, the emailed SBWU statement continued. “Starbucks should not be able to make life-altering decisions about our health benefits while CEO Brian Niccol makes 1,794 times more than the typical barista and flies around in a company-paid private jet”.</p>
<p>Starbucks’ claims of “competitive” pay and “industry-leading” benefits are directly contradicted by the experiences of Foxe, Loptmann, and the many other baristas who have to rely on public assistance to get by. It is not the only corporation to benefit from its workers utilising food stamps and Medicaid—but it is one of the few that has <a href="https://www.thepourover.coffee/starbucks-dei-backslide/"><u>actively sold itself</u></a> as progressive and equitable.</p>
<p>For decades, Starbucks’ public image was of a benevolent corporation that looked after its employees. But that image has not matched reality for a long time, if it ever did. Starbucks <a href="https://www.reuters.com/article/world/starbucks-to-cut-up-to-12000-jobs-close-600-stores-idUSN01299942/?ref=thepourover.coffee"><u>closed stores and conducted huge layoffs</u></a> in 2008; it <a href="https://www.thenation.com/article/archive/starbucks-history-union-busting/?ref=thepourover.coffee"><u>has been accused</u></a> of busting unions <a href="https://web.archive.org/web/20211210174757/https://www.huffpost.com/entry/howard-schultz-and-starbucks-long-history-of-fending-off-unions_n_5c535aa1e4b01d3c1f11b1f5"><u>as far back as 1987</u></a>; and has settled or lost multiple lawsuits over <a href="https://inthesetimes.com/article/starbucks-california-workers-wage-theft-howard-schultz-supreme-court?ref=thepourover.coffee"><u>unpaid wages</u></a> and <a href="https://journalrecord.com/2012/11/28/starbucks-must-pay-14m-for-unlawful-tips-policy-law/?ref=thepourover.coffee"><u>tip-pooling violations</u></a>.</p>
<p>Despite that, many still seek out jobs at Starbucks because of its reputation as a good employer. Foxe was one of those people: Even while working at a local cafe in high school, she wanted to move to her local Starbucks. “Around here, they do talk a lot about the benefits and all the things you’d get with it”, she says.</p>
<p>However, the reality was very different. “Once you’re behind the curtain, it’s not exactly all it’s talked up to be”, she says. “But that’s what we’re trying to fight for with the contract and the union”.</p>
