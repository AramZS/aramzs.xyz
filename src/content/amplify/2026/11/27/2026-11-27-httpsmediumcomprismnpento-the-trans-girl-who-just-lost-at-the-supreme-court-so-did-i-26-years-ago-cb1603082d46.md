<h4>A letter to Becky Pepper-Jackson about yesterday's trans sports case defeat</h4><figure><img height="563" src="https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*rsc9-ZSUhMGUQIgrGzsj5Q.png" width="1000"/><figcaption>Me, James Dale, minutes after the ruling in “Boy Scouts of America v. Dale.” June 28, 2000.</figcaption></figure><h4>June 30, 2026</h4><p>Today, the United States Supreme Court ruled against a sixteen-year-old girl named Becky Pepper-Jackson. She spent five years fighting for the right to compete on her school’s girls’ track team as a transgender athlete. Six justices said no. I lost at that same court twenty-six years ago, in a gay rights case I brought against the Boy Scouts after they expelled me.</p><p>I have something to tell her.</p><p>Dear Becky,</p><p>Three weeks ago, I watched you walk off a stage at the <a href="http://lambdalegal.org">Lambda Legal</a> National Liberty Awards, and I thought: she already knows who she is. That’s the hardest thing. Most people spend their whole lives trying to figure it out. You’re sixteen, and you’ve got it.</p><p>I introduced myself to you and your mom outside afterward. I told you I was proud of you and that I believed in you. I meant it then. I mean it more today.</p><p>I don’t know what it’s like to be transgender. I don’t know what it’s like to be as young as you are right now, fighting something this big in front of the whole country.</p><p>What I do know is what it feels like to lose at the Supreme Court, because I lived it. On June 28, 2000, the Supreme Court of the United States ruled against me in <a href="https://www.oyez.org/cases/1999/99-699"><em>Boy Scouts of America v. Dale</em></a>. Five justices said yes to discrimination. Four said no. Lambda Legal had fought alongside me for ten years, all the way to that building, and then we lost. They’re in your corner today, too.</p><p>The sympathy comes fast. It always does. But the loss stays.</p><p>Mine has stayed for twenty-six years. Then I remember the silence after. The world kept moving as if nothing had happened, while something enormous had happened to me.</p><p>So I’m not going to tell you it doesn’t hurt. It does. It will for a while. But it’ll also make you stronger.</p><p>What I want to tell you is this: if you need to step away and breathe, please do. You have your whole life ahead of you. You have done more than enough for your community and your country. You were eleven years old when this started. You didn’t ask to be a test case for anybody. You just wanted to play sports with your teammates.</p><p>And instead of quietly disappearing the way they hoped you would, you fought, for five years, all the way to the United States Supreme Court. Standing up to bigotry in the first place was winning. Six justices ruled against you. The other three said the Court was moving too fast, deciding your case before anyone had fully heard your story. That doesn’t undo what you already won just by standing up. You also went on to win the West Virginia state championship in shot put while maintaining a 4.0 GPA as your legal fate was still being decided, which might be the most athlete thing I’ve ever heard of. You have earned rest. Take it if you need it.</p><p>I needed it after my case. I ran, and I hid. I needed time away from all of it. That’s not quitting. That’s surviving.</p><p>I want to tell you what I heard when you said this from that stage three weeks ago: you don’t quit. You show up. You trust the process, and you come back stronger. You were talking about sports. But you were also describing exactly how change actually happens in this country. It’s slow, and it’s messy. And it usually takes way longer than it should. And then, eventually, it happens anyway.</p><div class="rw-embed-wrapper"><embed src="https://cdn.embedly.com/widgets/media.html?src=https%3A%2F%2Fwww.youtube.com%2Fembed%2FTjpa7v40j8U%3Ffeature%3Doembed&amp;display_name=YouTube&amp;url=https%3A%2F%2Fwww.youtube.com%2Fwatch%3Fv%3DTjpa7v40j8U&amp;image=https%3A%2F%2Fi.ytimg.com%2Fvi%2FTjpa7v40j8U%2Fhqdefault.jpg&amp;type=text%2Fhtml&amp;schema=youtube"/></div><p>I came back. Not out of obligation, but because a specific fight came along that I knew I could help with. The <a href="https://apnews.com/article/transgender-pentagon-scouting-america-complaint-agreement-foia-891a84f95decf4a2f3923e66f3b3b1de?utm_source=copy&amp;utm_medium=share">Department of Defense</a> has been threatening to push transgender kids and girls out of Scouting America, the same organization that fought for the right to expel me twenty-six years ago.</p><p>That organization has changed since then. Gay scouts, gay leaders, girls, trans kids, they’re part of it now. Not perfectly. Scouting America has a long way to go. But the door opened. And now somebody is trying to close it again. That’s a fight I understood. So I got back in the ring.</p><p>That’s the thing nobody tells you about these long fights. The arc doesn’t move in a straight line. The Boy Scouts changed, not because any court told them to, but because people came to understand how wrong it was to exclude a kid for who he was. That understanding built slowly, over the years, because people like you refused to disappear. Someday, I believe, America will understand with the same clarity why excluding transgender kids is wrong. When that day comes, it will be because of what you built. The record you made. The five years you actually competed and showed everyone watching that the sky did not fall.</p><p>Today’s ruling will never erase that.</p><p>I don’t know exactly when the next one will come. I waited longer than anyone should have to wait, and you deserve better than that. Every transgender kid in this country deserves better. But I’ve watched this country learn things slowly, painfully, things it should have known all along. We keep learning. I believe that, even today.</p><p>You don’t quit. You show up.</p><p>With so much admiration and love,</p><p>James Dale</p><p>For more reflections and updates on civil rights advocacy, visit my personal website at <a href="http://JamesRDale.com">JamesRDale.com</a></p>
