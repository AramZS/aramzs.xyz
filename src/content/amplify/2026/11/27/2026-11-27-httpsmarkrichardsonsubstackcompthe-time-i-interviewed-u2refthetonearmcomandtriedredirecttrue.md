I </span><a href="https://www.wsj.com/arts-culture/music/carnaval-de-luz-review-u2-returns-with-a-stylistic-sampler-4d9c6223">reviewed it</a><span> for the WSJ a couple of weeks back and thought it was OK—they have released worse records, and I liked parts of it, but it was hard for me to get excited about it, even though I wanted to. I really, </span><em>really</em><span> love U2, have since I was a kid. Two of my favorites when I first started following music closely were the Who and Bruce Springsteen, so I had a natural inclination toward big, humanist rock music that seemed like it wanted to change the world. In the ’80s, and for a little while after, U2 fit the bill. And in some ways, they still do, though I haven’t loved an album of theirs since </span><em>Zooropa</em><span>, which was 33 years ago. Still, I’m open to the possibility of being deeply moved by a new U2 record.</span></p><p><span>I often lose my connection to the new work of even my favorite artists, and when it happens I enjoy wrestling with the question Morrissey posed in 1986 in “The Queen Is Dead”: </span><em>Has the world changed or have I changed? </em><span>(I haven’t much enjoyed a new Morrissey-related song since roughly the time </span><em>Zooropa </em><span>came out, come to think of it). I feel a tinge of jealousy when I see people who sustain an interest in music by their favorite artists over decades, mostly because growing older with artists is fun, and I experience it too rarely. But I still root for U2—despite all the not-great music they’ve made, they seem comfortable with themselves and, as massively famous rock stars go, reasonably down-to-earth.</span></p><div class="subscribe-widget"><div class="pencraft pc-display-flex pc-justifyContent-center pc-reset"><div class="container-IpPqBD"></div></div></div><p>I met the band once—three of them, anyway—so long ago now that it seems like it happened to someone else. But the feelings around the whole episode are closer to the surface. In early 2009 I was living in Chicago and working for Pitchfork as managing editor. One day, my boss Scott mentioned that U2’s publicist had emailed about Pitchfork interviewing Bono on the telephone to discuss their forthcoming album. Scott asked if I was interested, and I said yes. A few days and a few emails later, the possible phoner turned into a possible on-camera interview with the entire band, to be shot during the Chicago stop on their press tour.</p><p>I didn’t love the sound of that. The year before, I’d done a video interview with Animal Collective when they played the Pitchfork Music Festival, and it was a minor disaster. I enjoy talking to artists about their work, but the way interviews worked best for me, especially then, was to try and build trust and engage the subject in one-on-one conversation. This had to do with my own comfort as much as theirs—if I was relaxed and listening and responding, I was more likely to elicit something interesting. I never had any burning desire to be an on-camera personality, and talking to someone with managers, publicists, and technical people looking on wasn’t something I had much experience with.</p><p><span>When I spoke with Animal Collective in the field house at Union Park in 2008, there were five or six other people in the room along with several cameras. I’d interviewed the band before, but trying to drive a conversation with all those eyes watching just wasn’t working for me, and at one point I basically froze. I sat silent for at least a minute, maybe longer, fumbling as I tried to remember the next question, or </span><em>any</em><span> question, while everyone was looking at me, probably taking pity. Not gonna lie—it was awful, a humiliating incident turned into a thought-loop that still plays over in my mind once in a while 18 years later. I got through it, and fortunately, there’s this thing called editing, which saved me further embarrassment. In the years since, I’ve done video interviews and hosted conversations in auditoriums and so forth, and it could be enjoyable—doing these things well is mostly a matter of practice. Back then, though, whew, it was rough sledding.</span></p><p><span>So you can imagine that I was a wee bit nervous about the prospect of sitting down with U2 on video. The publicist was pitching it as something like “their first-ever internet-only video interview,” or maybe their second, either of which sounds odd now, but the internet was a different thing then and YouTube was barely a few years old. They wanted to know who would be conducting this interview, what that person felt about the band, and whether that person had written about them previously. There was a screening process with several rounds of questions between Scott and the publicist—I wasn’t involved, except to feed him answers—and someone scrutinized the review of the </span><em>U218 Singles</em><span> compilation I’d written for the site in 2006 (rather tossed-off, I admit, befitting the tossed-off nature of the release). Eventually, they said the interview would move forward and I could conduct it.</span></p><p>The period leading up to the interview was a chaotic blur for other reasons entirely. A massive redesign of the site that we’d been working on for months was almost complete, and it was an all-hands-on-deck situation—reviewing pages on the staging site, editing metadata, futzing around in the new CMS, logging bugs. We’d mostly start on these fixes once the workday was complete, so I was often working until 8 or 9 pm on redesign tasks. Before I knew it, the interview was scheduled to happen the following morning, and I’d barely given it a thought. So I crammed—I listened to the new record a few more times, tried to patch holes in my knowledge about the band and its catalog, and wrote out enough questions to fill the 45 minutes we’d been allotted.</p><p>Waking up the next morning after very little sleep, I surprised myself by feeling relatively relaxed—still nervous, but not to the point of paralysis. I tried not to think about what would happen if the Animal Collective situation repeated. It’s just a conversation with a rock band about their new record, I told myself, no lives are at stake. Sometimes that reminder works. The interview was to take place at Smart Bar, the club in the basement of the Chicago live music institution the Metro, and I took the bus there from my apartment in Lincoln Square. I saw some Pitchfork.tv people unloading gear on the sidewalk when I got there—the site’s video operation was based in Brooklyn and these were contractors, so I didn’t know them personally, at least not well—and we made our way downstairs where the publicist waved me down as I entered the space.</p><p><span>She looked concerned. First thing, she mentioned the review Pitchfork had run of U2’s new album, </span><em><a href="https://pitchfork.com/reviews/albums/12730-no-line-on-the-horizon/">No Line on the Horizon</a></em><span>, and said, “The team was really disappointed to see that.” Ryan Dombal had rated the record a 4.2 on the site’s 10-point scale, and his piece included observations like “‘Surrender’—reportedly improvised in one seven-minute take—comes across as lazy indulgence, and the title track’s hard-nosed verse is torpedoed by its deflating fart of a hook” and rendered final judgment with “</span><em>Horizon</em><span> is clearly playing not to lose—it’s a defensive gesture, and a rather pitiful one at that.” For comparison, Rolling Stone had just given the record 5 stars, and the thrust of the critical response was mostly “U2 is experimenting with Eno and Lanois, like the old days!”</span></p><p>The review had gone up several days earlier, but somehow—this seems crazy to me now—I was so busy I didn’t clock the implications of it. As she spoke, it hit me: after the long screening process and everything it took to make the interview happen, we’d published a harsh pan of the band I was going to be sitting down with. I felt bad for the publicist, who was just doing her job and probably had people angry at her, wondering how this had slipped through. I thought about the transactional nature of the music press, and how frustrating I found it, and how much I preferred being a critic to chasing exclusives. All I could say was, “Sorry about that, reviews are a separate thing,” and she responded with something like, “I told the team: that’s just the way they do it,” though I wasn’t sure exactly what she meant. Did “the team” include the band, I wondered? My anxiety level climbed a bit.</p><p>Between our crew and the U2 entourage, there were roughly 25 people around, busily setting up equipment and arranging the space. I was surprised to see that U2 had an advance team, several people whose job appeared to be mapping out how the band would get in and out of the space. I overheard them saying things like “OK, they’ll walk through those doors, down to this booth, and they will turn left here” and someone else would respond “Yeah, that sounds good, let me check with Dan,” and then another person would be consulted about their path from the green room to the tables near the cameras. </p><p>We were ready to go, and somewhere on the far side of the venue, U2 filed in. I heard Bono before I saw him, singing “O Sole Mio,” just messing around. The Edge and Adam Clayton followed him, and when they arrived at where we’d be shooting they mentioned Larry wouldn’t be joining us. I ran through a couple of shaky takes introducing the segment with the band behind me, and started on my questions. I had them typed out, and the paper folded up next to me on my seat, but I was hoping I wouldn’t have to consult it, and it turned out I didn’t. As you might guess, Bono loves to talk, and I bounced between asking the questions I could remember and following up on the band’s responses. They were relaxed and charming, and ultimately it was pretty easy.</p><p><span>Finally, someone signaled that we needed to wrap it up—believe me when I tell you I was happy to see that. Then Bono cut in and asked, “Can I say something about Pitchfork?” This was unexpected. In the single second between his question and me responding with “Yes,” many thoughts went through my mind, most of which had to do with the fact that we’d just shit on his band’s new album from a great height, when nearly every other publication was praising it. And then </span><a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xSUAZGq0YI4">he said</a><span>:</span></p><blockquote><p>Why we wanted to be with you today—apart from to talk about ourselves, which is really hard, and sell you our wares—is because what I’d like to think we have in common is that people who go on Pitchfork are people who take music very seriously and without being pious or po-faced or just a boring bastard about it. We do too. Even people who loathe us and can’t stand the sound that we make, they know at least that we see music as a kind of sacrament, something sacred. And we live in a moment when music is becoming less and less a precious thing in the culture. It’s like tap water. It’s just background music. And I just wanted to pay respect to your audience, because there are people who really want to know and have a curiosity about what’s right and what’s wrong, what’s great and what’s happening, and we respect that.</p></blockquote><p>The cameras were turned off, and we hung around in the space for a few minutes. I spoke to the Edge, he shared that he pulled up the site sometimes and enjoyed the writing. I can’t remember what else was discussed. I felt like I was going to fall asleep. Someone snapped a few pictures, and a person I didn’t know joined me in a photo with the band. Someone handed me a cup of coffee. Hands were shaken, U2 went on their way, and I started heading back to the office, preparing to squash some more bugs.</p><div class="captioned-image-container"><figure><a class="image-link image2 is-viewable-img can-restack" href="https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!GyOI!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe9e33900-d9f3-4f41-b57d-49f8f6ede108_500x375.jpeg"><div class="image2-inset"><picture><img alt="" class="sizing-normal" sizes="100vw" src="https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!GyOI!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe9e33900-d9f3-4f41-b57d-49f8f6ede108_500x375.jpeg" srcset="https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!GyOI!,w_424,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe9e33900-d9f3-4f41-b57d-49f8f6ede108_500x375.jpeg 424w, https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!GyOI!,w_848,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe9e33900-d9f3-4f41-b57d-49f8f6ede108_500x375.jpeg 848w, https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!GyOI!,w_1272,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe9e33900-d9f3-4f41-b57d-49f8f6ede108_500x375.jpeg 1272w, https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!GyOI!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe9e33900-d9f3-4f41-b57d-49f8f6ede108_500x375.jpeg 1456w"/></picture></div></a></figure></div><p></p></div></div>
