title: '‘I Can’t Bring You Back to 2005’: PW Talks with Bryan Lee O’Malley'

<p>It’s been 22 years since the pencil of comic artist Bryan Lee O’Malley first conjured a 23-year-old, video game-obsessed underachiever named Scott Pilgrim in the graphic novel <em><a href="https://www.publishersweekly.com/9781932664089">Scott Pilgrim’s Precious Little Life</a></em> (Oni Press).</p>
<p>Five more graphic novels followed between 2004 and 2010, which was also the year that O’Malley’s creation made the leap to Hollywood with <em>Scott Pilgrim vs. the World</em>, the Edgar Wright film that instantly became a Millennial touchstone. Since then, there have been video games, a 2010 animated short, a Netflix series (which O’Malley himself helped oversee), and this year, the grand return: At the 2026 San Diego Comic-Con in July, it was announced that O’Malley would write and draw a new Scott Pilgrim comic, <em>Scott Pilgrim EX: Dawn of Metal Scott,</em> to coincide with the latest video game—his first comic starring his signature character since 2016.</p>
<p>O’Malley hasn’t been frozen in amber since then: His comic series <em>Snotgirl</em>, which began in 2017 and has been published irregularly since then, is a collaboration with artist Leslie Hung that earned O’Malley a nomination for the Joe Shuster Award the year it debuted. But as O’Malley prepares to come full circle, <em>PW </em>spoke with him about how he’s drawing from both past and present.</p>
<p>It's been 16 years since you've done a Scott Pilgrim comic. What brought you back, and why now?</p>
<p>I kind of backed into it, because I was working on this [video] game, and they were just like, “Why don't you do a comic to promote the release?” I said yes, because when I’m working on a big project like that, I’ll do anything for the project. I wasn't thinking about how it would feel aesthetically or emotionally for me—I just figured it would be a flimsy little promo thing. But the second I started writing it and drawing it, I realized it had more weight than that.</p>
<p>What did you realize once you started working on the characters again?</p>
<p>It's a process I had done for years of my life that I'd lost touch with. I decided to do it very similar to the way I used to do it. I use the computer to pencil the pages now, but I broke down the script the same way I used to, and I started inking on boards the same way I used to. It just felt like Scott Pilgrim—like resuming something without any time having passed.</p>
<p>Of course, time <em>has </em>passed, and when you first started the Scott Pilgrim series you weren’t all that far from Scott’s own age. Now it’s two decades later, but Scott and Ramona are still in their twenties. Could you still reconnect with them the way you used to?</p>
<p>It's a good question. I had to time travel back to Toronto in the 2000s a little bit. I don't live there, and I haven’t been back for any extended period of time since about 2015. So it's more a Toronto of the mind at this point. I wrote the anime series we did, <a href="https://ew.com/scott-pilgrim-takes-off-review-netflix-8404084"><em>Scott Pilgrim Takes Off</em>,</a> so I explored, in a tongue-in-cheek way, this idea of Scott Pilgrim aging. If I ever choose to do more Scott Pilgrim comics, that's what I'll have to confront—is Scott Pilgrim going to be in his mid-40s? Is that fun? I don't know.</p>
<p>Do you find that the elements of the movie or the other media have seeped into the way that you see the characters now?</p>
<p>Yeah, that's one of the big questions. During the movie, I was still writing books five and six, so I remember writing a draft of book five that felt too movie-voiced—it felt like the actors' voices were in my head a little too much. I had to reset and get back to what was in my mind. With this comic, I found a way to bring it back to my version as much as I could. I'm sure some fans will say my version sucks now, that I fell off—but I'm doing my best. It's not 2005 anymore. I can't bring you back there. All I can do is follow my own process and see what comes out.</p>
<p>At the same time, you have done other projects since then, and <a href="https://www.publishersweekly.com/pw/by-topic/industry-news/tip-sheet/article/72853-o-malley-and-hung-take-on-fashion-social-media-in-snotgirl.html">Snotgirl</a> has been very well received. Can we expect it to come out on any more reliable schedule?</p>
<p>Oh, God. It’s all my fault. I feel bad—the pandemic really slowed me down on that book. Scott Pilgrim isn't about modern culture, which maybe is part of why it persists. Snotgirl is about what's happening right now. That project is something I really struggle with, because I'm trying to make something out of all this stuff that I kind of hate—all this cultural stuff that bubbles up and grinds my gears. I grew up on the internet, but now it's this toxic thing, and I've watched all that happen in real time. So Snotgirl, in some ways, is a documentation of the [decline] of everything that I know and love.</p>
<p>Any plans to do more Scott Pilgrim comics after this, or are you hoping to close the book for good now?</p>
<p>I mean, in the world we live in, [intellectual property] is king. I backed myself into having this IP that people are still excited about, even though a lot of older fans are just like, “leave it alone, it's dead”—and I totally agree! But if I ever did come up with a story idea, it would be stupid of me not to do it. As long as I'm having fun, I think some people would read it and have fun, other people would read it and hate it, or not read it and hate it. That's just another part of the world we live in.</p>
