<p>In late October, a new allegation of rape was made against the President of the United States in <a href="https://democrats-judiciary.house.gov/sites/evo-subsites/democrats-judiciary.house.gov/files/evo-media-document/2026-10.hjc-dems-staff-report-epstein-and-trump.pdf?utm_source=www.thehandbasket.co&amp;utm_medium=referral&amp;utm_campaign=nyt-romance-columnist-fired-for-reposting-criticism-gets-the-last-word">a report</a> published by Democrats on the House Judiciary Committee. Did you know?</p>
<p>Perhaps if you read <a href="https://time.com/article/2026/10/26/house-democrats-unveil-new-details-from-trump-accuser/?utm_source=www.thehandbasket.co&amp;utm_medium=referral&amp;utm_campaign=nyt-romance-columnist-fired-for-reposting-criticism-gets-the-last-word">TIME</a>, the <a href="https://www.miamiherald.com/news/nation-world/article317782501.html?utm_source=www.thehandbasket.co&amp;utm_medium=referral&amp;utm_campaign=nyt-romance-columnist-fired-for-reposting-criticism-gets-the-last-word">Miami Herald</a>, <a href="https://www.huffpost.com/entry/beatrice-keul-donald-trump-rape-allegation_n_6adb428ce4b04787f03a7bdc?utm_source=www.thehandbasket.co&amp;utm_medium=referral&amp;utm_campaign=nyt-romance-columnist-fired-for-reposting-criticism-gets-the-last-word">HuffPost</a> or <a href="https://www.huffpost.com/entry/beatrice-keul-donald-trump-rape-allegation_n_6adb428ce4b04787f03a7bdc?utm_source=www.thehandbasket.co&amp;utm_medium=referral&amp;utm_campaign=nyt-romance-columnist-fired-for-reposting-criticism-gets-the-last-word">Newsweek</a>, you might have heard about Beatrice Keul’s allegation of “brutal” sexual assault by Donald Trump at the Plaza Hotel in 1993. You might have heard about how Trump “forcibly raped her; in the process, tearing her dress and causing bleeding and bruising on multiple parts of her body.” Otherwise, <a href="https://democrats-judiciary.house.gov/sites/evo-subsites/democrats-judiciary.house.gov/files/evo-media-document/2026-10.hjc-dems-staff-report-epstein-and-trump.pdf?utm_source=www.thehandbasket.co&amp;utm_medium=referral&amp;utm_campaign=nyt-romance-columnist-fired-for-reposting-criticism-gets-the-last-word">this blockbuster story</a> may have eluded you since many of the country’s top news outlets decided just days before the midterms to either not cover the report, or report on it without key details—like the <i>New York Times</i> did.</p>
<p>Though the worlds of romance novels and political journalism may not have an obvious nexus—though, as you’ll learn further down, they most certainly intersect—they came crashing together when the site <i>Smart Bitches, Trashy Books</i> <a href="https://smartbitchestrashybooks.com/2026/11/the-new-york-times-terminates-contract-with-romance-columnist-olivia-waite-after-bluesky-repost/?utm_source=www.thehandbasket.co&amp;utm_medium=referral&amp;utm_campaign=nyt-romance-columnist-fired-for-reposting-criticism-gets-the-last-word">posted a story</a> on November 3rd (Election Day) with the headline “The New York Times Terminates Contract with Romance Columnist Olivia Waite After Bluesky Repost.” And in the first sentence, there was my name: Waite, the story said, had been terminated “after she reposted [former Chicago Tribune Editor] Mark Jacob’s repost of Marisa Kabas’ criticism of the Times’ coverage of new rape allegations about Trump.”</p>
<p>I had emailed Patrick Healy, assistant managing editor for standards and trust at NYT, the previous week about the omission of the rape allegation in <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/26/us/democrats-special-counsel-trump-epstein.html?utm_source=www.thehandbasket.co&amp;utm_medium=referral&amp;utm_campaign=nyt-romance-columnist-fired-for-reposting-criticism-gets-the-last-word">their story about the report</a>, which also detailed new revelations about Trump’s ties to Jeffrey Epstein. I then posted a screenshot of the email <a href="https://bsky.app/profile/marisakabas.bsky.social/post/3myufsyikq223?utm_source=www.thehandbasket.co&amp;utm_medium=referral&amp;utm_campaign=nyt-romance-columnist-fired-for-reposting-criticism-gets-the-last-word">to Bluesky</a>. A much larger conversation about the omission ensued, with other journalists — including Jacob — weighing in. After multiple follow ups, Healy eventually replied on Friday, October 30th defending his outlet’s coverage. A few hours after posting screenshots of his response to Bluesky, Waite was fired from the gig she’d had for six years.</p>
<p>As soon as I heard the news, I reached out to Waite to get a better understanding of what happened and to see how she was feeling. We didn’t know each other before, but she graciously agreed to chat. Instead of telling you what she said, however, I figured it’d be much more interesting to hear from the writer herself—and give her back some of the column inches she’s been denied moving forward.</p>

<p>At the start of all this — “this” being “losing my contract as a romance fiction columnist for the New York Times Book Review after a Bluesky repost about the paper’s lack of coverage of rape allegations against President Trump” — Marisa asked me a pointed question: Had reposting her critique of the NYT felt like a risk at the time?</p>
<p>The <a href="https://bsky.app/profile/marisakabas.bsky.social/post/3mz4theh3nk23?utm_source=www.thehandbasket.co&amp;utm_medium=referral&amp;utm_campaign=nyt-romance-columnist-fired-for-reposting-criticism-gets-the-last-word">original post</a> included screenshots of an email exchange between Marisa and NYT editor Patrick Healy, responding to a question about why the Times had not used the word “rape” in their story about a <a href="https://democrats-judiciary.house.gov/sites/evo-subsites/democrats-judiciary.house.gov/files/evo-media-document/2026-10.hjc-dems-staff-report-epstein-and-trump.pdf?utm_source=www.thehandbasket.co&amp;utm_medium=referral&amp;utm_campaign=nyt-romance-columnist-fired-for-reposting-criticism-gets-the-last-word">newly-released report</a> from the House Judiciary Democrats that included new allegations of rape against Trump. Mark Jacob, a former long-time editor at the Chicago Tribune, had commented that Healy’s response in which he defended the Times’ decision to Marisa was “unacceptable”—and I agreed. Using the word “rape” seemed the bare minimum requirement of coverage on the report.</p>
<p>While this topic may seem outside my area of coverage, I’d argue the complete opposite. Romance authors are accustomed to being precise when writing about sex — and rape in particular is a significant facet of the genre’s history and discourse. The original mass-market bodice-rippers in the 1970s often featured scenes of sexual assault or outright rape, and ever since the genre has continued to explore how desire and its expression intertwine with political and social power.</p>
<p>There are books that eroticize a hero’s control and violence (Johanna Lindsey, classically, but also modern dark romance and a ton of romantasy) as well as books that speak against this, valuing empathy and mutual tenderness in practical, physical ways (<a href="https://www.courtneymilan.com/the-governess-affair/?utm_source=www.thehandbasket.co&amp;utm_medium=referral&amp;utm_campaign=nyt-romance-columnist-fired-for-reposting-criticism-gets-the-last-word">The Governess Affair</a> by Courtney Milan.) Many of the books I featured in the column foreground contexts where sexual consent is imperfect or impossible: historical eras where marital rape is not a crime, but consensual sex between men is, or speculative worlds where magic or science influence the course of relationships.</p>
<p>The romance genre allows for a lot of nuance on how readers respond to scenes of sexual violence — we use terms like “noncon” (short for “nonconsensual sex,” with the related <i>dubcon</i> for “dubious consent”) or “forced seduction” (if you’re an elder millennial who remembers romance forums on the internet circa 2006.) These are terms for fiction; they do not apply to real life rape, alleged or otherwise. To see a journalistic piece making the same demurral about an alleged crime was wildly inappropriate and, in my view, worth calling out. Hence: The repost.</p>
<p>It should have felt risky to repost the criticism, even nested as it was. But in the lead-up to the hotly-anticipated midterms after such an anxious few years, this fear barely registered. It didn’t feel nearly as risky as reviewing a trans romance in my <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2020/08/07/books/review/summer-romance-novels-vanessa-riley.html?unlocked_article_code=1.Q1E.qLXs.NPSmQinzQ-T0&amp;smid=url-share&amp;utm_source=www.thehandbasket.co&amp;utm_medium=referral&amp;utm_campaign=nyt-romance-columnist-fired-for-reposting-criticism-gets-the-last-word">very first Times column</a> six years ago.</p>
<p>This was in August of 2020. The now-infamous <a href="https://harpers.org/a-letter-on-justice-and-open-debate/?utm_source=www.thehandbasket.co&amp;utm_medium=referral&amp;utm_campaign=nyt-romance-columnist-fired-for-reposting-criticism-gets-the-last-word">Harper’s letter</a> had been published the month prior, framing progressives on the left as the primary threat to freedom of speech in modern American institutions. The pandemic closures were in full effect in Seattle where I lived, and the Capitol Hill Autonomous Zone established as a protest against police brutality had just been dissolved. Pamela Paul, now <a href="https://glaad.org/gap/pamela-paul/?utm_source=www.thehandbasket.co&amp;utm_medium=referral&amp;utm_campaign=nyt-romance-columnist-fired-for-reposting-criticism-gets-the-last-word">well-known for her anti-trans views</a>, was still leading the Book Review, though the most poisonous of her essays were still to be written.</p>
<p>The Times had also recently burnished the reputation of a journalist who had expressed similar views to Paul — Katie Herzog of Seattle alt-weekly <i>The Stranger</i>. An article in the Style section presented Herzog as a victim of cancel culture after <i>Stranger</i> readers vociferously objected to <a href="https://www.transgendermap.com/issues/topics/media/katie-herzog/?utm_source=www.thehandbasket.co&amp;utm_medium=referral&amp;utm_campaign=nyt-romance-columnist-fired-for-reposting-criticism-gets-the-last-word">her largely unsubstantiated piece</a> about the ex-trans movement.</p>
<p>I’d been reading <i>The Stranger</i> regularly since the 1990s, long before I was out as bisexual; and like many, I saw Herzog’s article as a profound betrayal of that paper’s queer history and readership — both of which were associated with the same neighborhood streets where protestors fended off tear gas canisters with <a href="https://www.seattlemet.com/news-and-city-life/how-to-livestream-seattle-protests/?utm_source=www.thehandbasket.co&amp;utm_medium=referral&amp;utm_campaign=nyt-romance-columnist-fired-for-reposting-criticism-gets-the-last-word">a rainbow of umbrellas</a>. John McDermott’s piece in the Times included a source comparing Herzog to Galileo — someone punished by orthodoxy for stating a scientific truth. Herzog was also a signer of the Harper’s letter, along with her future podcast co-host Jesse Singal, a media figure lionized by proponents of anti-trans sentiment and policy.</p>
<p>This kind of recounting can feel conspiratorial, I know. But it’s the water in which we swam in the summer of 2020, when I sent the Times a piece praising a trans second-world fantasy romance (<a href="https://www.maypetersonbooks.com/books/the-immortal-city/?utm_source=www.thehandbasket.co&amp;utm_medium=referral&amp;utm_campaign=nyt-romance-columnist-fired-for-reposting-criticism-gets-the-last-word">The Immortal City</a> by May Peterson) from Harlequin’s digital imprint. The book was written by a trans author, centered trans characters and queer desire, and was published in the least prestigious format from a famously unprestigious press. A lot of people who make jokes about Harlequin romances have never actually read one — just like a lot of people who say they “just have concerns” about trans people existing in public don’t know any trans people in real life, or give weight to what trans writers say about their experiences.</p>
<p>While reading Peterson’s romance novel, I had also been reading through the New York Times’ <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/editorial-standards/ethical-journalism.html?utm_source=www.thehandbasket.co&amp;utm_medium=referral&amp;utm_campaign=nyt-romance-columnist-fired-for-reposting-criticism-gets-the-last-word">ethical guidelines and editorial standards</a> which were linked in my freelance contract. They are aimed primarily at Times employees in the News and Opinion pages, and there is a lot in there about remaining neutral and non-partisan. It’s the same pose of objectivity which we would see publicly debated in October 2020, when the BBC asked its reporters to avoid attending events centered around “contested issues” (widely interpreted by <a href="https://www.theguardian.com/media/2020/oct/29/bbc-no-bias-rules-prevent-staff-joining-lgbt-pride-protests?utm_source=www.thehandbasket.co&amp;utm_medium=referral&amp;utm_campaign=nyt-romance-columnist-fired-for-reposting-criticism-gets-the-last-word">BBC staff</a> as referring to Pride marches and specifically trans rights and Black Lives Matter protests.) <a href="https://www.theguardian.com/media/2020/oct/30/bbc-boss-clarifies-rules-for-staff-attending-pride-marches?utm_source=www.thehandbasket.co&amp;utm_medium=referral&amp;utm_campaign=nyt-romance-columnist-fired-for-reposting-criticism-gets-the-last-word">The Guardian</a> wrote that internally the BBC was attempting to recruit a more diverse slate of reporters, but also was being castigated for progressivism by conservative politicians — this conflict “can lead to the corporation trying to recruit more LGBT staff or people of colour to its news division, while at the same time stopping them for speaking out on issues that directly affect their lives in case it is perceived to be biased.”</p>
<p>The NYT’s guidelines similarly specify that staff members and journalists “may not campaign for, demonstrate for, or endorse candidates, ballot causes or efforts to enact legislation … As far as possible, freelance contributors to The Times, while not its employees, should be held to the same standards as staff members in connection with their Times work.” The phrase <i>as far as possible</i> leaves a lot of room for interpretation. The guidelines specifically address the position of “freelance contributors who are not journalists but who provide a useful perspective to readers by virtue of their experience or professional specialty … By definition, these contributors may be writing about matters in which they have a personal stake, in a way The Times would not normally allow for professional journalists.” In theory, this was my role, and there is flexibility here. Yet immediately after hinting at this flexibility, the guidelines repeat the call for neutrality: “Strident partisan advocacy or offensive personal attacks risk undercutting a journalist’s credibility.”</p>
<p>When in doubt, the guidelines say: Ask the Times to make the decision, but odds are we’ll say no: ”A staff member with any doubts about a proposed political activity should consult the standards editor … Though The Times will consider matters case by case, it will be exceedingly cautious before permitting an exception.”</p>
<p>The current assistant managing editor for Standards and Trust who would likely make such a decision is Patrick Healy, a character in the Bluesky repost saga.</p>
<p>The vague detachment of these guidelines was immediately untenable for me, both personally and professionally. I have been a romance reader for four decades, and an author and reviewer for two: Believe me when I tell you that absolutely nobody turns to romance for neutrality. It is a genre that depends entirely upon passionate commitment and a willingness to take chances in the pursuit of happiness. I knew the moment I signed that contract that highlighting marginalized voices in romance could be interpreted as a violation of those guidelines. My very existence as a queer writer was partisan, even before I began writing.</p>
<p>So, I thought, why pretend neutrality at all? That first column not only reviewed trans author May Peterson, but also a historical romance novel by Black author Vanessa Riley, a contemporary romance by Latina author Alexis Daria, and Rosie Danaan’s debut featuring a hero who is a sex worker. These were mostly established authors and favorites of mine for their skill, but collectively, these choices were a statement. I figured that I should make it clear from the start where I stood, and let the Times editors decide whether or not that position aligned with their values.</p>
<p>Six complicated years passed.</p>
<p>I chose what books to review in each column by picking what sounded exciting, talking to publicists and authors and combing NetGalley listings like a hungry dragon raking claws through a hoard of gems. The pandemic continued apace, and romance was exploding in both traditional publishing and indie author spaces, which meant, among other rising trends, far more trans authors and queer romances to choose from. Not long ago, you could name all the sapphic romances of any given year on one hand; now I’d lose track if I wasn’t keeping lists. It felt like a gift to be a queer critic able to celebrate this flourishing.</p>
<p>It was busy, more than anything, and in the tumult — and as the second Trump administration ran roughshod over democratic norms — I learned to ignore the background radiation of “this isn’t neutral and therefore isn’t safe.” I went to protests; I donated items to Romancing the Runoff and Romancing the Vote, romance author-led charity auctions to support progressive voting rights organizations; I joined Authors Against Book Bans to fight censorship in public and school libraries. All of these actions could have served as cause to lose my contract with the Times. The stance I took in the column was comparatively safe: I wasn’t being teargassed or shot or disappeared by masked unidentified agents. I was simply showcasing some very good books by authors whose voices were routinely ignored or outright attacked in other sections of the paper. It wasn’t a secret agenda — how could it be secret from the Times if the Times were the ones printing it?</p>
<p>And then, just before Election Day, my contract was terminated via email — with no mention of any guideline violations. It merely included phrases about my “views” about the New York Times itself, presumably, though not explicitly stated as expressed by my Bluesky timeline (which the Times had apparently been reading.)</p>
<p>There is a joke among writers that we’re all on watchlists for our alarming plot-based search histories. “How long does it take someone to die of a gunshot wound?”; “How much blood is in the human body?” Romance authors have given this imagined surveiller a name, NSA Brad, and he even makes a cameo in Alyssa Cole’s <a href="https://alyssacole.com/books/when-no-one-is-watching/?utm_source=www.thehandbasket.co&amp;utm_medium=referral&amp;utm_campaign=nyt-romance-columnist-fired-for-reposting-criticism-gets-the-last-word">When No One Is Watching</a>. Now I’ve come to learn that I’ve had my own personal NSA Brad this whole time. Only instead of being employed by the government, this was someone at the New York Times.</p>
<p>Of course, under my contract, the Times holds the copyright to all those romance columns. If I cannot criticize the Times except in the column, then I cannot make any critique of the Times that the Times does not own. That’s a lot of my voice to give up for a freelance role where I’m not even eligible for health insurance.</p>
<p>I know my editor has been reading my column — I’ve got the edits to prove it — but given events, maybe she was the only one? There are two possibilities, equally ridiculous. One: that everyone else at the Times has been ignoring what their romance critic has been writing in their own paper because it cannot possibly be important or political. Or two: that the New York Times gives more weight to what their romance critic says on Bluesky than what she’s been saying in their pages for six years.</p>
