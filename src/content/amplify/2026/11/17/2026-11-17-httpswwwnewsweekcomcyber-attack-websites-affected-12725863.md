<p>Hackers have planted malicious software on some of the world's most popular websites, potentially exposing visitors to the theft of their personal and financial information.</p>
<p>The attack affected websites belonging to 178 organizations, including Zoom, CNN, HSBC, the BBC, IKEA, Salesforce, Equifax, Fox News, Alaska Airlines and GitLab, according to researchers at cybersecurity firm Report URI who identified the attack on Monday.</p>
<p>Hackers injected malicious JavaScript, a common computer language, into scripts hosted by Optimizely, a company that provides software allowing businesses to test different versions of websites.</p>
<p>The malicious JavaScript was designed to steal credit card details, redirect cryptocurrency payments and target some iPhones and iPads.</p>
<p>Optimizely said in a status update on its website on Tuesday that "an unauthorized party exploited a vulnerability" in its Experimentation platform. "We've fixed the vulnerability and removed the injected code from affected projects," it said.</p>

<h2>How Did the Cyber Attack Happen?</h2>
<p>Rather than hacking into 178 organizations separately, <a href="https://www.newsweek.com/how-cyberattacks-on-u-s-infrastructure-are-reshaping-security-strategies-12266643">the attackers exploited </a>a vulnerability in Optimizely's website testing service, which is used across numerous major websites.</p>
<p>Companies use the service to test changes to their websites, such as different page layouts or features.</p>
<p>By tampering with the software, the hackers were able to place malicious code on websites belonging to organizations that relied on it.</p>
<figure><img src="https://assets.newsweek.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/3X2-pop-upS3-1.jpg"/></figure>
<p>This meant visitors could encounter the software simply by opening an affected website.</p>
<p>Researchers found that the malicious code was designed to carry out several types of theft.</p>
<p>One method involved changing cryptocurrency wallet addresses copied by users, potentially sending payments to the hackers instead of their intended recipients.</p>
<p>The software could also attempt to steal credit card details, access information stored in browsers and capture screenshots.</p>
<p>Some of the code targeted iPhones and iPads, attempting to exploit weaknesses in older versions of Apple's operating systems.</p>
<p>The researchers also traced approximately $7,040 in cryptocurrency payments to wallets linked to the attack on November 16, although the full financial impact is unknown.</p>
<p>Importantly, the findings do not mean that hackers broke directly into all 178 companies or <a href="https://www.newsweek.com/facestealer-north-korean-scam-12709539">successfully stole information</a> from everyone who visited their websites.</p>
<h2><strong>What Should I Do Now?</strong></h2>
<p>Anyone who visited an affected website on November 16 should be aware of the potential risks, particularly if they entered payment details or made cryptocurrency transfers.</p>
<p>However, simply opening a website does not mean information was stolen.</p>
<p>What you can do is:</p>
<ul><li><strong>Update your devices</strong>: Make sure your iPhone, iPad and other devices are running the latest available security updates.</li><li><strong>Check your bank accounts:</strong> Look for payments you do not recognize, particularly if you entered card details on an affected website.</li><li><strong>Review cryptocurrency transfers</strong>: If you sent cryptocurrency, check that it went to the correct wallet address.</li><li><strong>Watch for suspicious account activity</strong>: Be alert to unexpected login notifications or changes to your online accounts.</li><li><strong>Contact your bank if necessary</strong>: If you notice an unauthorized payment, report it immediately.</li></ul>
<p>Optimizely said it had addressed the vulnerability and removed the malicious code.</p>
<p>However, investigators have not established how many people may have been affected.</p>
