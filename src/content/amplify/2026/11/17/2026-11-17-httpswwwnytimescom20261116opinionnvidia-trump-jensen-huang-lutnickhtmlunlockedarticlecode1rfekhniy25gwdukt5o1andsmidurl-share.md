title: The Nvidia Scandal Reveals an Administration Willing to Sell America Out
---
<figure><img alt="Donald Trump and Jensen Huang shaking hands outside." src="https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/11/16/multimedia/16goldberg-zplt/16goldberg-zplt-articleLarge.jpg"/><figcaption> Jonathan Ernst/Reuters</figcaption></figure><p>Ever since Donald Trump returned to the White House, flanked by a phalanx of supplicating tech billionaires, his administration has emphasized the need to win the A.I. race with China. Trump and his cronies have claimed repeatedly that we can’t constrain A.I. development because America needs to maintain an advantage over its chief geopolitical rival.</p><p>Yet the Nvidia scandal has revealed that the administration at best turned a blind eye when a leading American company gave an enormous boost to China’s A.I. industry, all while Trump and his cronies were profiting from it. Though the scandal started with sex — more precisely, the revelation that Jensen Huang, head of the company at the center of the A.I. boom, had an affair with a woman believed to be a Chinese spy — it is really about the administration’s utterly careless attitude toward American security. And it demonstrates the bad faith behind its efforts to shove A.I. down our collective throats.</p><p>To understand why Trump’s tech policy has been such a national betrayal, it’s worth going back to Joe Biden’s administration. Ben Buchanan, who served as Biden’s White House special adviser for A.I., told me that America’s A.I. policy under Biden had two, interconnected pillars: slowing China down, and making sure that American A.I. was developed responsibly.</p><p>“It was our conviction that it would not be possible, or it would be much harder, to domestically regulate our companies if we were neck and neck with China,” said Buchanan, the author, along with his former colleague Tantum Collins, of an extremely well-timed new book about the global battle for A.I. supremacy, “The Bitter Struggle: Superintelligence, Superpowers, and the Fate of the World.” By denying China the chips that underpin advanced A.I. — most of which are made by Nvidia, and which China cannot yet replicate — the United States would try to maintain some control over the pace of A.I. growth.</p><p>When Donald Trump took over, he set about dismantling efforts to rein in A.I. On the first day of his second term, he <a href="https://www.axios.com/pro/tech-policy/2025/01/21/trump-rolls-back-biden-ai-executive-order">revoked</a> Biden’s executive order requiring the developers of frontier models to share the results of safety testing with the government. His administration has tried to <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/03/20/us/politics/white-house-unveils-ai-policy-aimed-at-blocking-state-laws.html">block</a> state-level A.I. regulation and limit the liability of A.I. developers should their systems harm people. When the N.A.A.C.P. filed a lawsuit accusing Elon Musk’s xAI of polluting Black neighborhoods in Mississippi with the makeshift power plants fueling two of his data centers, the Department of Justice <a href="https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/jun/16/elon-musk-xai-datacenters-trump-administration">intervened</a> on Musk’s behalf.</p><p>Often, the rationale for these policies was the A.I. arms race with China. “There is a SICK conspiracy going on against AI and Data Centers, and the only one that is happy about it is China,” Trump wrote on Truth Social in September. In response to mounting panic about A.I. escaping human control, Trump called the worriers “negative forces” and insisted that America had to retain its competitive edge. “Whoever wins AI, wins,” he said.</p><p>But even as Trump was using China as a pretext to undermine A.I. regulation, his administration tacitly enabled Nvidia as it helped Chinese enterprises dodge American restrictions on powerful chips.</p><p>Initially, it seemed that Trump would continue Biden’s hawkish attitude toward chip exports to China. At his confirmation hearing, Howard Lutnick, Trump’s commerce secretary, promised to crack down on China’s evasion of American export controls. He later attacked companies attempting to profit from China’s A.I. industry: “We have had enough of people trying to make a dollar supporting the people who seek to destroy our way of life,” he said. In April, Trump restricted the sale of Nvidia’s H20 chips to China.</p><p>But in the late spring or early summer of 2025, Buchanan pointed out, Trump’s posture flipped. In July, he lifted the restrictions on the H20 chips. Then, in December, he allowed the Nvidia to sell much more powerful H200 chips to China. “The decision is a major win for Jensen Huang. Nvidia’s chief executive, who spent months lobbying the White House to ease its export restrictions,” The New York Times <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2025/12/08/business/trump-nvidia-chips-china.html">reported</a>.</p><p>We now know that by this time, Trump had already received his first briefing about Huang’s affair with Alice Huang. That apparently didn’t stop him from letting Nvidia shape American policy on chip exports. Trump’s policy evolution coincided with Nvidia’s $25 million donation to his beloved ballroom in October 2025. Further, as The Washington Post <a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/business/2026/11/16/trumps-stake-nvidia-grew-much-62-million-before-scandal-broke-out/">reported</a> on Monday, Trump held $13 million to $62 million in Nvidia shares as of the end of August, a considerable increase from his holdings in 2024.</p><p>Beyond selling chips to China directly, we now know that Nvidia has sold billions of dollars of chips to cutout companies in Southeast Asia that either provide China with computing power or smuggle equipment into the country. One such company, Megaspeed, was run by Alice Huang. On Sunday, The Wall Street Journal <a href="https://www.wsj.com/tech/jensen-huang-backdoor-ai-data-centers-a5e4499d">reported</a> that Megaspeed “arranged to send nearly $12 billion worth of top-of-the-line Nvidia chips to companies in China between March 2024 and June 2025.”</p><p>Lutnick, despite his belligerent rhetoric at the start of Trump’s term, has been extraordinarily accommodating of both Nvidia and Megaspeed. “Within the Commerce Department, Nvidia is treated differently than other firms, current and former officials say, with its issues given high priority and sent through specific channels,” <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/12/us/politics/lutnick-nvidia-commerce-china-chips.html">reported</a> The Times. According to <a href="https://www.axios.com/2026/11/11/bessent-lutnick-megaspeed-nvidia-china-chips">Axios</a>, Lutnick refused requests from others in the administration to add Megaspeed to a list of companies that pose national security concerns.</p><p>Lutnick’s about-face on chip exports may have simply been his attempt to do Trump’s bidding. But it’s worth noting that, <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/15/us/politics/trump-lutnick-ai-chips-nvidia-cantor-fitzgerald.html">according</a> to The Times, Cantor Fitzgerald, a company once led by Lutnick and now run by his sons, “has had business dealings with several data center companies in Southeast Asia that cater to Chinese clients, and could be affected by the regulations Mr. Lutnick is drafting.” (On Monday, in response to the growing Nvidia scandal, the administration finally took steps to <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/16/us/politics/trump-china-ai-chip-loophole.html">cut off</a> China’s access to American A.I. chips.)</p><p>Those who believe that Nvidia should sell more chips to China sometimes argue that America will be better off if China depends on our chips rather than developing its own. But as Buchanan points out, China is already trying hard to build its own chip industry — it’s just failing. His book cites U.S. government data concluding that China could make only 200,000 chips in 2025. If Alice Huang facilitated the sale of $12 billion in advanced Nvidia chips to Chinese companies, said Buchanan, that could be more than all the chips produced by China last year, which are of much lower quality than Nvidia’s.</p><p>“The scale of the smuggling and diversion and remote access is almost single-handedly propping up the Chinese A.I. ecosystem,” Chris McGuire, a senior fellow for China and emerging technologies at the Council on Foreign Relations, told me. It’s the difference, he said, “between us having a six-month lead in A.I. and a two-year lead in A.I., and it’s all because they’ve been permitted to have this kind of unfettered access to U.S. chips.”</p><p>Remember who gave them that access the next time Trump or one of his billionaire friends says we can’t curb A.I. lest China catch up to us.</p><p><em>The Times is committed to publishing </em><a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2019/01/31/opinion/letters/letters-to-editor-new-york-times-women.html"><em>a diversity of letters</em></a><em> to the editor.
