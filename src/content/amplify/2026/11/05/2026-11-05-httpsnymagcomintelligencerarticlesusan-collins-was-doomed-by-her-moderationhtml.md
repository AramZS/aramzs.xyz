<p>All summer and fall, Susan Collins’s campaign dotted Maine’s roads with red signs bearing the self-assured slogan, “Susan Collins: Our Senator.”</p>
<p>Indeed, Collins has been senator for a very long time, first winning office after a 1996 campaign in which she promised that, if elected, she’d serve only two terms. “Twelve years is long enough to be in public service and make a contribution,” a 43-year-old Collins said at the time. “Then come home and let someone else take your place.”</p>
<p>Now 73, Collins will be coming home to Maine in January. Her place in Washington will be taken by Troy Jackson, a union man, logger and former president of the state senate, who beat her soundly on Tuesday night. The response has been ecstatic verging on disbelief, so long has Collins been a fixture in Maine’s political firmament.</p>
<p>“OMG I don’t have words,” said Deqa Dhalac, former state legislator and mayor of South Portland, by text early Wednesday morning. “I believed in Troy’s vision and I was asking myself: is Maine gonna listen this time and send Susan to retire?”</p>
<p>One person who worked on Chellie Pingree’s 2002 campaign to unseat Collins told me how, 24 years ago, there had been an understanding about the fleeting opportunity of beating a senator during their first reelection bid: <em>“You have to beat her now,</em>” he remembered being told,<em> “or she’ll wind up there for 30 years.” </em>Pingree, running in the wake of 9/11, had not been able to take Collins out, and the 30-year prophecy came true.</p>
<p>As the news of her long-awaited defeat spread through the Portland watch party late on Tuesday, Pingree herself — who had just won her tenth term in Congress, and was celebrating the victory of her daughter Hannah as the state’s new governor — described how it felt to watch the people around her, many in “Solidarity” t-shirts, hugging each other. “This is a sea change for Maine,” said Pingree, “and people will spend many hours breaking down how something this dramatic could happen.”</p>
<p>Amidst all the history made on Tuesday night, Collins’s defeat feels momentous in a very specific way: a blaring signal of the desire for generational change, the rejection of purported moderation as some inherent political virtue, and an electrifying reminder that people at the bottom can still find a way to wrest power back from those at the top.</p>
<p>The Maine result is shocking because Collins has cultivated an aura of vampiric invincibility, a reputation rooted mostly in the 8.6 percent margin she posted in 2020 after trailing Democrat Sara Gideon in polls leading up to Election Day. That surprise win — in the same cycle in which Maine voters chose Joe Biden over Donald Trump by 9 points — burnished Maine voters’ grim belief that this woman would rule in deathless perpetuity.</p>
<p>But prior to 2020, Collins’s victories weren’t supernatural feats; she won repeatedly because a lot of people liked her. A startling number of Maine Democrats, even liberal Democrats, have voted for Collins in the past. This summer Troy Jackson told me that he was pretty sure he had voted for her years ago; Jackson’s predecessor on the ticket, Graham Platner, has said that <em>he</em> had voted for her as well.</p>
<p>“It’s hard to explain to people outside of Maine,” the writer Shay Stewart Bouley said, “<em>Why did you all keep voting for her?</em> But I moved here as a Black woman from Chicago 24 years ago and I asked around, and everybody rocked with her back then.”</p>
<p>Collins was hailed as the last of a dying breed, a throw-back to the misty memories of bipartisanship. Now the last Republican Senator left in New England, and a practitioner of a kind of politics often described as moderate, Collins was celebrated for her occasional willingness to vote across the aisle and broker compromise with her Democratic colleagues, especially the <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2013/10/15/us/senate-women-lead-in-effort-to-find-accord.html">women</a>. In 2015, a Morning Consult poll pegged Collins’s approval rating at 78 percent, making her the second most popular senator, behind only Bernie Sanders. As strange as that may sound, she was understood to share a quality with the senator from Vermont: independence.</p>
<p>Independence is important to Maine, a state where a third of voters are unenrolled in either party, with a history of elected officials who defied their parties’ leadership to take stands they believed in. Collins’s role model was four-term Republican senator Margaret Chase Smith, the first woman ever to serve in both houses of Congress, and an odd duck who supported much of the New Deal, advocated for the U.S. to get into Vietnam and to use nuclear weapons against the Soviet Union, voted for the establishment of Medicare and Medicaid, <em>and</em> delivered a legendary 15-minute “Declaration of Conscience” address against McCarthyism.</p>
<p>To be persuasively independent, in the spirit of Smith, is to possess a set of values, however zany or mismatched they may be. Collins’s early career showed flashes of ideological commitments, especially to abortion and gay rights. The beginning of her end was her fealty to Trump, a president she <a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/opinions/gop-senator-why-i-cannot-support-trump/2016/08/08/821095be-5d7e-11e6-9d2f-b1a3564181a1_story.html">proclaimed</a> she would not support, then whose bidding she mostly did.</p>
<p>But independence is different from moderation, and <em>moderation</em> was much more Collins’s calling card, the thing that she was no longer capable of plausibly performing. Tom Allen, a former Democratic Congressman who ran against Collins and lost in 2008, said, “Five terms — thirty years — is a problem, especially for someone trying to walk down the middle in this political environment. It’s much more polarized than when I ran against her.”</p>
<p>Polarization implies equivalent moves toward the margins, but most of the movement toward a radical edge has thus far come from Collins’s Republican party, which has grown increasingly open in its bigotry and drive toward authoritarian control. Collins’s former Senate colleague and longtime rival, Olympia Snowe, understood this and got out while the getting was good, retiring in 2013. It is perhaps the ultimate indignity for Collins that Snowe remains a respected figure in Maine, while Collins will be remembered as a politician who steadily nudged her moral center closer to her party’s ever more acidic resentments.</p>
<p>This is what made her brand of moderation untenable. It was advertised as a form of political collegiality, an impulse to compromise in the service of functional government, but as the years wore on, and her party became ever more hellbent in its plan to strip Americans of voting rights, reproductive care, and healthcare, it grew harder to pretend that civility had anything to do with it, or that there was anything moderate or functional about finding middle ground with a party that had a state police force kidnap people and put them in concentration camps, and in July murdered a father in her own state.</p>
<p>“Susan Collins may be the same,” said Bouley, “but politics has changed, and Maine has changed.” She noted rising costs, the ever-widening gap between rich and poor, and demographic changes, both the blue-tilting urbanites moving in since COVID, and the growth of a Somali immigrant population. “Yes, Maine is still predominantly white,” said Bouley, “but it went from 98 percent white to 92 percent white and that’s a big deal with such a small population. Not only does Collins not represent those communities, she hasn’t made any effort to connect with them in any way.”</p>
<p>During this campaign, Collins’ veneer of civility slipped. As the conservative outlet “The Maine Wire” stoked racist fears of voter fraud by Somali Americans, Collins commented that “only Americans should be voting in American elections” and cheerfully posed, with Joe Manchin, alongside a prop sword that a conservative journalist had recently wielded outside a Lewiston mosque.</p>
<p>For Collins, moderation was perhaps never about finding some moral middle ground between political poles. Rather, hers was a politics of deference to hierarchies in which no ideas are better than other ideas, and the highest calling is simply the preservation of entrenched systems through an ideologically blank commitment to “working across the aisle.”</p>
<p>What Collins seems to stand for most intensely is the protection of institutional power. She voted to acquit both Bill Clinton and Donald Trump of impeachment charges when they were president, only voting to convict Trump after January 6, when he was not in office. She proudly argued that she made decisions on judicial and cabinet appointments, including Supreme Court Justice Brett Kavanaugh, based not on ideology but on a candidate’s qualifications and her belief that presidents had the right to select the people they wanted to select by dint of being presidents. She excused her worst sleights of hand — in which she would vote an egregious appointee or piece of legislation out of the committees where she had power, then vote against them when on the floor where her vote didn’t matter — with claims about the propriety of the full senate body being able to deliberate.</p>
<p>But passive commitment to simply letting those in power wield it without meaningful check or balance, to preserving the institutional norms and authority for which she had such reverence, ironically hastened the corrosion of those institutions. The destruction of everything from Medicaid to the Supreme Court to USAID to the Kennedy Center to the White House itself leaves old-timey institutionalists with no institutions left to defend. They all failed so profoundly that the moderate approach that Collins clung to for so long no longer looks rational at all.</p>
<p>In her last desperate bid to retain her seat, Collins revealed more than she should have about her sneering disdain for those people <em>without</em> institutional power or resumes for her to evaluate. She was open in mocking Jackson’s socioeconomic background, kicking off her campaign against him with a cruel aside about the mother of his children being his second cousin. And at the end, it was her deployment of retired Maine broadcaster Bill Green — long a cozy, moderate presence himself, who in 2020 cut an ad about Collins that many credit with saving her skin — to drop the hammer on Jackson, by noting that he’s the kind of guy you’d call if you needed a cord of wood, unqualified for the complex work of senatoring.</p>
<p>Green’s ad may have again sealed the deal in this election, this time in reverse. It exposed the emptiness and elitism of those whose snug, homey moderation was perhaps <em>never</em> about values or belief in anything other than money and power. A view in which simply being “our senator” was argument enough to justify remaining a senator.</p>
<p>On Tuesday, those people — the loggers and the immigrants, the ones whose hospitals are closing, who cannot even imagine the kinds of dollars that have flowed through Collins’s <a href="https://www.propublica.org/article/fbi-susan-collins-navatek-campaign-donations-investigation">campaigns coffers</a> in her thirty years in office — found in themselves a willingness to do what Collins never could: to change the shape of who has seniority and authority, to sacrifice their senator’s power in order to get their own back.</p>
