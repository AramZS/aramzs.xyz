<p><a href="https://www.infohawk.com/news"> All news</a> </p>
<p>InfoHawk research has uncovered a network of 820 suspicious restaurant domains impersonating major US chains, including Jersey Mike's, Jimmy John's, Firehouse Subs, Chipotle, and Dave's Hot Chicken. Some are running sophisticated payment-skimming attacks that capture credit card information and ask customers for their bank's SMS verification codes.</p>
<figure><img alt="Copycat homepages: a Jersey Mike's look-alike (left) and a Dave's Hot Chicken look-alike (right)" src="https://www.infohawk.com/_app/immutable/assets/hero.BVcKpwn9.jpg"/><figcaption>Source: jarrysaymlckess[.]delivery and dawechlckhort[.]delivery</figcaption></figure>
<p>When customers order food online, they expect a familiar experience: find their restaurant, choose their meal, and pay. Increasingly, criminals are exploiting that familiarity by creating convincing clones of restaurant ordering websites, right down to the original site's underlying code.</p>
<p>In early November 2026, InfoHawk researchers investigated a collection of suspicious websites that appear to be part of one or more coordinated restaurant impersonation and payment-skimming operations.</p>
<p>Across two research passes, we identified 820 domains impersonating 35 restaurant brands. Of those, 808 use the <code>.delivery</code> top-level domain, and the rest use <code>.com</code> or <code>.shop</code>.</p>
<p>The scale is concerning, but what makes these sites particularly dangerous is how convincing they can be.</p>
<h2>These aren't ordinary fake websites</h2>
<p>The investigation began with a suspicious domain: <code>mlckessejersstey[.]delivery</code>.</p>
<p>The site impersonated Jersey Mike's, the popular sandwich chain, and reproduced its online ordering experience.</p>
<p>Rather than simply displaying a crude imitation of the restaurant's website, the attackers used a more sophisticated approach: reverse-proxying the legitimate site while replacing the checkout payment form with a malicious one.</p>
<p>A reverse proxy can retrieve content from a legitimate website and present it to visitors under a different domain. This allows an attacker-controlled site to display genuine pages and assets while selectively modifying the parts of the experience that matter to the attacker.</p>
<p>In this case, the malicious checkout requested credit card information and also asked customers for the SMS verification code sent by their bank.</p>
<p>This is a particularly dangerous combination. Capturing a credit card number is valuable to criminals; obtaining a one-time verification code could also help them authorize fraudulent transactions, depending on how the victim's bank uses that code.</p>
<p>For consumers, the experience can appear nearly indistinguishable from ordering directly from the restaurant. The site looks familiar, the menu and ordering flow can behave normally, and the fraudulent payment form appears at the moment a customer expects to pay.</p>
<p>Importantly, this does not require the legitimate restaurant's website to have been hacked. The attackers can operate on infrastructure they control while using the authentic restaurant website as the source of the content they display.</p>

<h2>How the attack works</h2>
<p>Each clone is a <b>reverse proxy</b>: a server that sits between the victim and the real restaurant site, fetches every page from the real site as it is requested, edits it on the way through, and passes it on. Menus, prices, store hours, and the cart are live and correct, because they come from the real site. Only the payment step is replaced. Described from mlckessejersstey[.]delivery (Jersey Mike's); we believe the clones of the other brands run the same kit.</p>
<ol> <li> <h3>The victim arrives from a search ad</h3> <p> A Google Search ad imitates the restaurant's listing and shows a look-alike domain. The domain is behind Cloudflare, so its real server is hidden, and Cloudflare gives it a valid certificate automatically (Google Trust Services here), so the browser shows "Connection is secure". </p> </li> <li> <h3>Every page is fetched live from the real site</h3> <p> The proxy forwards each request to the restaurant's own servers, adjusting the host name and cookies, and relays the answer. The real site's headers come through (on Jersey Mike's, <code>x-powered-by: Nuxt</code> and Amazon CloudFront headers), which is how we could tell which paths came from the real site and which from the scammer's server. </p> </li> <li> <h3>Addresses are rewritten to keep the victim on the fake domain</h3> <p> The proxy replaces the brand's own host names in pages, scripts, and redirects with the fake domain, and maps each backend to a path on it, such as <code>/__bapi__/</code> or <code>/__payments__/</code>. Store lookup, menus, login, and cart all work, because those calls go back through the proxy to the real APIs. Even the brand's own bot-detection script still runs, with its endpoints rewritten. </p> </li> <li> <h3>Three scripts are injected into every page</h3> <p> <b>A script blocker</b> patches <code>HTMLScriptElement.prototype.src</code> so that listed scripts never load (on Jersey Mike's, the OneTrust cookie banner and Branch, which could send phone users to the app or the real domain). <b><code>_jmu()</code></b> finds the real checkout payment frame and points it at <code>/js/index.html</code> on the fake domain; a MutationObserver puts the fake one back whenever the page restores the real one. <b><code>_chkT()</code></b> reads the order total from the "Place order" button every half second and saves it, so the fake form shows the right amount. </p> </li> <li> <h3>The card form is the scammer's</h3> <p> A few paths are answered by the scammer's server instead of the real site: <code>/js/*</code> (the card form and its scripts) and <code>/payment/socket.io</code>. The form looks like part of checkout. On submit it collects every field (card number, expiry, CVV, name, phone) and sends it, with the site name, to the operator over a live socket.io connection. </p> </li> <li> <h3>An operator gets past the bank's check live</h3> <p> We believe a person watching a control panel uses the card elsewhere and, when the bank challenges that payment, tells the victim's page what to show next: "card declined, try another", a page asking for the SMS code (<code>/js/confirmation/</code>), an "approve in your bank app" page (<code>/js/app/</code>), or "invalid code, try again". We believe the code the victim types is what lets the operator's charge through 3-D Secure. </p> </li> <li> <h3>The victim is sent away</h3> <p> When the operator marks it a success, the page sets a cookie <code>valid=77777</code> lasting until 2038 and sends the victim to another chain's real site (<code>href.li/?https://www.jimmyjohns.com/</code>; href.li hides where they came from). No order reaches the restaurant: the real payment step never runs, so there is no confirmation email and no food. We believe the victim's card is then charged for whatever the operator bought. </p> </li> </ol>
<p><b>Why this design.</b> The clone never goes out of date, the victim builds a real order with a real total before anything looks wrong, and one kit works against any restaurant by changing the target site, the domain, and a few path rules. The scammer's server does very little work, which makes it cheap to move. Some variants skip the payment wording and only pin the frame's address, re-setting it every time the page changes it.</p>

<h2>What this means for restaurant brands</h2>
<p>These clones don't need to break into a restaurant's systems. They borrow the real site, page by page, and insert the attack only at the moment a customer pays. That makes them hard to spot from the outside, but it also means the brand's own servers see every page the clone serves. Brands and their ordering providers that watch for their site loading under someone else's domain, or for checkout sessions that never reach the real payment provider, are well placed to catch these sites early.</p>
