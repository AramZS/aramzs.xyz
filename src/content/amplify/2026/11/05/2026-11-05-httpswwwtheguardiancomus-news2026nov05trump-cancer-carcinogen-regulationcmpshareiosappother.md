<h2 data-gu-name="standfirst">Natural Resources Defense Council shows how federal rules changes will spike up public’s exposure to carcinogens</h2>
<p>Amid a <a data-link-name="in body link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/society/2026/oct/06/cancer-rates-rising-more-quickly-among-younger-people-international-study-finds">surge in early onset cancer</a>, the Trump administration has taken more than 500 actions to systematically dismantle protections against carcinogens once in place across the federal government, an October<a data-link-name="in body link" href="https://www.nrdc.org/resources/trump-administrations-pro-cancer-agenda"> report alleges</a>.</p>
<p>The Natural Resources Defense Council report, and comments from current and former officials from the Environmental Protection Agency, bring into focus how myriad changes in federal rules or protocol will dramatically increase the public’s exposure to carcinogens. These are taking effect at a time when some forms of early onset cancer are spiking by about 30% per decade.</p>
<p>Some of the changes have drawn widespread outrage and backlash, including the administration delaying new protections around asbestos, or <a data-link-name="in body link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/mar/12/epa-chief-bayer-ceo-supreme-court">siding with Bayer</a> in its fight to keep the carcinogenic pesticide glyphosate on the market.</p>
<p>But most changes lie in the nuts and bolts of the federal government. These include <a data-link-name="in body link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2025/oct/21/trump-epa-toxic-chemicals">alterations to important scientific processes</a> that determine the level of risk people face in exposure to carcinogens. With a few minor adjustments, once-problematic levels of exposure to carcinogens, <a data-link-name="in body link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2025/dec/19/trump-epa-formaldehyde-protection-proposal">like formaldehyde</a>, are no longer considered a danger in the federal government’s eyes.</p>
<p>Other actions include the elimination of protective regulatory rules, the exploitation of presidential exemptions for polluters, the delay of new regulations already in motion, the elimination of research funding and wide-scale cuts to cancer research staff.</p>
<p>The actions amount to a “pro-cancer” agenda, the report states. NRDC lead author Erik Olson said he was “surprised by the breadth of the whole government effort to undermine all the protections against cancer”. It is not just the EPA, Olson said, but the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, the Nuclear Regulatory Commission, the Occupational Safety and <a data-component="auto-linked-tag" data-link-name="in body link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/society/health">Health</a> Administration, independent commissions and many other governmental entities.</p>
<p>“The vast majority of people have no idea that this is happening because the <a data-component="auto-linked-tag" data-link-name="in body link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/trump-administration">Trump administration</a> is mostly doing it without a lot of fanfare, so it’s hard to piece together this jigsaw puzzle, but we did and it shows a stark and shocking picture,” Olson said.</p>
<p>Officials estimate there will be more than 2m US cancer diagnoses this year. People younger than 50 years old have experienced a nearly 3% increase in colon cancer rates each year from 2013 to 2022. More broadly, rates for 14 types of common cancers <a data-link-name="in body link" href="https://www.nih.gov/news-events/news-releases/incidence-rates-some-cancer-types-have-risen-people-under-age-50">have jumped in recent decades</a>, including a 35% increase in childhood leukemia.</p>
<p>The situation calls for more protections, but those rules, regulations and scientific processes that are most essential have long been on industry’s hit list, said an EPA employee who declined to use their name for fear of retribution. Former industry officials are now <a data-link-name="in body link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2025/feb/05/trump-epa-lobbyists-attorneys">running the EPA</a>, and it is “horrifying to watch it all play out,” the employee said.</p>
<p>“It is coming from the top [of the agency], and they are being methodical, and doing a lot of damage to these very critical protection points, which is exactly what the chemical industry has wanted,” the employee said. “There is no way to sugarcoat what is happening.”</p>
<p>Among the most widely reverberating changes is <a data-link-name="in body link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2025/dec/19/trump-epa-formaldehyde-protection-proposal">the shift away</a> from the “no safe level of exposure” principle that guided EPA science in developing protections for carcinogens. This works by assuming any level of exposure to carcinogens presents a risk, and the risk grows as the exposure dose grows.</p>
<p>The Trump EPA has started implementing a “threshold” approach in which it determines that some exposures to carcinogens is safe. This change has already resulted in weaker protections against formaldehyde, a toxicant widely used in consumer goods. The administration appears to be <a data-link-name="in body link" href="https://www.osha.gov/hexavalent-chromium/health-effects.">taking a similar approach</a> to its regulation of hexavalent chromium, a widespread water contaminant.</p>
<p>Nuclear regulators are also <a data-link-name="in body link" href="https://www.nrdc.org/bio/matthew-mckinzie/nuclear-regulatory-commission-rewriting-rules-are-supposed-keep-nuclear-safe">eliminating the obligation</a> to reduce exposure to radioactive carcinogens to the lowest reasonably achievable level, despite decades of scientific evidence that any level of radiation poses a cancer risk, the report states.</p>
<p>David Fotouhi, the second in command at the EPA, <a data-link-name="in body link" href="https://www.propublica.org/article/trump-epa-directive-chemical-assessments">wrote in an early 2026 memo</a> that the agency had been too conservative in estimating toxic chemicals’ health risks, including carcinogenity. Those values should be revisited and loosened, Fatouhi suggested.</p>
<p>This undermines work on 500 risk assessments done since 1985 by the Integrated Risk Information System (Iris), the small EPA office that determines chemical toxicity. The changes to risk assessments are among the most pernicious Trump EPA moves because the findings underpin regulations that protect people from carcinogens in the air, water, consumer goods and elsewhere, said Betsy Southerland, a former water office director at the agency.</p>
<p>The change to the threshold approach is part of that, and the agency is also targeting a mechanism that protects children by factoring in an increased risk in their exposure to chemicals.</p>
<p>“This is a killer for any emerging contaminant work, and for [carcinogens and other chemicals] that they are revisiting,” Southerland said. The changes could also weaken cleanup requirements at Superfund sites, which is the designation for the nation’s most polluted areas, many of which contain cancer-linked substances.</p>
<p>The Trump EPA quietly loosened guidance around the type of studies required to set regulations around carcinogens, the report alleges. Long-term human or animal studies are the norm, but the agency now uses short-term modeling studies called new approach methodologies (Nams) that are known to be unreliable and easy to manipulate. A Nam study was used to weaken the risk assessment for p-DCB, a widely used consumer chemical that most regulatory agencies label a carcinogen.</p>
<p>Trump has also <a data-link-name="in body link" href="https://www.nytimes.com/video/climate/100000011186318/how-trump-rolled-back-limits-on-toxic-air-pollution.html">dramatically increased</a> the use of “presidential pardons” for carcinogenic air pollution, which the report claims will increase exposure to arsenic, ethylene oxide and similar carcinogenic chemicals.</p>
<p>Meanwhile, the agency appears to be systematically using stall tactics via multiple channels to grind the regulatory process to a halt. For example, it <a data-link-name="in body link" href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/climate-environment/2025/06/12/epa-toxic-chemicals-safety-data/">delayed</a> requirements for industry to provide cancer data for carcinogenic chemicals like benzene, bisphenol A and vinyl chloride, the building block for PVC material, which were supposed to be reviewed during the administration. It is unclear when this will happen.</p>
<p>Olson likened the situation to someone “dumping a huge dump truck full of termites on your house”.</p>
<p>“It’s not like any one termite is causing the house to collapse, but this is a totally intentional strategy of having innumerable termites eating away at the foundation of all our protections, and the house is going to collapse,” Olson said.</p>
<p>The Guardian has contacted the White House and the EPA for comment.</p>
