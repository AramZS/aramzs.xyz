<ul><li>Childhood nearsightedness is becoming more common and appearing earlier, with doctors blaming more close-up work and less time outdoors.</li><li>The earlier myopia starts the faster it progresses, raising the risk of serious eye disease and vision loss later in life.</li><li>Regular outdoor time, screen breaks and treatments such as low-dose atropine may help slow progression, though researchers say screens are only part of a complex picture.</li></ul>
<p>Neither she nor her husband needed glasses until middle age, so Cassia van der Hoof Holstein was a bit surprised to learn that her 8-year-old daughter, Viva, was struggling to see the board in class.</p>
<p>When Van der Hoof Holstein started reading on nearsightedness in children, that surprise turned into concern. Understanding of pediatric myopia had advanced dramatically since her own school years, when needing glasses seemed little more than an inconvenience.</p>
<p>Now modern life was straining the limits of human vision, at ever younger ages. More hours spent with screens close to our faces and fewer hours in natural light are changing the shape of our eyeballs, elongating the spheres from front to back.</p>
