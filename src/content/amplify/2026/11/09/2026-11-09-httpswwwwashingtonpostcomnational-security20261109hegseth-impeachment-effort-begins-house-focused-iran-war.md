<html><body><figure><img alt="" src="https://cloudfront-us-east-1.images.arcpublishing.com/wapo/3D5OJDNADLOEAHROR7QTPM6ZRA.jpg"/><figcaption>Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth arrives to meet with Australian officials at the Pentagon in September. (Kent Nishimura/AFP/Getty Images)</figcaption></figure><p>A Republican lawmaker introduced articles of impeachment against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth on Monday, in a bid to force House members to vote on whether they support the polarizing Cabinet official. </p><p>The effort, led by Rep. Thomas Massie (Kentucky), centers on Hegseth’s role in the unpopular Iran war, which top GOP officials have blamed for their crushing losses in last week’s midterm elections. </p><p>Massie has <a href="https://massie.house.gov/uploadedfiles/hegseth_articles_of_impeachment.pdf" rel="noreferrer" target="_blank" title="https://massie.house.gov/uploadedfiles/hegseth_articles_of_impeachment.pdf">alleged</a> that the war is unconstitutional, because the Trump administration launched the conflict without the approval of lawmakers and has since continued military action despite Congress <a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/politics/2026/06/23/senate-votes-block-trump-resuming-iran-war/?itid=lk_inline_manual_8" rel="noreferrer" target="_blank" title="https://www.washingtonpost.com/politics/2026/06/23/senate-votes-block-trump-resuming-iran-war/?itid=lk_inline_manual_8">demanding</a> that the Pentagon cease hostilities. </p><p>Reading the impeachment articles on the House floor, Massie alleged that Hegseth had “abused” his powers as defense secretary and had “knowingly executed and implemented unlawful orders” by carrying out strikes against Iran. The congressman said that Hegseth “will remain a threat to the constitution if allowed to remain in office.”</p><p>Introducing the impeachment articles Monday will force a vote by Wednesday evening, or House Republicans could punt the effort by either referring the resolution to the House Judiciary Committee or voting to table it. If passed, the measure will then head to the Senate, which will have to hold a trial or seek to dismiss the charges. </p><p>Massie has framed the effort as an <a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/national-security/2026/11/01/hegseth-digs-confident-democrats-plan-go-offense/" rel="noreferrer" target="_blank" title="https://www.washingtonpost.com/national-security/2026/11/01/hegseth-digs-confident-democrats-plan-go-offense/">attempt to stain Hegseth’s legacy</a>. Still, the vote is highly unlikely to result in a conviction in the Republican-controlled Senate. </p><p>The Trump administration has pushed Republicans to oppose the vote, distributing <a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/politics/2026/11/09/hegseths-team-gives-talking-points-allies-before-expected-impeachment-vote/" rel="noreferrer" target="_blank" title="https://www.washingtonpost.com/politics/2026/11/09/hegseths-team-gives-talking-points-allies-before-expected-impeachment-vote/">talking points</a> this week that argue Hegseth’s role in leading the Iran war is not impeachable and could discourage future defense secretaries from carrying out presidential orders.</p><p>In comments to reporters traveling with him in Rhode Island, Hegseth brushed off the impeachment effort. “We’re not worried about that,” the secretary said, adding that he would be addressing the situation during a news conference Tuesday morning at the Pentagon. It will be Hegseth’s first time taking questions at the Pentagon since May, despite ongoing military operations in Iran.</p><p>In a statement, Pentagon spokesperson Joel Valdez called the impeachment articles “vindictive and baseless” and said the Defense Department would be “taking the offensive” in countering the effort. </p><p>In a <a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/national-security/2026/11/06/hegseth-draws-fire-after-government-resources-used-partisan-video/" rel="noreferrer" target="_blank" title="https://www.washingtonpost.com/national-security/2026/11/06/hegseth-draws-fire-after-government-resources-used-partisan-video/">social media video</a> last week, Hegseth called Massie a “disgruntled, defeated congressman” and denounced the impeachment effort as a “sham.” </p><p>Massie is one of the most prominent anti-war Republicans and lost his primary earlier this year after President Donald Trump supported his opponent, whom Hegseth campaigned alongside in a departure from norms for a sitting defense secretary. </p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cloudfront-us-east-1.images.arcpublishing.com/wapo/MNWPJ2IJAFC3CN2ZE36BL3SYZM.jpg"/><figcaption>Rep. Thomas Massie (R-Kentucky) speaks to reporters outside the Capitol in September. (Roberto Schmidt/Getty Images)</figcaption></figure><p>Republican lawmakers have grown increasingly critical of Hegseth in recent weeks, frustrations that have only intensified after the party lost both chambers of Congress in the midterms. </p><p>Rep. James Comer (R-Kentucky), a staunch Trump ally, declined to back the defense secretary <a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/politics/2026/11/06/trump-ally-declines-back-hegseth-when-asked-whether-he-should-be-replaced/" rel="noreferrer" target="_blank" title="https://www.washingtonpost.com/politics/2026/11/06/trump-ally-declines-back-hegseth-when-asked-whether-he-should-be-replaced/">in an interview Friday with News Nation</a>, asserting that Republicans have “no idea what the strategy is moving forward in Iran.”</p><p>Speaking Monday to reporters in Paris, Secretary of State Marco Rubio, who doubles as Trump’s national security adviser, called Hegseth “very smart” and said that he has performed well in the job and acted with “professionalism.”</p><p>“I see perhaps a side of him that the media doesn’t see, and members of Congress may not see,” Rubio said.</p><p>But while the White House and Trump have publicly defended Hegseth, within the West Wing there remain questions of how strongly the new chief of staff, Stephen Miller, is willing to go to bat for Hegseth. </p><p>The departure of Miller’s predecessor, Susie Wiles, was seen as welcome news for the defense secretary — not just because she was one of Hegseth’s top detractors in the administration, but also because it fulfilled Trump’s need for a high-profile shake-up in the immediate aftermath of Republicans’ midterm trouncing, according to two people with knowledge of White House strategy. Like others interviewed for this report, they spoke on the condition of anonymity to discuss internal deliberations.</p><p>Before Tuesday’s election, Trump and his advisers had conversations about Hegseth potentially being the first to go, these people said. That involved Trump calling friends and allies to solicit their opinion on Hegseth and whether to keep him on. Trump’s increasing frustration with Wiles in the lead-up to the election, however, appears to have bought Hegseth time to continue to defend himself.</p><p>Miller and Hegseth are neighbors on the military base where both now live, and their families socialize together.</p><p>But within the White House, despite the rapport between Miller and Hegseth, the defense secretary’s impeachment and the corresponding hearings are seen as a foregone conclusion — and vociferously defending him will have limited effect on the outcome, some people around Trump say.</p><p>A person close to the president, who has spoken to Miller in recent days, said it’s not out of the question that Hegseth will, sooner than later, be “cut loose” by the White House instead of fiercely defended through the impeachment process.</p><p>The White House did not immediately respond to a request for comment.</p><p>Two former Trump administration officials who remain in contact with the White House staff said there appears to be little appetite for spending significant political capital on a protracted defense of Hegseth. These people noted that the president, so far, has not publicly attacked the impeachment process and, rather, seems to be letting it play out. </p><p>Another former administration official said that Miller’s appointment to replace Wiles does not bode well for Hegseth. Miller, this person said, will need to focus on the president’s agenda for the remaining two years of his term, not defending personalities. </p><p>“Miller knows the Senate very well, and unlike Pete, he knows where the limits are,” this person added, citing the new chief of staff’s experience working on Capitol Hill for then-Sen. Jeff Sessions, the Alabama Republican. “He knows that they need to get stuff done, and as chief of staff, this isn’t the Hill they want to die on.”</p><p><i>Joshua Keating in Paris contributed to this report.</i></p></body></html>
