title: The Internet Broke Our Taste. Will A.I. Destroy It for Good?
---
<p>In a world of slop capitalism, vibe-crafted culture and taste paranoia, why we like what we like feels harder than ever to know.</p><figure><video controls="" poster="https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/11/05/22mag-taste-88369-cover/22mag-taste-88369-cover-master768.jpg"><source src="https://vp.nyt.com/video/2026/11/05/182821_1_22mag-taste-88369_wg_1080p.mp4" type="video/mp4"/></video></figure><p>To move through A.I.-era San Francisco as a relatively normal person is to confront the sheer volume of bewildering concepts that tech has lately thrust upon us. Looking at billboards and bus ads, you gather that agentic virtual agents are here, and that something called “sharded Postgres” is here, too. You learn that inference is more than just vibes, and even more inscrutably, that ▲ ~/ npm i ai. Rather than give yourself a headache trying to parse these ciphers, you might feel tempted to ignore them entirely: not my lingo, not my problem. But a nagging dread lingers. What if all that gibberish portends irreversible changes already in the process of reshaping our lives? What if agentic agents really <em>are</em> here, and what if none of us can opt out of the future they’re ushering in?</p><figure><audio controls="" src="https://static.nytimes.com/podcasts/2026/11/13/magazine/13audm-22taste-weiner/261113-22taste-weiner-nytmag-audm.mp3"></audio><figcaption>Listen to this article, read by Ron Butler</figcaption></figure><p>Recently, lots of people who work in tech — and lots of us who don’t but feel forced to think about it anyway — have faced disquieting questions about what computers are capable of. Near the top of the list is whether they’ll achieve superintelligence, turn malevolent and bring about the apocalypse. More fun to mull, as dystopian musings go, is another newly burning question: whether computers can learn taste.</p><p>“Taste” became an unlikely tech buzzword this year, invoked and debated in essays and X posts by founders, investors, engineers and other would-be Silicon Valley oracles. We’ve long understood taste, in its simplest terms, as a capacity for judgment that certain people possess in uncommon degrees. Someone with taste can behold a made thing (a song, an outfit, a living room) and deftly evaluate its relationship to an ever-shifting set of aesthetic rules and cultural conventions. Because computers currently lack this capacity — and because much of what generative A.I. produces today is of such abysmal quality that we’ve adopted the pejorative “slop” to describe it — some in the industry have come to see taste as a sort of market differentiator for humanity itself.</p><p>They tout taste as a “new core skill.” They hail it as a “moat,” protecting us from our theoretical robot usurpers whose output will never be as “net new and resonant” as ours. Others counter that computers are pretty good at learning rules, and predict that sufficiently sophisticated models will one day cross the moat and learn to be tasteful, too. “<a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/91591873/the-remarkably-human-task-of-giving-ai-good-enough-taste">One by one,” an A.I. founder told Fast Company, “the things that we were sure were uniquely human just turn out to be patterns the models can learn.”</a></p><p>There are obvious philosophical dimensions to this debate. For tech bosses, there may be a psychological dimension too. Silicon Valley has created the first class of elites in history whom we associate with taste’s utter absence. If you’re among this class, the sense that a pesky humanities blind spot occludes your all-seeing STEM vision may irk you. If you’re a company like OpenAI, which recently put out a streetwear drop in a clumsy play for Supreme-style cool, you may dream of merchandising the blind spot away. If you’re someone like Mark Zuckerberg, currently engaged in a highly publicized wardrobe pivot toward Prada loafers and knits from the Row, you may dream of shopping it away.</p><p>Above all, if you are an A.I. true believer, you may dream of engineering it away. A.I. labs have been expending enormous resources to try and teach their models how to tell the ineffably good from the ineffably bad. A multibillion-dollar A.I. subindustry has emerged, in which start-ups throw ungodly sums at legions of freelancers, hiring them to critique A.I. output across various creative endeavors, like writing, music and VFX. In July, one such company, <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/07/10/business/ai-white-collar-jobs.html">Mercor, was reportedly shelling out more than $4 million daily to 30,000 contractors</a>. As of last month, <a href="https://www.cbsnews.com/news/will-ai-take-my-job-60-minutes-transcript/">according to “60 Minutes,” that number had swelled to more than 100,000</a>.</p><p>It’s possible that the most significant outcome of such efforts will not be to make generative models any more “tasteful” but to funnel an obscene amount of A.I. funny money into the coffers of well-positioned players. Yet the moonshot goal at the big A.I. labs remains to develop a technology that can automate every conceivable kind of labor — not just the task-driven, white-collar labor of paralegals but more abstract creative labor too. Hence the emphasis on taste.</p><p>If Silicon Valley seems short on taste these days, it’s because until now, basically no tech companies cared about tastefulness, save one: Apple. Steve Jobs was a refined showman who owned black Issey Miyake mocknecks in multiple. His longtime lead industrial designer, Jony Ive, was an avowed admirer of Dieter Rams. Together they developed a sleek array of devices, including the iMac and iPhone. But other industry behemoths aren’t in the business of selling high-end consumer goods, and so they’ve tended to build themselves around coders like Zuckerberg, who as a cohort aren’t known for their interest in squishy intangibles.</p><p>“To me, taste is by definition fabulously subjective, and fabulously human,” Jony Ive told me recently. This might help explain why taste can stymie engineers, supply-chain machers and other corporate number-crunchers who worship at the altar of hard data. “There’s a tendency for people to be more comfortable talking about issues you can measure easily,” he says.</p><p>The truly vexing thing is that taste isn’t Silicon Valley’s bugbear alone. Thanks to its creations, the ways in which all of us relate to our preferences have undergone immensely alienating changes. We discover favorite songs listening to playlists created by machines, we develop “personal style” watching TikTok influencers, we rate movies on Letterboxd to generate engagement from strangers. We go to our feeds to figure out what to like and how to be.</p><p>As we scroll, we fear miring ourselves in algorithmic loops where the total possible field of “what we can ever like”<em> </em>draws only from the digital heap of “what we have liked before.” Even interests that would have once registered as arcane, we can now compress into knowing “starter pack”-style memes, which taxonomize the putative taste markers of subcultural costumes (Chinati souvenir cap, Rachel Cusk novel, Dashwood Books tote, matcha latte). The implication is that any set of tastes can be reduced to a playbook. Or, if you like, to a piece of code.</p><p>In 1961, the literary theorist René Girard argued that people like what they like because they see others liking it. He called this “mimetic desire.” Today, in our online panopticon of constant surveillance and self-presentation, our preferences are liable to feel less our own than ever.</p><p>A.I. promises only to compound this. Many of us have already begun turning to chatbots not just to replace human interactions but also to do our thinking for us, creating what even <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2025/09/26/well/is-ai-validation-healthy.html">OpenAI has acknowledged is a danger of “social deskilling.”</a> It adds up to a moment of unprecedented unease around taste, so pronounced as to feel existential. What will we like, and<em> </em>who will it make us, when we come out the other side?</p><figure><video controls="" poster="https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/11/05/22mag-taste-53117-cover/22mag-taste-53117-cover-articleLarge.jpg"><source src="https://vp.nyt.com/video/2026/11/05/182823_1_22mag-taste-53117_wg_1080p.mp4" type="video/mp4"/></video></figure><p><strong>Human beings have</strong> felt anxiety about taste as long as we’ve had the concept. A 19th-century French count takes pride in his exquisite Beauvais tapestries until a cooler count comes by and won’t shut up about his Gobelins. These nobles’ grandchildren sneer at the gauche industrialist filling garages with flashy cars, and he sneers back at the fading gentry’s threadbare rugs. A 1980s commodities broker flashes his Patek at the maître d’ in hopes of scoring a better table. The sculptor with whom the broker’s wife is having an affair thinks fancy watches are the compensatory pastime of philistine goobers. A 2026 creative director posts a picture of that sculptor’s monograph on an Aalto stool, impressing some and inspiring others to roll their eyes at the obviousness of the tableau. Taste is a means of judging the world that has long created a means for the world to judge you.</p><p>We once understood taste primarily as a function of aristocratic wealth and “good breeding.” It wasn’t until the 1920s that a disruptive new figure was born — the modern consumer — and with it a new, more putatively democratic concept of taste. Until that point, unless you were a leisure-class elite, you only bought new shoes when you wore out your old pair. But with interwar production lines booming, advertisers set about crafting a “new and essential message,” as the filmmaker Adam Curtis puts it in his 2002 documentary “The Century of the Self”: “you bought things not just for need but to express your inner sense of yourself to others.”</p><p>Conveniently for manufacturers, this inner sense was far more chasmic than any pair of shoes could ever satisfy: An infinite market, defined by our urges, aspirations, insecurities, vanities and tastes, sprawled within each and every one of us. The counterculture pushed back against this annexed vision of the self in the 1960s, rejecting consumerism as an arm of the capitalist-imperialist war machine it sought to topple. But that energy was eventually redirected into the free-market-amenable pursuit of “self-actualization.” Seekers were assured that selfishness was, in fact, anything but, because the best way to improve society was to focus on improving yourself. The result was a generation of post-counterculture consumers who insisted on buying only what Adam Curtis calls “products that would express their individuality, their difference in a conformist world.”</p><p>It was here that the lines between identity, consumption and taste got severely scrambled.<em> </em>Having anti-consumerist inclinations didn’t have to mean rejecting consumerism. It just meant buying products “aligned” with your “values.” By the 1980s, the advertising industry had coined an indelible term for the canniest approach to dividing and conquering customers: “lifestyle marketing.”</p><p>Four decades later, those lines feel harder than ever to separate. Streaming Agnès Varda films on Criterion and watching inane, Sephora-sponsored “Get Ready With Me” reels on Instagram are muddled into a single act: “content consumption.” Everything we do online and, increasingly, offline, accumulates into dossiers about us that data brokers sell to advertisers. Lifestyle marketing is so complete that today we aren’t just our own markets but our own marketers, too, pressured to steward our “personal brands.”</p><p>This can take bluntly literal forms. Friends trading recommendations on social media, and even in private group chats, have started to share affiliate links, which entitle them to kickbacks if those tips lead to purchases. As <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/08/02/books/review/the-story-of-your-life-kathryn-jezer-morton.html">the journalist Kathryn Jezer-Morton puts it in her book “The Story of Your Life,”</a> about the warping effects of social media on consciousness, we’ve come to see ourselves as “little entrepreneurs of selfhood.”</p><p>The things we like, the things we buy, even moments of friendship — all can represent sales opportunities.</p><p><strong>When it comes</strong> to taste, much of our discomfort has long boiled down to a single question: Did my preference arise organically from somewhere within me, or was it implanted, artificially and insidiously, from without? This dilemma has always been crazy-making because it depends on a false binary. Taste, like language, is irreducibly social. It can never exist in some vacuum of pure interiority. What’s different now is that the internet has created a vast asymmetry between the internal and the external. We’ve dissolved the boundary between living our lives and representing them online, and the ramifications for taste are dizzying.</p><blockquote>Taste is a means of judging the world that has long created a means for the world to judge you.</blockquote><p>“Instagram has been one of the most incredible media platforms ever for showing taste and broadcasting it to the world,” says <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2022/09/06/books/review/status-and-culture-w-david-marx.html">W. David Marx, author of the 2022 book “Status and Culture.”</a> “But the very medium distorts and devalues the flex as it’s happening, because there’s no subtlety to it. The thing about taste is, it’s supposed to be behavioral residue — these signals are supposed to be part of your life, not something you intentionally aspire to signal. But the internet makes it almost impossible to tell the difference.”</p><p>In tracing the genealogies of our tastes, we could once point with more confidence to seeming gold-standard factors like organic discovery, time spent digging and serendipity. Online, though, the cultivation of taste can feel less like a process of messy chance and more like updates we download on schedule. I thought wearing muted earth tones when everyone was wearing loud prints was my<em> </em>idea, until I picked up my phone and saw 20 people in earth tones, too.</p><p>“I remember seeing all these memes in the past few years about being able to judge someone’s screen time” — how many hours they spend on their phone — “by the outfit they were wearing,” says Biz Sherbert, who co-hosts <a href="https://www.nymphetalumni.com/s/podcast">the podcast “Nymphet Alumni”</a> and writes <a href="https://bizsherbert.substack.com/">the newsletter “American Style,”</a> which focus on youth-culture trends. “That felt really depressing to me. Not because I couldn’t believe people were getting references from the internet but because people had developed this kind of taste paranoia.”</p><p>Taste paranoia isn’t exactly new. People have long filled bookshelves with books they haven’t read — impersonators fetishizing objects of distinction to flatter their self-image and impress houseguests. But as we articulate our public selves online, the opportunities for impersonation multiply. And so, while we’ve always been prone to some confusion about our own motivations, and to some mistrust of others’, today we’re more confused and mistrustful than ever. We fear liking the wrong things, or liking the right things the wrong way: We fret about appearing “performative” in the books we read, accuse others of perpetrating “cosplay” in the clothes they wear and dismiss legions more as mere “N.P.C.s.” — automatons following trends in lock step, like “non-playable characters” in video games.</p><p>Secretly we might worry we have more N.P.C. tendencies in ourselves than we care to admit. When we get meager likes on a pic of a Noguchi lamp, we wonder: Are our friends too “basic” in their tastes to get it? Or were we too basic to know to buy an Ingo Maurer instead?</p><p>Talking with Sherbert, who is 29, I was struck by the fact that she identified “the biggest driver” in the development of her own taste as Tumblr, an image-heavy blogging platform that reached peak popularity during her pre-algorithmic, early-2010s adolescence. Tumblr has long since been supplanted in the zeitgeist by other image-heavy platforms, like Instagram and TikTok, but it has played a sneakily pivotal role in rewriting the workings of taste as we know them.</p><p>On the one hand, it was a place ripe with discoveries: “It’s where you saw the screenshot of a Sofia Coppola movie, then went and watched your first Sofia Coppola movie,” Sherbet said. And since your feed consisted entirely of images from people you chose to follow, it felt like an active reflection of your own taste. In hindsight, though, Tumblr’s most consequential feature was the way it let such “discovery” happen at a marked remove from “context.” Someone you followed reblogged a picture that someone else had reblogged after someone else reblogged it, daisy-chaining back to a hard-to-identify original poster who’d scanned in the image from … well, that could be tough to know, too.</p><p>Particularly curious and determined users like Sherbert could go deeper and sleuth out answers, but by and large the site became synonymous with a base-line level of epistemological disorientation. Learning the details of any picture’s origins came second to the narcotic experience of letting vibey imagery wash over you, in a provenance-dissolving logic that has come to characterize much of our scrolling today. “WHAT IS THIS?” a gag T-shirt featuring the cover art of Joy Division’s 1979 album, “Unknown Pleasures,” asked several years ago. “I’VE SEEN IT ON TUMBLR.”</p><p>Today, in the algorithmically organized climate of taste paranoia Sherbert describes, any group that forms around shared cultural interests — amateur film photography, midcentury-modern Brazilian furniture, artisanal Japanese clothing — can be derided as its own little herd of sheep, grazing their own subsection of the same predictive pasture we’ve all been fenced into.</p><p>Are there gaps in the fences we might wiggle through? Perhaps I’m committing the fallacy of “romanticizing the way things were when I was 15,” but I think there are clues hiding in the last decade of life humans knew before the internet changed everything, which also happens to be the decade when I turned 15: the 1990s.</p><figure><video controls="" poster="https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/11/05/22mag-taste-69717-cover/22mag-taste-69717-cover-articleLarge.jpg"><source src="https://vp.nyt.com/video/2026/11/05/182822_1_22mag-taste-69717_wg_1080p.mp4" type="video/mp4"/></video></figure><p><strong>When I was</strong> in high school, I saw a classmate wearing a green T-shirt decorated with a photo of a crocheted doll. Two words flanked its face: “Sonic Youth.” This classmate played in a band with older kids and he had a “suffer no fools” aura I found intimidating. It was 1994. Sonic Youth was a legendary New York City noise-rock band, and the tee captured the cover of “Dirty,” their 1992 album.</p><p>Not that I knew this at the time. Maybe I’d seen Kurt Cobain mention Sonic Youth in a magazine, but I had no idea what they sounded like. Still, their name seemed cool, the shirt looked cool and its owner was cool, so — in an act of raw mimetic desire — I made it my conscious mission to like Sonic Youth.</p><p>This was a multistep, multiyear process. In a later era, I could have watched their top-viewed videos on YouTube, bought a T-shirt and declared myself a fan. But in the ’90s, that would have incurred an untenable social penalty: “You were scared of being called a ‘poser,’” David Marx said when I told him this story, invoking the silver-bullet insult of the era.</p><p>Instead, I had to save up for a cassette of “Dirty” (CDs were out of my budget), find a shop that stocked it and then grapple with the alarming discovery, when I actually listened to the album, that I found much of the music strange and unpleasant. I was a kid, with little frame of reference for what Sonic Youth were doing, but I stuck with it, sensing that some important epiphany awaited me on the other side of my discomfort.</p><p>I listened to the band with friends, making sense of choices that excited and flummoxed us. I read music criticism in The Village Voice, noted which bands were on the Lollapalooza lineup alongside Sonic Youth and which bands’ posters were tacked up beside theirs at record stores. In halting, uncertain steps, I situated my interest in the band within a broader world, working my way toward an appreciation that felt deeper and more personal.</p><p>With time, Sonic Youth became a base camp for further explorations. Who was Mike Kelley, the artist behind the doll on the “Dirty” cover? Who was Glenn Branca, the downtown composer whose guitar squalls, a friend informed me, had inspired Sonic Youth’s? Who was Steve Reich, the minimalist legend whose undulating figures had influenced Branca? What sublime mysteries would knowing all these answers unlock?</p><p>When I try to recall how my tastes formed, pre-internet, this is where my mind goes. I think about how my ’90s adolescence corresponded with the brief dominance of Gen-X values: a concerted effort to resuscitate a defiant countercultural position after the crass reign of ’80s yuppiedom, prizing the holdout over the sellout, the obscure over the obvious, the difficult over the easy.</p><p>I also think about how my naked desire to like something cool, counterbalanced by my dread of peer scorn, forced me to devote elaborate effort to the ostensibly simple goal of “telling people I liked Sonic Youth.” And how, at a certain point, like any novice working toward competence, I felt I’d actively earned my taste.</p><p>This is not how things have worked for a while. For starters, we’re inclined to remember ’90s Gen X-ers as annoying people with archaic values about purity. Their unapologetically materialistic archnemeses, the yuppies, have, for the moment anyway, triumphed.</p><p>What’s more, <em>earning something</em> requires time and sustained attention, and that’s not how anyone would characterize contemporary life’s cadence. As often as not, we look at our screens to let meaningless information inundate us in a semi-aware haze, or to get specific answers instantly. If we see, say, Kylie Jenner wearing a vintage Sonic Youth shirt in a paparazzi shot, we can just buy a repro on Depop. The social internet is a supernova of context collapse, with no penalties for taking shortcuts. Assembling your identity need not be very different from assembling a mood board.</p><p>This has a certain liberatory aspect, along the lines of “we can be whomever we choose.” But it can also leave us feeling unsatisfied and anchorless. And in our milieu of frictionless efficiency, we’ve started to sense that something valuable is getting lost. “I think it really builds your self-esteem to solve a problem,” Sherbert said. “That’s why there’s benefits to children playing sports — it’s good to fall and get back up again, in whatever you do.”</p><p>What if we conceive of taste, then, as an immensely valuable skill for tackling a problem, the problem being: How do we come to know other people, and come to know ourselves, better?</p><p>Taste, after all, is what steers us toward culture that’s not merely pleasurable, not merely flattering to our self-image and advantageous to our social standing, but rich in meaning too. This culture can thrill us even as it confounds us, putting us on unfamiliar terrain where we glimpse the subjectivities of others, and broaden our own.</p><p>Crucially, taste leads us to other people, and for all its exclusionary uses, it has an essentially pro-social function. Exclusion is a necessary part of this, because it’s important to be able to draw a line between an “us<em>,”</em> defined by values we find ennobling, and a “them,” defined by values we find abhorrent, or at least banal. When we talk about finishing a great book and “running outside to tell somebody,” going to a concert and “finding our tribe,” meeting someone who hates the same popular thing we hate and “feeling less insane” or watching a beautiful film and “feeling less alone,” this is what we’re saying. Taste is a rumble in me that comes into phase with a rumble in you.</p><p>For a time i t was possible to see life online as amplifying the rumblings. We were sold a dream of the internet as a place of unprecedented connection. But over the last decade or so, this technology of interconnectedness has combined with shifting social conditions to make us feel profoundly lonely. In the process, something vital about the way we relate to our taste — which is to say, the way we relate to ourselves and to other people — has been damaged. Is there any reason to believe A.I. won’t make things worse?</p><p><strong>A few short</strong> years ago, an outlandish, sci-fi-flavored premise began circulating that, in retrospect, gets to the heart of how A.I. is changing things. Known as the Dead Internet Theory, this premise emerged around 2020, proposing that most seemingly human activity we encountered on the web was actually the work of bots pretending to be humans. We thought we still traveled an internet dominated by real people like us, but real people were few and far between — stray ghosts haunting a machine.</p><p>This theory caught on because it captured what online life was starting to feel like. Today, as the internet teems with A.I.-generated Google results, A.I. customer-service agents, A.I. influencers shilling medical advice, malicious swarms of A.I. hackers and A.I. possums who can do tricks on A.I. skateboards, the notion of a Dead Internet has gone from sci-fi to plain fact: This June, the web-hosting giant <a href="https://fortune.com/2026/09/18/cloudflare-ceo-matthew-prince-ai-bots-human-web-traffic-pay/">Cloudflare announced that bots now represent nearly 60 percent of all web traffic.</a></p><p>Properly speaking, the Dead Internet Theory describes an <em>undead</em> internet. There is something inherently necrotic about A.I., designed as it is to feed on, regurgitate and reanimate the entire corpus of human culture in the form of weird digital corpse-flesh. This helps account for the queasy feeling that A.I.-generated culture can provoke in us. What’s more, A.I. feeds on itself, creating new corpse-flesh from old in an inbred dynamic that researchers at Microsoft internally described as a “doom loop.” None of which, it goes without saying, is a very tasteful thing to do. (The Times has sued OpenAI and Microsoft for copyright infringement, which the companies deny.)</p><p>A personalized-entertainment extrapolation on the Dead Internet Theory in a scenario where computers have learned taste would look something like this: Imagine logging on to some future version of Netflix and finding a selection of high-quality entertainment produced just for you. Say you’re a fan of the sui generis comedian Tim Robinson. Imagine prompting the platform to generate a new season of <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2023/06/02/magazine/tim-robinson-i-think-you-should-leave.html">“I Think You Should Leave,” Robinson’s beloved cult sketch series.</a> “Make the six episodes Tim Robinson would make if he brought the show back today,” you might say, “with contemporary references and jokes that capture the best parts of his sensibility without ever feeling like old, warmed-over Tim Robinson.”</p><p>Imagine the platform quickly creating thousands of would-be skits, and thousands more simulated viewers to appraise them, taking their feedback into account, narrowing the options and serving up your request. Computationally speaking, this model would be capable not only of mining past data to identify and replicate patterns but also of extrapolating on and departing from those patterns in nuanced, satisfyingly unpredictable, functionally <em>tasteful</em> ways.</p><p>“That’s the full A.G.I.-pilled version,” Joey Flynn, a veteran tech product designer, said, when I put this hypothetical to him. He was invoking the theoretical state of so-called Artificial General Intelligence, when, as he put it, “whatever insight you would have, the model can have. It has so much information, it knows when it’s time to flip the script.”</p><p>Flynn, who has held positions high up at companies including OpenAI and Instagram, is among many A.I. enthusiasts prone to regard A.G.I. as a pipe dream — “Easier said than done,” he said. Perhaps it’s no coincidence that he also has taste, and that, as it happens, he never planned on a career in tech at all. Facebook hired Flynn straight out of college in 2009, even though he had no product-design education, and none as a coder. He studied Bauhaus principles and Swiss typography, imagining a life creating fonts. When I first met him, at a party in San Francisco in 2021, he wore a faded old Stone Island jacket, crunchy Keen sandals and a vintage Cartier Tank: nothing tech-bro about it. He designed an anorak in collaboration with an influential Manhattan men’s wear shop, and helped some friends open a loud, sweaty, beloved wine bar in San Francisco.</p><p>That same year he founded a company with two prominent machine-learning engineers he had befriended at Instagram, which OpenAI later bought, intent on bringing the trio into its ranks. Flynn was tasked with crafting an interface for the generative-video tool Sora, which turned prompts (“a dragon fighting a shark in space”) into realistic clips.</p><p>When I asked Flynn what he thought was driving Silicon Valley’s taste obsession, he put it in practical terms. If a bunch of different people give an A.I. model “the same prompt, like, ‘Make an app that looks like it was designed by Apple,’” he explained, “it’s basically going to give us the same output. So when you hear people saying it’s all about taste now, I think it’s: Who are the people with some insight to where they can guide the model into doing something more resonant?” Jony Ive echoed this line of thinking. He told me that he and the designers he employs at his company, LoveFrom, incorporate unreleased OpenAI models into their practice, which allows them to “spend less time trying to embody an idea” — but “the primary tool I use,” he said, remains “my pencil and my sketchbook.”</p><p>In tech jargon, someone who uses technology to enhance his powers is known as a “centaur”: human actor up top, software boosting his capabilities from down below. Riffing on this image, the author Cory Doctorow has written about a distressing shadow figure called the “reverse centaur,” where the human is reduced to a mere appendage, toiling in technology’s service. Amazon drivers, electronically monitored down to the movements of their pupils, are totemic reverse centaurs. With generative A.I. — a technology whose “allegiances,” as it were, are split between us, its owners and, most murkily, itself — it can be tricky to say who is ultimately in control. It can be tough, that is, to tell one kind of centaur from the other.</p><blockquote>The social internet is a supernova of context collapse, with no penalties for taking shortcuts.</blockquote><p>When we prompt an A.I. model to produce better work, in the way Flynn describes, there is a disconcerting sense in which we’re acting as unwitting reverse centaurs, actively training our would-be replacements. While scrolling the Mercor site, I saw a listing for design experts to train A.I. models. Isn’t the tech bosses’ dream, I asked Flynn, to reach the point where their models get so good they can eliminate you?</p><p>“I don’t know that I’d use the word ‘eliminate,’” he said. “It’s more: Can we triple the output of a human? That’s what we’re already seeing, where now an engineer isn’t writing code all day, they’re just controlling a fleet of agents and having them do 100 times the work they could do before.” Flynn was referring to so-called vibe coding, where programmers and laypeople alike can ask A.I. to build programs for them using plain-English prompts.</p><p>Of course, we tend to seek fundamentally different things from culture than we do from code, and there’s something deeply unsettling about the notion of “vibe crafted” culture, produced prompt by prompt by a centaur auteur.</p><p><strong>Among creative people</strong> who <em>don’t</em> work in tech, generative A.I. seems broadly unpopular. But it does have some thoughtful converts. An experimental music producer who goes by the name Lil Internet has talked about trying to “crack” popular music-making A.I. tools like Udio and Suno in the pursuit of sounds that emerge “out of the triangulation of, like, 800 points in machine space.” <a href="https://notagspodcast.substack.com/p/62-lil-internet">On the music podcast “No Tags,” Lil Internet cautioned against dismissing all A.I. as </a><a href="https://notagspodcast.substack.com/p/62-lil-internet"><em>a priori</em></a><a href="https://notagspodcast.substack.com/p/62-lil-internet"> slop.</a> “There’s a huge spam problem with A.I., of course,” he said. “But short-form video itself, the entire physics of TikTok, is just this brain-hacking thing without A.I. content, right?” The worst A.I. slop, he ventured, is simply “an intensification.”</p><p><em>Intensification </em>is a great way to understand lots of the A.I. output we encounter today. Insipid therapy speak, dumb novelty songs, fast-fashion churn, bludgeoningly incoherent action sequences, cynical recombinations of existing I.P. — A.I. didn’t invent any of these. It’s just extremely good at reproducing them, and other addictive modes of pre-existing junk culture, inexpensively and at near-infinite scale.</p><p><a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/03/24/technology/openai-shutting-down-sora.html">Earlier this year, OpenAI announced it was shutting down Sora</a>, its video-generating model. Not long after, Flynn left the company. But at a time when video clips, with their direct pipelines to our lizard brains, are busy devouring all other forms of media, this seems like an isolated hiccup rather than some broader indicator.</p><p>Generative A.I. will most likely continue to thrive in intrinsically tasteless contexts, where the only thing that matters is maximizing attention and minimizing cost. Absent-mindedly scrolling X not long ago, I was served a grainy surveillance video in which a bumbling armed man barges into a store. His gun flies comically out of his hand and, failing to hop the counter to retrieve it, he does a 180 and jump-kicks the door, at which point his pants fall down.</p><p>I didn’t know if it was real. Ultimately it made no difference. I let it loop three times, contemplating it like an idiot koan, and as my life force dwindled, someone, somewhere, made some money. The writer Aidan Walker calls this “slop capitalism.”</p><p>New attempts at elite connoisseurship may well emerge in this mind-numbing landscape. Susan Sontag famously argued for the “good taste of bad taste,” and how ironically distanced enjoyment of campy mass culture could become its own form of discernment. Perhaps an erudite class of would-be sloptimists will arise, with their own neo-camp strategies for separating “good” A.I. trash from “bad” A.I. trash.</p><p>But with enough time and exposure, A.I. output might shed its uncanny telltales altogether, frustrating such moves. Children raised on A.I.-generated YouTube animations and other jabbering brain-rot content might be primed to accept so-called synthetic culture, along with synthetic companionship, on its ever-more-humanlike face.</p><p>We can already see a sort of Chatbot Canon of Taste beginning to assemble itself. This year, a young social media strategist named Cait Cavell helped conduct an informal “A.I. shopping experiment” that doubled as an A.I. taste test. She prompted seven different A.I. models to recommend “the perfect white T-shirt,” “the best Japanese denim” and “the best Approach shoes.” Whereas “you might assume they’d recommend completely different brands,” Cavell reported, the models all “clustered around the same handful” of answers, with clear consensus picks. Along similar lines, a newsletter called “Not Boring” recently published an article noting that, when asked to recommend a page-turner, both Claude and ChatGPT demonstrated a seeming affinity for the novel “Lonesome Dove.”</p><p>A.I. is poised to push into even more jarringly intimate territory. There are all kinds of stories of people outsourcing romance, spiritual counsel and cognition itself to Chatbots. It’s no big leap from there to people feeding works of culture into A.I. models wholesale, asking bots to explain why we like them — relying on algorithms not just for recommendations but to define our taste itself.</p><p>To relate to taste this way, though, would be to powerfully diminish, if not eliminate outright, taste’s social function. It would be to usher in a post-taste future, which is to say, a post-human future.</p><p>For that reason, some will be bound to resist it. As the dead internet continues to fester, we’ll most likely see a countervailing, neo-’90s-style move toward smaller, self-regulating, private communities online — gated message boards, pay-walled newsletter chats and other virtual Balkans yet to be established. There is already a resurgence of interest across contemporary culture in bygone, analog modes of culture-making. We might well see a return to cultural production that happens wholly offline, unmediated by the internet altogether.</p><p>I thought about this as I heard Joey Flynn describe certain moments from his life with special fondness: mocking up band tees and Myspace pages in Photoshop with his older brothers, learning about composition and scale with fellow students in college, making apps in offices with friends. These moments sit in tension with A.I.’s overwhelmingly isolating potential: the way it promises, in his own telling, to leave a single engineer manning a fleet of virtual agents, and the way it threatens to put a mesh of yet more agents — yet more dead internet tissue — between us and other people.</p><p>Let’s imagine that the wildest A.G.I.-pilled dreams of A.I. apostles come true. Say you can prompt some future descendant of Netflix, or Spotify, to generate art tailored nanoscopically to your taste. Say the platform does this so well that not merely dumb people with no standards but smart, discerning people like you are amused, entranced and possibly even moved by the results. It strikes me that something nonnegotiable is missing from this magical future: everybody else.</p><p>On this point, Flynn and I agree. “I don’t know what that does to humans,” he said, seizing on the onanistic implications. “If it’s a show I watch and love but can’t talk to anyone about, is that still a good show?” He let the scenario play out a few beats further. “Maybe we get rewired to have this very singular experience of the world. Maybe that’s the final form, and that’s how it’s meant to be: I only listen to music made for me, you only listen to music made for you. … ”</p><p>It’s an unsavory vision. Each of us installed in our own little lighthouse, masters of our own fully agentic domain. Our taste is still a beacon we flash out into the darkness, except now no one ever sees it, and no one but bots ever shines a light back.</p><hr/>
