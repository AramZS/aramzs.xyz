<p>IN ITS SO-CALLED golden age, “porn” meant movies with serious budgets, fleshed-out plots, and competent actors. You could see these movies in theaters and not be mortified. No less a personage than Jackie Kennedy once waited outside a New York theater to attend <em>Deep Throat</em> in 1972.</p>
<p>Fifty-four years later, things have changed. High-production-value, cogently structured, well-acted porn is an oxymoron, and the hardcore clips that have taken its place would have given Jackie a heart attack. The main difference between the golden age and now—and what presumably explains porn’s transformation—is the internet. When porn went online, it became cheaper, shorter, and more extreme, much like the rest of online content. Indeed, porn’s enshittification was part of a massive trend whose most visible evidence is on social media platforms like Facebook and Instagram. Conventional wisdom holds that the enshittification of porn has been downstream of the enshittification of the internet at large.</p>
<p>In her new book <em>Guilty Pleasure: The Pornification of the Internet, the Economy, and Everything Else</em>, reporter Patricia Nilsson argues that the conventional wisdom is primly mistaken. Actually, the purveyors of online porn pioneered many of the internet’s most emblematic features. “From live streaming and targeting to the algorithmic engineering of arousal, porn sites were often first to develop and scale up many of the technologies that serve as the foundation of the digital ecosystem,” she writes. Many content creators, for example, make money by earning a fee whenever someone clicks their way to a third-party purchase. This is called affiliate marketing, and the technology that makes it work was developed by one of the early owners of Pornhub. The early brokers of online sexual content, in developing methods to monetize free porn, accidentally developed methods to monetize any free content. The result is that the entire internet now models itself on porn’s “onanistic logic,” trapping us in an “endless loop of compulsive self-gratification” in order to capture and sell as much of our attention as possible. The internet didn’t invent modern porn. Modern porn invented the internet.</p>
<p>This reversal is tantalizing. Is it true? Nilsson shows that if you look closely, porn has always been in the picture throughout the history of the internet. Porn helped get people online in the first place. In the 1980s, there was a popular French videotex service called Minitel, a precursor to the World Wide Web. Much of the reason the service became so popular was its sex chats, its “messageries roses.” These “pink messages” attracted millions of people to an internet-like system. “Minitel,” Nilsson writes, “was a microcosm of the internet: sexual desire was what got people online.”</p>
<p>But the internet would only become a commercial juggernaut if people could use it to buy things. Porn helped make that happen too. After her friend was attacked walking home from her job at a San Francisco strip club, Madeleine Altmann started a business called Babes4U, an early cam site that was “part cabaret, part computer 101 class.” The redeployed strippers, on top of performing, taught their clients how to use the computers that connected them. One of their most important lessons was that it was acceptable to make payments over the internet: “By 1996, a few years before PayPal was founded, men were sending their card details to Madeleine, trusting a stranger to charge them by the minute.” Fast-forward 30 years, and <a href="https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/11767165">more than 20 percent</a> of all retail transactions happen online.</p>
<p>Cam sites were also among the first businesses to offer infrastructure for the low-latency, high-quality video content—i.e., live streaming—that undergirds the influencer economy. The most successful cam girls learned to monetize more than their bodies, internalizing “the core principles of platform labour such as self-branding, the performance of authenticity and the conversion of attention into income.” Such commodification of the self has become banal, but at the time, it was unprecedented. Without camming, Nilsson argues, there would be no OnlyFans, let alone TikTok. Madeleine Altmann walked so <a href="https://www.tiktok.com/@alixearle/video/7625800786420829471">Alix Earle could get ready with us.</a></p>
<p>But the most consequential technology that porn sites helped popularize was undoubtedly the internet’s premier revenue model. Until the mid-2000s, porn sites tried to make money the old-fashioned way, by asking viewers to pay money for their product. But this strategy, in the age of Napster and LimeWire, turned out to be a nonstarter. As many industries selling digital products learned the hard way, it was just too easy to circumvent paying per item. People came to expect internet content to be free, or at least to feel free. Companies like Spotify and Netflix responded with subscription services, which maintain the illusion of free content by offering unlimited access for a nominal fee. (Some porn sites, like Brazzers, use this model.) Companies like Google—and Pornhub et al.—responded by actually offering their products for free, and finding someone other than users to foot the bill. Mostly, that meant advertisers. Build it and they’ll come; make it free and they’ll flock. And it is a truth universally acknowledged that a large captive audience must be in want of some advertising. “Traffic was king,” Nilsson writes, “and the goal was simple: lure in as many people as possible and serve them ads”—even if those ads were for sketchy products like penis-enlargement pills and online casinos.</p>
<p>But traffic, eventually, wasn’t enough. Once ad-blocking software became standard practice, and people started browsing more on their phones, the traditional ad model started to break down. Revenue across porn sites fell, and the sites had to adapt. To keep offering free content, the platforms sought a way to make more money from each user. As it happened, they didn’t need to search for long. All these user-generated platforms realized they were already sitting on gold mines: “huge deposits of behavioural data.” This was “not just what we clicked on, but what we hovered over without tapping and what we returned to when we couldn’t sleep. A record of desire, but also of contradiction—the things we wanted and wished we didn’t.”</p>
<p>The real money, it turned out, wasn’t in “counting clicks” but in analyzing them to “predict, and influence, our behaviour.” Pornhub proved uniquely adept at this, and it’s not hard to see why. Google may steward more of our data, but Pornhub sees us at our most excruciatingly intimate. If only it ended at what we click; if only we knew where it ended at all. Clicks, searches, watch times, pauses, scrolling, IP addresses, device types, locations—these, and countless other data, can be tracked and used to deduce something about us. Meanwhile, one research article <a href="https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1218772110">showed</a> that Facebook likes alone can accurately predict everything from age to religion. What Pornhub can infer from all the data we give off is almost unimaginable.</p>
<p>The utility of this behavioral data is twofold. First, it allows porn sites to prop up their ad revenue. Advertisers will pay more to know whom they’re selling to, and porn sites’ behavioral data—despite most people’s unwillingness to register an account—furnished that information. (Private browsing, Nilsson reminds us, does nothing to stop tracking.) Second, and with more significant repercussions, this data allowed porn sites to optimize their products. What if you could serve the right video, made as appealing as possible, to the right person every time?</p>
<p>Behold data-driven creativity, to use another somewhat oxymoronic phrase. Not only has Pornhub, like Netflix, Spotify, and other data-rich distributors, mastered the art of recommendation, feeding us the videos most likely to keep our attention; it has also figured out how to use its enormous data reservoirs to decide what gets made in the first place. Gone are the days—the 1970s, for instance—when pornographers, like all artists, had to take a creative swing, using what they knew about life and the medium to make movies people would want to see. Now, pornographers—though Netflix directors face an increasingly similar fate—are spared the guesswork otherwise known as art. Everything from the sex acts to the color of the furniture is engineered to maximize engagement and retention. And by everything, I really do mean everything:</p>
<blockquote>Girl1 and Girl2 make Guy3 their sex toy. Guy3 does whatever they ask of him, eager to please them. […]</blockquote>
<blockquote><em>Girl1 and Girl2’s clothes remain ON during sex, while Guy3 is completely naked</em>.</blockquote>
<p>That’s from a script written and produced by MindGeek, now called Aylo, Pornhub’s parent company, which was shared with the authors of <a href="https://nyulawreview.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/12/NYULawReview-94-6-RaustialaSprigman.pdf">a <em>New York University Law Review</em> article.</a> According to MindGeek, the script’s elements, derived from millions of data points, were “essential to success within the ‘CFNM’ (Clothed Female, Naked Male) genre.” This makes sense. It seems both necessary and sufficient that a member of the CFNM genre should include both CFs and NMs. Other directions defy intuitive interpretation. Why is it that Girl2 “lies on her stomach and looks over her shoulder at the camera”? Who knows? But she does, because the data demands it.</p>
<p>The seventies were a very long time ago indeed. The San Fernando Valley <a href="https://www.theatlantic.com/technology/archive/2018/06/porns-uncanny-valley/561521/">has become</a> Silicon Valley. <em>Boogie Nights</em> has become <em>The Social Network</em>. Does the evidence prove that the former invented the latter? Not exactly. Following Nilsson, I’ve been careful to prefix verbs like “pioneer” with qualifiers like “help.” Porn did not invent the internet; many people did, across continents and decades. Porn did not invent payment processing either; that patent is similarly diffuse, but Amazon, for example, was accepting online payments as early as 1995. And Pornhub did not invent the behavioral advertising and algorithmic personalization models that power the attention economy. That honor, if it belongs to anyone, arguably goes to Google, as scholar Shoshana Zuboff contends in her influential 2018 book <em>The Age of Surveillance Capitalism:</em> <em>The Fight for a Human Future at the New Frontier of Power</em>.</p>
<p>At best, Nilsson might reply that we have a Newton-Leibniz situation on our hands, a case of independent invention. If that’s so, then Google would be the better-known Newton. Perhaps history will hold that Pornhub, like Leibniz, indisputably invented one ubiquitous practice—data-driven creativity as the integral sign—but let us not further strain this analogy. The most that can be said for Pornhub, I think, is that it has consistently been at the cutting edge of the digital economy. Is that not remarkable enough?</p>
<p>¤</p>
<p>“This isn’t about judging porn, or defending it,” Nilsson declares toward the end of the book’s introduction. “It’s about taking it seriously.” Taking it seriously, however, may eventuate some judgments. <em>Guilty Pleasure</em> doesn’t bill itself as a polemical book, but it’s rife with polemical implications. Even if Nilsson’s historical claims must be qualified, she has nevertheless shown that Pornhub is more like Google than like <em>Playboy</em>. The implications of that analogy are breathtaking. By putting porn in its proper context, Nilsson has, deliberately or not, laid the groundwork to make porn political again.</p>
<p>Fifty years ago, to get your hands on adult content, you had to venture into the world and forfeit some currency. You might buy a copy of <em>Penthouse</em> at a newsstand, or a ticket to a feature at some Times Square fleapit. Now, you just open a private browser and search. You can get the stuff for free, anytime, anywhere, and you don’t have to interact with anyone. Today, Nilsson writes, as many people have access to free porn as have access to clean water. It’s tantamount to a human right. And yet, as porn has become globally ubiquitous, it has also lost much of its political significance. Social conservatives may lament porn’s corrupting influence, and child-safety groups may fight to restrict access for minors, but few today (at least on the secular left) argue, as anti-porn feminists did in the seventies, that porn actively harms the adults who consume it.</p>
<p>Probably no one argues this point because it falls on deaf ears. We can condemn vivid, immediate, tangible harms—Nicholas Kristof’s <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2020/12/04/opinion/sunday/pornhub-rape-trafficking.html">viral op-ed,</a> “The Children of Pornhub,” denounced the company for hosting videos of rape, child sex abuse, and torture—but we can’t bring ourselves to condemn vague, delayed, intangible harms, what we might call porn’s <em>diffuse</em> evils. The notion that porn models misogyny, for example, is perfectly plausible, but that putative harm is neither traceable nor proven enough to legislate against. The need for hard evidence, combined with the constitutional guarantee of free speech, leaves legislators with little choice but a kind of libertarian politics of porn, one that washes its hands of the matter unless you can prove a felony.</p>
<p>This, to my mind, is what’s most thrilling about Nilsson’s book. She doesn’t explicitly set out to make the case for porn’s diffuse harms, but the implication is there, like an undeveloped negative. Let me be slightly reductive and say that Nilsson’s innovation is to apply Zuboff’s findings in <em>The Age of Surveillance Capitalism</em> to the porn industry. Pornhub uses behavioral surplus—all the petabytes of data excreted by our clicks and scrolls—to make billions selling targeted ads. Sex is the bait; data is the catch. What follows?</p>
<p>If you can detect how people respond to various elements on your website, it’s only natural to test which content and structures are most likely to engage and retain people. Subliminally nudging behavior that benefits companies is what Zuboff calls “tuning,” and it’s everywhere on the internet. It’s the reason accepting all cookies is usually the default option; they’re basically the bedrock of surveillance capitalism. It’s the reason Netflix shows <a href="https://netflixtechblog.com/artwork-personalization-c589f074ad76">different thumbnails for the same movie to different people.</a> A romance fan will see the kissing scene from <em>Good Will Hunting </em>(1997), while a comedy fan will see Robin Williams. This kind of choice architecture shows up on Pornhub too. Consider the fact that heterosexuality is the site’s default setting. By funneling users toward heterosexual content, this default drives up view counts for those videos, making heterosexuality itself seem more popular.</p>
<p>While tuning subtly sways users to do what you want, “herding” makes it impossible for them to do anything else. Options other than what benefits the company simply drop away. The example par excellence is autoplay. Samantha Cole, in her 2022 book <em>How Sex Changed the Internet and the Internet Changed Sex</em>, argues that Pornhub’s clunky interface, with its endless thumbnails and obtrusive advertisements, is intentionally designed to “throw just enough grit into the gears” to keep users stuck on the site. There’s no option that gets you in and out quickly.</p>
<p>Zuboff’s final tool for controlling behavior is called “conditioning,” which gets its name from Harvard behaviorist B. F. Skinner. Skinner is famous for his experiments on rats. By rewarding the rats with food pellets whenever they pushed a lever, he reinforced his “selected” action and taught them to do, of their own accord, what he wanted. Surveillance capitalists treat their users the same way. An unpredictably timed like or comment is a “variable reward” that makes users post more, in search of more hits of dopamine. There’s less documented evidence that porn sites condition users in quite this way—although an endless grid of related videos is not unlike a slot machine—but they certainly condition performers. Samantha Cole relates how the Lovense Lush Bluetooth vibrator buzzes whenever a fan tips enough tokens. If Skinner could’ve trained his rats with this device, I bet he would have.</p>
<p>Armed with these three tools, surveillance capitalists have proven their ability to impose their will on entire populations. Facebook has been a leader in this effort. In 2012, their researchers published—with astonishing blitheness—a paper detailing <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22972300/">an experiment they’d conducted on 61 million people,</a> without their consent, in which they’d manipulated the voting-related messages in users’ news feeds. The team estimated that their intervention had increased turnout in the 2010 midterms by about 340,000 voters. For reference, the 2016 presidential election was decided by about 78,000 votes. Nudges, it turns out, could swing an election.</p>
<p>“We are learning how to write the music, and then we let the music make them dance,” one software developer told Zuboff. And dance we do, whether we like it or not. These systems of surveillance and control are so powerful—constantly watching, constantly molding—that what’s at risk, Zuboff warns, is nothing less than our right to self-determination, to a future tense. The worry is that, with surveillance capital fracking our attention at all times, we will lose the ability to will at all.</p>
<p>Aylo is a secretive company. We do not, and may never, know everything they do with our data. But there is no reason to believe that Pornhub is not subject to the full scope of surveillance capitalism’s imperatives. There is no reason to believe that Pornhub draws the line at a self-serving user interface and heteronormative funneling. We may lack the smoking guns that Facebook once published, but absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. And almost any way you fill in that absence is disquieting. If Pornhub were doing even a 10th of what its peers are, we’d have on our hands a company whose supreme goal was to make sure as many people as possible spend as much time as possible consuming pornography. To accomplish this goal, it would stop at nothing. It would target children in order to create lifelong customers. It would aim to addict us so we couldn’t escape. It would feed us content that grew increasingly extreme to counteract the habituation the company itself was engineering. And it would do this while taking care to distract from the true nature of its project, by publishing fun little listicles about what users are watching in Bulgaria, by fundraising for a bee charity, by celebrating International Women’s Day.</p>
<p>I would consider the deprivation of our ability to will an intangible delayed harm. (Hannah Arendt described the will as the “organ of the future.” A damaged organ works equally poorly in all cases.) The motive may be to keep us on a website, but the results range far wider. If you set out to impair someone’s ability to choose, it’s hard to control the fallout of that impairment. The person who finds it harder to choose what porn they watch, or whether they watch it at all, will find it harder to choose what they do in the bedroom. Disempowered to choose, they’ll copy what they see. Monkey see, monkey do. And when monkey’s being fed a lot of fucked-up porn, monkey may begin to act as if “no” means “yes,” as if “yes” means anything at all. Porn would be the theory and rape would be the practice, in Robin Morgan’s famous formulation. The anti-porn feminists would find themselves vindicated by the history of technology.</p>
<p>That world does not sound dissimilar to our own, and Nilsson’s book helps us recognize this. What is to be done? The author doesn’t set out to answer that question, though I think we can make some reasonable inferences. Recognizing porn’s “onanistic logic” in social media, for example, gives us a new vocabulary to articulate how these platforms exploit our nervous systems for profit. And, conversely, analogizing porn to social media points the way toward remedies for the harms of Big Porn. Perhaps that means effective age verification. Perhaps it means forbidding user-hostile design. In any case, pursuing such remedies will only become more urgent with the rise of artificial intelligence, whose designers want nothing more than to supercharge data-driven creativity and the extraction of behavioral surplus. To avoid plunging headlong into that dystopia, it will be necessary, in Zuboff’s words, to “rekindle the sense of outrage” over what is being done to us—what we are allowing to be done to us. Only then might we find common cause to imagine what we want porn’s next golden age to look like.</p>
