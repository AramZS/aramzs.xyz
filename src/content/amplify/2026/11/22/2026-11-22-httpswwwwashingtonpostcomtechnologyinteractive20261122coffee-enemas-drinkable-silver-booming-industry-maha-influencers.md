<p>Some of the movement’s champions are advertising more than ever, building businesses that could outlast the political moment.</p>
<p>November 22, 2026 at 5:00 a.m. ESTToday at 5:00 a.m. EST</p>
<p><b>Alex Clark</b> <a href="https://www.instagram.com/reels/DN8b0XxEd85/?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">advertised</a> “Microplastic Daily Detox” supplements that cost $39.95, saying a capsule every morning helped stop her from feeling like she was “turning into a human plastic bag.”</p>
<p><b>Carly Brown </b><a href="https://www.instagram.com/reels/DRP5apTEY8b/?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">marketed</a> Happy Bum Co.’s $74.95 silicone enema kits, saying coffee enemas were a part of her cancer “healing journey” and helped give skin a “radiant glow.”</p>
<p><b>Joel “Dr. Gator” Warsh</b> <a href="https://www.instagram.com/reels/DVJbhD9EfDz/?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">touted</a> bottles of drinkable silver promoted for children’s immune systems that doctors warn can turn the skin bluish gray.</p>
<p>This wave of wellness promoters and internet personalities have audiences that have ballooned over the last two years as they’ve championed the Make America Healthy Again movement, which often rejects mainstream science and plugs <a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/health/2026/02/17/keto-diet-schizophrenia-rfk-jr/?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">alternative medicine</a> <a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/health/2026/07/24/peptide-boom-has-states-scrambling-protect-consumers-post-investigation-finds/?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">popularized</a> by Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr.</p>
<p>For many, it’s now a revenue generator, helping them reach new viewers they can convert into paying customers.</p>
<p>While some of the promoted companies and their products may be benign, like beef-tallow baby balm and “shielding beanies” they say protect from electromagnetic fields, other offerings pose serious potential health risks, a Washington Post analysis has found.</p>
<p>At least 14 of the brands promoted by the movement’s fastest-growing influencers offer products that environmental watchdogs say contain levels of lead high enough to warrant special labeling under California law, which is stricter than federal standards. The Federal Trade Commission fined one company promoted by a MAHA advocate for falsely claiming that its products could make children taller. And at least 15 of the products would likely score as a “no-go” on a Defense Department test designed to tell troops which supplements are not recommended for use.</p>
<p>Some MAHA figures promoted products by suggesting vaccines are unsafe, which public health experts warn could further erode already <a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/health/interactive/2025/measles-vaccine-schools-outbreaks-public-health?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">declining vaccination rates</a> despite the safety of vaccines.</p>
<figure><img alt="" data-nimg="1" src="https://img.washingtonpost.com/wp-apps/imrs.php?src=https://arc-anglerfish-washpost-prod-washpost.s3.amazonaws.com/public/GLWMJBX42CWEIVT4KS5QCSDOBA_size-normalized.jpg&amp;high_res=true&amp;w=2048" srcset="https://img.washingtonpost.com/wp-apps/imrs.php?src=https://arc-anglerfish-washpost-prod-washpost.s3.amazonaws.com/public/GLWMJBX42CWEIVT4KS5QCSDOBA_size-normalized.jpg&amp;high_res=true&amp;w=2048 1x"/><figcaption>Health Secretary Robert F. <p>Brown, a wellness influencer who touted juice cleanses and coffee enemas as part of a regimen she said could help the body “heal itself” from cancer, promoted an alternative cancer treatment center in Mexico that faced since-settled lawsuits claiming it gave “false promises” to two cancer patients in the months before they died. (Brown said in an email that she couldn’t speak to legal matters the clinic was involved in and that she doesn’t identify as a MAHA influencer.)</p>
<p>The Post’s analysis reveals the extent to which these figures have built businesses that could outlast the political moment. Unlike past wellness entrepreneurs, these influencers strengthened their platforms and pursued potential profits under the mantle of a federal political agenda.</p>
<p>The influencers are looking to “capitalize on … very misleading beliefs” they perpetuate, said Peter Lurie, president of the nonprofit Center for Science in the Public Interest and a former official at the Food and Drug Administration during the Obama administration and Trump’s first term.</p>
<figure data-component="Ai2html"><p class="rw-outer-content"><span>Some content could not be imported from the original document.</span> <a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/interactive/2026/11/22/coffee-enemas-drinkable-silver-booming-industry-maha-influencers/">View content ↗ </a></p></figure>
<p>To be sure, many of this set espoused views about wellness, nutrition or vaccines before the movement began that have now become synonymous with MAHA. And some bristled at the label of influencer, despite promoting products on social media. They pointed instead to their credentials, work experience or accomplishments before they shaped the movement Kennedy leads.</p>
<p>Emily Hilliard, a spokeswoman for the Department of Health and Human Services, said in a statement that “MAHA’s mission is to improve Americans’ health through better nutrition, prevention, and rigorous science” and that “Kennedy’s leadership should not be interpreted as a blanket endorsement of every influencer, commercial partnership, or product associated with the movement.”</p>
<p>To examine the movement’s retail arm, The Post identified more than 80 podcasters, wellness gurus and other internet personalities who’ve described themselves as MAHA supporters and gained at least 100,000 followers on a single social network. The Post winnowed the group down to the 18 whose follower counts on Instagram climbed by 50 percent or more between August 2024 and June 2026, as the movement gained prominence and political power.</p>
<p>Reporters examined more than 37,000 social media posts, podcasts and newsletters since January 2024 and found a huge rush of commercialization: All but one promoted products, unleashing a flood of juices, supplements and serums into Americans’ social media feeds and everyday lives.</p>
<p>Their promotions ballooned from 257 products or brands in the first half of 2024 to 795 over the same period this year.</p>
<figure><a href="https://datawrapper.dwcdn.net/b99xX"><img alt="MAHA influencers began posting more ads as their audience grew" src="https://datawrapper.dwcdn.net/b99xX/full.png"/></a></figure>
<p>The Post examined the promoted goods and services in the group’s feeds by reviewing regulatory filings, nutritional labels, lawsuits and customer complaints, and interviewing more than a dozen public health experts, scientists and former regulators about the products’ purported health benefits.</p>
<p>The MAHA proponents consistently mentioned the movement in their posts in early 2025. But they have invoked the movement less than half as often in recent months, a change that comes at a time when some MAHA proponents say they have become <a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/health/2026/02/19/glyphosate-executive-order-maha-backlash/?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">disenchanted</a> with the Trump administration’s inability to deliver on sweeping changes around food, pesticides and vaccines.</p>
<figure><a href="https://datawrapper.dwcdn.net/l3lA7"><img alt="MAHA is on the decline" src="https://datawrapper.dwcdn.net/l3lA7/full.png"/></a></figure>
<p>By building a sales and marketing infrastructure, some of the movement’s biggest names have already cemented their online staying power.</p>
<p>Many of the 18 figures whose Instagram followings have soared contributed to Kennedy’s political rise. This group includes Del Bigtree, a communications director for Kennedy’s 2024 presidential campaign; Nicole Shanahan, his vice presidential running mate; and <a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/health/2025/10/09/casey-means-surgeon-general-nominee/?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">Casey Means</a>, who was chosen by President Donald Trump as his second nominee for surgeon general before he <a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/health/2026/04/30/surgeon-general-nominee-means-saphier/?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">withdrew</a> her nomination this spring.</p>
<p>Bigtree, who runs a group that <a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/health/2026/06/02/anti-vaccine-movements-best-shot-victory-may-be-supreme-court/?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">questions the safety of several vaccines</a>, said in an interview that he is different from other MAHA influencers because he doesn’t have a product line or get paid for promotional sponsorships. “I don’t sell products,” he said. “I sell the truth.”</p>
<figure><img alt="" data-nimg="1" src="https://img.washingtonpost.com/wp-apps/imrs.php?src=https://arc-anglerfish-washpost-prod-washpost.s3.amazonaws.com/public/HJRHBGVHRYLAJ466WPXKDB23MI_size-normalized.jpg&amp;high_res=true&amp;w=2048" srcset="https://img.washingtonpost.com/wp-apps/imrs.php?src=https://arc-anglerfish-washpost-prod-washpost.s3.amazonaws.com/public/HJRHBGVHRYLAJ466WPXKDB23MI_size-normalized.jpg&amp;high_res=true&amp;w=2048 1x"/><figcaption>Del Bigtree speaks to supporters in front of the Supreme Court in 2024. (Jabin Botsford/The Washington Post)</figcaption></figure>
<p>Bigtree later acknowledged that he had promoted a few products, including a Halloween candy called Tricked by the Treat promoted as less ”toxic” than other brands as well as a parasite cleanse, as part of a paid promotion deal or in exchange for a donation to his group, the Informed Consent Action Network, but said he only promotes products he believes in or supports.</p>
<p>He said that he is “as skeptical of alternate approaches to health as [he is] to pharmaceutical” ventures and that there is a “greed problem” on both sides inflamed by the profit motives of social media influencing. Of holistic products, he said, “They’re no more snake oil than almost every drug you take.”</p>
<p>A previous <a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/health/2025/10/09/casey-means-surgeon-general-nominee/?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">Post investigation</a> found Means, a health care entrepreneur and <a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/health/2026/03/23/casey-means-surgeon-general-nomination-stalled-maha/?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">influential author</a>, was paid more than half a million dollars for partnerships as well as for book-tour and newsletter sponsorships with companies her disclosure forms described as selling “diagnostic testing,” “herbal remedies and wellness products” and “teas, supplements, and elixirs” from 2024 into last summer.</p>
<figure><img alt="" data-nimg="1" src="https://img.washingtonpost.com/wp-apps/imrs.php?src=https://cloudfront-us-east-1.images.arcpublishing.com/wapo/7CTQTGV7ZIB2FZV6QQD47IXWEU.jpg&amp;high_res=true&amp;w=2048" srcset="https://img.washingtonpost.com/wp-apps/imrs.php?src=https://cloudfront-us-east-1.images.arcpublishing.com/wapo/7CTQTGV7ZIB2FZV6QQD47IXWEU.jpg&amp;high_res=true&amp;w=2048 1x"/><figcaption>Casey Means takes her seat in front of senators as part of her confirmation process for U.S. Surgeon General. Her nomination was eventually pulled by the White House. (Tom Brenner/AP)</figcaption></figure>
<p>She stopped posting on Instagram before the announcement of her nomination for surgeon general <a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/health/2025/05/07/casey-means-surgeon-general/?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">last May</a>.</p>
<p>Shanahan did not respond to requests for comment.</p>
<p>Others have worked unofficially as MAHA surrogates, using their viral content and sizable fan bases to hype up the movement to the American public, including Alex Clark, the “Culture Apothecary” podcaster for Turning Point USA, a conservative group founded by the late Charlie Kirk, and Courtney Swan, the creator of the podcast Realfoodology, who has attended Trump administration food-policy events and sells hats labeled “Make Food Real Again.”</p>
<p>Their growing audiences coincided with an explosion of sponsored content as they appeared to monetize the political spotlight, the Post analysis found. Clark posted 352 promotions for brands or products in the first half of this year, nearly three times as many as she had during the first half of 2024. Swan went from promoting 25 products or brands in the first half of 2024 to 137 this year, including for Lineage Provisions, a meat-snack brand whose co-founder Paul Saladino <a href="https://www.youtube.com/shorts/AI0hBi4QSb8?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">drank shots of raw milk</a> last year with Kennedy in the White House. (Neither the company nor Saladino responded to requests for comment.)</p>
<figure><img alt="" data-nimg="1" src="https://img.washingtonpost.com/wp-apps/imrs.php?src=https://arc-anglerfish-washpost-prod-washpost.s3.amazonaws.com/public/IXABNGTSXSULYZPA5XYJWPMHC4_size-normalized.JPG&amp;high_res=true&amp;w=2048" srcset="https://img.washingtonpost.com/wp-apps/imrs.php?src=https://arc-anglerfish-washpost-prod-washpost.s3.amazonaws.com/public/IXABNGTSXSULYZPA5XYJWPMHC4_size-normalized.JPG&amp;high_res=true&amp;w=2048 1x"/><figcaption>Alex Clark listens as Courtney Swan speaks during the Turning Point USA summit AmericaFest last year. (Cheney Orr/Reuters)</figcaption></figure>
<p>The Post shared a detailed outline of its reporting with all of the people and businesses named in this report, including Swan, who responded that she is an “Integrative Nutritionist with a Master of Science in Human Nutrition, not a ‘MAHA influencer.’” She said that she had “been advocating for greater transparency and a healthier food system long before MAHA became a national movement” and that she believes it’s “important to hold independent creators and traditional media organizations to the same standards of transparency and accountability.”</p>
<p>“A commercial relationship does not automatically constitute an endorsement of every product, statement, or action associated with a company, and I believe that same distinction should apply to independent creators,” she added.</p>
<figure data-component="Ai2html"><p class="rw-outer-content"><span>Some content could not be imported from the original document.</span> <a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/interactive/2026/11/22/coffee-enemas-drinkable-silver-booming-industry-maha-influencers/">View content ↗ </a></p></figure>
<p>Clark referred comment to Turning Point USA, where Marina Minas, the group’s chief marketing officer, said in a statement that Clark “isn’t an ‘influencer’ in the usual sense” and is instead “a podcast host who has spent years speaking with and listening to young women and moms about the problems they’re actually facing.”</p>
<p>“When you build a real connection with a big audience around a topic a lot of people care about, advertisers show up. That’s not a conspiracy,” Minas said.</p>
<p>Clark, she added, “has turned down more sponsors than she takes,” and “is incredibly picky” for what she’ll “put her name behind,” Minas said, noting she charges lower rates due to working with small businesses.</p>
<p>Some wellness influencers capitalized on mistrust of the medical establishment for years before the MAHA movement began, working to harness Americans’ disillusionment with the health care system and their “desperate desire for alternatives,” said Megan Ranney, an emergency physician and dean of the Yale School of Public Health.</p>
<p>But these MAHA proponents have used this political movement to monetize their messages. It’s not possible to determine from publicly available information exactly how much money these people have made from this rush of merchandising, though some have spoken about their revenue. Brown, the self-described “grounded, results-driven” influencer who has supported Kennedy, <a href="https://www.instagram.com/reels/DYmlM8jBTyy/?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">said on Instagram</a> in May that she had built a “million-dollar online business” that earned her $10,000 a month alone from brand partnerships.</p>
<p>“The more my online community + impact grew, the more my income grew,” she said.</p>
<p>Brown said in an email that she has made few political posts but has “cared about holistic health for many years.”</p>
<p>“I share from my own personal experience, and I’m mindful of the language I use,” she said.</p>
<figure><img alt="" data-nimg="1" src="https://img.washingtonpost.com/wp-apps/imrs.php?src=https://arc-anglerfish-washpost-prod-washpost.s3.amazonaws.com/public/DO5ZP2N2DYTHJKFFHXGXIZLKSM_size-normalized.jpg&amp;high_res=true&amp;w=2048" srcset="https://img.washingtonpost.com/wp-apps/imrs.php?src=https://arc-anglerfish-washpost-prod-washpost.s3.amazonaws.com/public/DO5ZP2N2DYTHJKFFHXGXIZLKSM_size-normalized.jpg&amp;high_res=true&amp;w=2048 1x"/><figcaption>A man in the audience wears a ‘Make America Healthy Again’ hat during a Senate hearing on Kennedy's nomination in 2025. (Allison Bailey/AP)</figcaption></figure>
<p>Mallory DeMille, a social media creator who records fact-checks and commentary about the wellness industry, said MAHA influencers have built networks of loyal customers through frequent appearances on one another’s podcasts and sponsorships, urging fans to buy products on the basis that they were promoted by people they had begun to trust.</p>
<p>For viewers, DeMille said, “it’s almost like a package deal. ... Folks online talk about: I bought all of these things because Alex Clark told me to.”</p>
<p>David Gorski, a professor of surgery and oncology at Wayne State University, said he worries that the influencers’ claims could hurt the consumers who turned to them for help.</p>
<p>“If it sounds too good to be true, it almost certainly is,” he said. “Anyone who denigrates scientific knowledge in favor of something like this almost certainly has something to sell you.”</p>
<h2 data-component="Heading" data-contentid="C3MJR76J5NAXFOJJZA7NMRI35U">“Vaccine detox”</h2>
<p>Some of the biggest MAHA influencers have echoed Kennedy’s <a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/health/2025/01/28/rfk-jr-disparaged-vaccines-dozens-times-recent-years-misled-race/?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">longtime disparagement of vaccines</a> — and shown an interest in selling products to vaccine skeptics.</p>
<p>Swan, the “Realfoodology” podcaster, has promoted what she called a covid-shot “detox” from The Wellness Company, an online storefront that also sells “<a href="https://www.twc.health/products/contagion-emergency-kit?variant=43932120613080&amp;itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">contagion emergency kits</a>” of hydroxychloroquine, which Trump previously promoted, and ivermectin, an antiparasitic drug that some prominent right-wing figures have promoted to prevent or treat covid infections, though <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32673060/?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">clinical</a> <a href="https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2801827?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">trials</a> have shown that neither medication offers meaningful benefit on this front. The “Ultimate Spike Detox,” Swan said, fights against “spike proteins,” an element of the vaccine that scientists say is safe.</p>
<p>“We’re diving into how to detox your body from the Spike Proteins linked to COVID and the shots with my favorite detox product,” she <a href="https://www.instagram.com/reels/DH_rG1_STCW/?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">wrote on Instagram</a> in April 2025. “I personally buy it for my loved ones. Drop ‘SPIKE’ in the comments and I’ll DM you a special link with an exclusive offer!” At full price on the company’s website, a month’s supply costs $89.99.</p>
<figure><a href="https://datawrapper.dwcdn.net/Bw6Iw"><img alt="As Courtney Swan's audience grew, she posted more ads" src="https://datawrapper.dwcdn.net/Bw6Iw/full.png"/></a></figure>
<p>The company did not respond to requests for comment, and its <a href="https://www.twc.health/products/ultimate-spike-detox?variant=49407127257304&amp;itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">listing</a> for the Ultimate Spike Detox says its “products are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.”</p>
<p>Bruce Gellin, who headed the Department of Health and Human Services’ National Vaccine Program Office during the George W. Bush and Obama administrations, said he was unaware of any large-scale human trials that showed these supplements were safe or effective at clearing spike proteins from the body.</p>
<p>Moms Across America, a prominent MAHA group, also promoted “vaccine detox” herbal extracts, an acupuncturist who warned that women were having “menstrual abnormalities” from walking near covid-vaccinated people, and an institute that offers a set of online courses that says <a href="https://globalhealinginstitute.org/op/urine-therapy-summit/?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">drinkers of aged urine</a> can receive “clearer skin … and spiritual insight.”</p>
<figure><img alt="" data-nimg="1" src="https://img.washingtonpost.com/wp-apps/imrs.php?src=https://arc-anglerfish-washpost-prod-washpost.s3.amazonaws.com/public/BCP3ZM3JJPZODGE5S6RWO3YHBU_size-normalized.jpg&amp;high_res=true&amp;w=2048" srcset="https://img.washingtonpost.com/wp-apps/imrs.php?src=https://arc-anglerfish-washpost-prod-washpost.s3.amazonaws.com/public/BCP3ZM3JJPZODGE5S6RWO3YHBU_size-normalized.jpg&amp;high_res=true&amp;w=2048 1x"/><figcaption>Kennedy is broadcast on a large screen as he speaks during an anti-vaccine rally in front of the Lincoln Memorial in 2022. (Patrick Semansky/AP)</figcaption></figure>
<p>The CDC has said the idea that someone’s menstrual cycle could be affected by their proximity to a vaccinated person is a “<a href="https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/coronavirus/2019-ncov/vaccines/facts.html?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">myth</a>,” and The Post could find no scientific evidence to support the urine-consumption claims. The Global Healing Institute, which offers the courses, said in a disclaimer on its website that it “does not dispense medical advice” and that its views “are not intended to be a substitute for conventional medical service.” The institute did not respond to requests for comment.</p>
<p>In a <a href="https://www.momsacrossamerica.com/disclosures?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">disclosure page</a> on its website, Moms Across America says its “products are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease,” that “there is no guarantee of specific results,” and that “users should only purchase items they believe will be necessary or useful in achieving their goals.” The group did not comment when reached by The Post.</p>
<p>Other MAHA influencers capitalized on viewers’ covid skepticism through private “vaccine coaching” chats. Joel “Dr. Gator” Warsh, the physician and MAHA influencer, has <a href="https://x.com/DrJoelGator/status/2074116951316762635?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">said online</a> that other physicians have told him “they believe unvaccinated kids are healthier overall,” runs a $34.97-a-month Substack newsletter with more than 150,000 free and paid subscribers that offers one-on-one “Vaccine Coaching” sessions to VIP members, who pay $499 a year.</p>
<p>Warsh did not respond to requests for comment. On his Substack, “Dr. Gator — Between a Shot and Hard Place,” he <a href="https://substack.com/@drjoelgator?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">describes himself</a> as an “integrative pediatrician helping parents navigate vaccines, chronic illness, and modern childhood with nuance, evidence, and individualized care.”</p>
<h2 data-component="Heading" data-contentid="N7JMFRMUIBARHCDR65LAUMM5RU">“My life started to change”</h2>
<p>MAHA influencers haven’t just warned against vaccines. At times, traditional cancer care has also drawn their ire.</p>
<p>Brown, the influencer who previously used the name Carly Shankman, has promoted a variety of alternative treatments that she has said were critical to her healing after she was diagnosed with thyroid cancer in 2019.</p>
<p>In a <a href="https://atxwoman.com/2022/07/01/recipe-reveal-carly-brown-alchemy-juice/?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">2022 interview</a> discussing the “other ways to go about healing” she had found while researching cancer care, she said she “started juicing and doing things like coffee enemas and infrared saunas, high-dose vitamin C, and my life started to change.”</p>
<p>She started a juice line Alchemy Organics, offering three-day juice and drink packages like the $265 <a href="https://www.alchemyorganics.com/products/the-alchemist-cleanse?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">Alchemist Cleanse</a>.</p>
<p>Calling juice her “natural medicine,” she wrote in an <a href="https://www.instagram.com/reels/DEsDR77xUEY/?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">Instagram video</a> last year that she “opened an organic, raw, cold-pressed juice shop called Alchemy Organics. 5 years later + I am now 100% CANCER FREE and continue to rely on juice as a wellness tool.” Her company did not respond to requests for comment, and Brown did not directly address the company’s claims to The Post.</p>
<p>She also has advertised Hope4Cancer, the operator of alternative treatment centers in Cancún and Tijuana that <a href="https://hope4cancer.com/our-approach/research-and-outcomes/?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">says</a> it prefers “to focus on non-toxic, natural treatments” over conventional cancer care. Hope4Cancer notes that “in some instances, it may become necessary to recommend targeted conventional approaches,” and Brown said in an <a href="https://www.instagram.com/reels/DF0L8xNtZdJ/?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">Instagram video</a> last year promoting the company that conventional cancer treatments “have their place.” But Brown said she was grateful she had ignored her doctors’ advice to pursue surgery and radiation and had chosen an “alternative approach” featuring colonics and “spiritual healing” that she said had “empowered [her] body to heal itself.”</p>
<p>“I learned that there is SO much MORE you can do. But you won’t hear it from most oncologists because healing isn’t profitable — pharmaceuticals are,” she said. “There’s no billion-dollar industry behind coffee enemas, high-dose Vitamin C, hyperthermia, or detox protocols. But these therapies work.”</p>
<p>On her <a href="https://carlybrownwellness.com/about?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">website</a> and <a href="https://thyroidpharmacist.com/articles/podcast/ep-58/?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">in</a> <a href="https://www.buzzsprout.com/2013301/episodes/16766243-dance-like-your-cells-are-watching-with-carly-shankman?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">podcasts</a>, Brown said that she achieved reduction of her thyroid cancer through holistic methods but that the cancer progressed during her second pregnancy, during which she “chose to move forward” with a more conventional thyroidectomy. She said that she continued to go to Hope4Cancer afterward and “follow the same non-toxic, integrative therapies,” and that in 2023 she learned she was “healed” and “officially cancer free.”</p>

<p>Gorski, the cancer surgeon, disputed the claim that any of those measures could help with cancer care. Studies in the <a href="https://academic.oup.com/jnci/article/110/1/121/4064136?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">Journal of the National Cancer Institute</a> and the <a href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1091255X26002337?via%3Dihub=&amp;itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">Journal of Gastrointestinal Surgery</a> found that patients who relied on alternative cancer treatments had lower survival rates than those who pursued conventional care.</p>
<p>But Hope4Cancer has also faced legal challenges over its treatment of ailing patients. In 2015, the estates of <a href="https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.txnd.262532/gov.uscourts.txnd.262532.15.0.pdf?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">Margaret Nichols</a>, a 47-year-old in Texas with breast cancer, and <a href="https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.txnd.262531/gov.uscourts.txnd.262531.22.0.pdf?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">Allen Reuben</a>, a Dallas doctor with brain cancer, sued the company, its founder Antonio Jimenez and a Utah man named Dewayne Smith who developed a treatment offered by the company, alleging Hope4Cancer had induced them to spend money by promising a cure or a stoppage of the growth of their cancers, only for them to die within months of beginning treatment at the center.</p>
<p>The Reuben estate said it <a href="https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.txnd.262531/gov.uscourts.txnd.262531.30.0.pdf?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">settled</a> with Hope4Cancer and Jimenez in 2016, and a judge <a href="https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.txnd.262531/gov.uscourts.txnd.262531.36.0.pdf?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">dismissed</a> its case against Smith later that year on jurisdictional grounds, court filings show. The Nichols estate case was settled in 2017, according to a joint stipulation of dismissal <a href="https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/13382295/estate-of-margaret-johnson-nichols-v-hope4cancer-institute-llc/?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">filing</a>, which said both sides had resolved the case “to their mutual satisfaction.” The terms of the settlements were not publicly disclosed. The attorney for both estates did not respond to requests for comment.</p>
<p>A Hope4Cancer official said that the company believes everyone deserves “the freedom to pursue a treatment path aligned with their values and health needs” and that, “as a matter of confidentiality, we will not discuss private health journeys, past patient matters, or private organizational relationships.”</p>
<p>Brown said that she attended Hope4Cancer as a patient in 2019, having raised money for it through a GoFundMe account, and that she couldn’t comment on the clinic’s legal issues. “I can only speak to my own experience as a patient. I’m just a mom sharing what’s helped me,” she said.</p>
<p>Jimenez did not respond to requests for comment. His attorney on the case, Doug Dube, confirmed that it was settled but declined to comment further because he said he was no longer authorized to speak on Jimenez’s behalf.</p>
<p>In 2022, Smith <a href="https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.utd.135506/gov.uscourts.utd.135506.20.0.pdf?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">pleaded guilty</a> to three charges of selling misbranded drugs following claims that he had sold an alternative medical treatment called the Bx Protocol to people with cancer and chronic illnesses, generating more than $1.5 million in revenue between 2017 and 2018 despite federal tests that suggested the “miracle drug” contained nothing “other than water, bacteria and mold,” a <a href="https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.utd.135506/gov.uscourts.utd.135506.15.0_1.pdf?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">federal sentencing filing says</a>. Smith’s attorney said his client believed the treatment could help the chronically ill, but internal documents cited by prosecutors said 179 of the 550 people who used Bx Protocol died while taking it, according to the sentencing filing.</p>
<p>Smith disputed prosecutors’ claims in a statement to The Post, saying he had pleaded guilty only to misbranding. He said that most of the people who died were already in hospice care, and that the organization did not profit “heavily” and instead “operated close to break-even.” The prosecutors’ sentencing filing does acknowledge that the government “also identified customers who had positive experiences with Bx Protocol and Smith.”</p>
<p>Smith also said he had pursued studies to examine the treatment’s effectiveness.</p>
<p>“We were doing studies precisely because we wanted data, not testimonials,” he added. “Please don’t group us with organizations like [Hope4Cancer] that rely on anecdote.”</p>
<p>Joshua Sharfstein, who served as principal deputy commissioner of the Food and Drug Administration during the Obama administration, warned that there is an inherent danger if someone gives people with cancer false hope or encourages them to delay proven medical care. “Even a harmless product that keeps someone from using a needed treatment can be quite dangerous,” he said.</p>
<h2 data-component="Heading" data-contentid="23SQXECK2NHPLKJ3LVTCDDS53E">“Ripoff profiteering”</h2>
<p>The Post evaluated the supplements whose companies were promoted by the MAHA figures by using a “scorecard” from the U.S. military for service members called <a href="https://www.opss.org/opss-scorecard?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">Operation Supplement Safety</a>. Pieter Cohen, an associate professor of medicine at Harvard Medical School who studies the safety of dietary supplements, suggested The Post evaluate supplements using this tool.</p>
<p>Presented as a seven-question quiz, the test was developed by the Defense Department to offer “evidence-based guidance” for service members trying to decide whether a supplement is safe to consume. Among the <a href="https://www.opss.org/opss-scorecard?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">criteria</a>: The supplement should carry the label of a third-party certification group such as NSF; feature fewer than six ingredients, all of them easy to pronounce, on its Supplement Facts label; feature 200 milligrams or less of caffeine per serving; not include “questionable claims or statements” or terms such as “proprietary,” “blend,” “matrix” or “complex” on its nutrition label; and provide information showing how its ingredients should factor into a daily diet, with all of those “daily value” percentages equaling less than 200 percent.</p>
<p>A Pentagon official confirmed that the DOD continues to use the scorecard as an educational risk-assessment tool as part of the department’s “steadfast commitment to the long-term health and operational readiness of the joint force.”</p>
<p>The Post’s analysis found that at least 15 of the brands promoted by the set of MAHA influencers sold one or more supplements that probably would not get a passing score.</p>
<p>NeuroEffect — a supplement by Paleovalley, which frequently sponsored Clark’s podcast — would be marked a “no-go” because it does not provide nutritional information for how its ingredients should factor into a daily diet, is not certified by one of the outside groups that the military trusts, uses the word “complex” on the label and has more than six ingredients, including ones that are hard to pronounce, the analysis found.</p>

<p>Some of the MAHA champions have voiced their support for one of the checklist’s criteria: using or consuming only foods with ingredients they can pronounce. In 2023, Clark said in her video “<a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_5x9lj6sxdk&amp;itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">Seed Oils Are Turning You Into A LOSER</a>” that her rule was: “If I cannot pronounce it, I don’t know what it is. And if I don’t know what it is, I’m not eating it.”</p>
<p>But Clark has also advertised skincare products with hard-to-pronounce ingredients such as <a href="https://www.regenere.com/pages/ingredients?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">Myristoyl Hexapeptide-23</a> and drinkable supplements featuring <a href="https://www.rowecasaorganics.com/products/antioxidant-infusion-16-oz-1?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">liposomal glutathione extract</a>.</p>
<p>Minas, the Turning Point marketing chief, said Clark has since dropped Paleovalley as a sponsor. Paleovalley, which did not respond to requests for comment, notes on its <a href="https://paleovalley.com/store/neuro-effect?srsltid=AYCX9vJaFBS7NoRJPxBVCwg4u2wrjDQDRJWmcmdGZMPjtV1747VCIP1o&amp;itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">website</a> that while NeuroEffect “doesn’t make any claims you will suddenly have a super Einstein like brain, it was created to ‘work’.”</p>
<p>Daniel Powers, a co-founder of Utzy Naturals, the maker of the “Microplastic Daily Detox” supplements Clark has advertised, said his “independent, family-run supplement company” worked with “creators in the health and wellness space based on their audience and interest in our products, not based on political affiliation.” The company, he said, recognizes “that the science surrounding microplastics is still developing, particularly when it comes to human interventions,” and that it does “not represent the product as a drug, treatment or cure.”</p>
<p>Clark’s post Powers added, was “her own description and not a claim made by Utzy.”</p>
<p>Many of the influencers promoted or sold supplements to their followers, portraying them as clean alternatives to what they’ve said are the toxic failures of Big Food. But Arthur Caplan, professor emeritus at the New York University Grossman School of Medicine, expressed dismay that so many prominent MAHA voices — including the physicians <a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/health/2024/12/02/dr-oz-medicare-medicaid-ozempic-conflicts/?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">Mehmet Oz</a>, now head of the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, and <a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/health/2025/10/09/casey-means-surgeon-general-nominee/?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">Means</a> — promoted supplements, an industry he pointed out that is regulated less than your local restaurant.</p>
<p>Caplan, a prominent voice in medical ethics, said MAHA influencers who sell supplements and the like that are not backed by science have engaged in “completely dishonest, unethical ripoff profiteering.”</p>
<p>A CMS spokesperson said in a statement that Oz “credits his social media team with producing informative, engaging content that reaches Americans where they are.”</p>
<h2 data-component="Heading" data-contentid="WD656UTUBVDW7JMZOUXKLVWZHM">“Kids being kids”</h2>
<p>The Post also found 14 instances of products that advocacy groups and others argued merit disclosure for lead under California’s Proposition 65 standard, which requires companies to label products that pose an increased health risk due to elevated levels of potentially hazardous chemicals. Two of the companies note on their website that at least one of their products requires such a label, and three other companies state that such labels do not necessarily make products unsafe.</p>
<p>Public health experts interviewed by The Post noted that California is much stricter about lead exposure than other states, requiring disclosure on products that could expose consumers to more than 0.5 micrograms of lead. Infants and young children face the greatest risk, and many of them are already experiencing low-level exposure that could cause dementia or other damage as they age, according to an <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41676992/?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">FDA study</a> and <a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/climate-environment/2026/09/15/how-lead-1970s-gasoline-exhaust-may-increase-your-dementia-risk-today/?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">recent medical research</a>.</p>
<p>There is no safe level of lead exposure, and even low levels of exposure to the neurotoxin can damage organs and disrupt brain development, according to the <a href="https://www.cdc.gov/lead-prevention/about/index.html?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">Centers for Disease Control and Prevention</a> and the <a href="https://www.epa.gov/lead/learn-about-lead?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">Environmental Protection Agency</a>. Thomas Galligan, principal scientist for food additives and supplements at Center for Science in the Public Interest, said the labels offer an important warning sign for products that could pose hazards for reproductive health. “You see lead contamination in the supplements, that should raise some alarm bells,” he said.</p>
<p>Vani Hari, a top MAHA figure known as Food Babe who decries the ultra-processed nature of American food, missed the cutoff of The Post’s analyzed group because her already-large follower count climbed about 30 percent over the time period. She co-founded the brand Truvani, which promises “real food without added chemicals” or “toxins.” Bigtree has highlighted the brand. Last year, however, the watchdog group CalSafe Research Center sent the company a <a href="https://oag.ca.gov/prop65/60-day-notice-2025-03435?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">letter</a> alleging that its organic vanilla chai plant-based protein powder had violated California’s lead standards by purportedly failing to provide a warning to purchasers.</p>

<p>Hari did not respond to requests for comment. In a Facebook video she <a href="https://www.facebook.com/thefoodbabe/videos/every-time-i-see-truvani-on-walmart-shelves-i-am-reminded-that-ordinary-people-l/1415560660317171/?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">posted</a> in February showing her holding up one of the brand’s vanilla protein powders, she wrote: “Every time I see @Truvani on @Walmart shelves, I am reminded that ordinary people like you and me can change the food system. No toxic chemicals.”</p>
<p>Truvani did not respond to requests for comment but said on its website earlier this year that it <a href="https://faq.truvani.com/en-US/is-it-tested-for-heavy-metals-and-other-contaminants-1905949?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">tests regularly</a> for lead and other metals and that <a href="https://faq.truvani.com/en-US/is-there-any-process-in-place-to-ensure-it-doesnt-contain-mold-1905950?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">all of its products</a> abide by Proposition 65 standards.</p>
<p>Asked about the letter to Truvani, Bigtree said that he knew Hari as a “heartfelt and passionate mom” who cared about promoting safe and healthy products and that he believed she was one of the “people who do things in the right way.” He added that he has spoken to people who have discussed the difficulty of acquiring ingredients that are perfectly safe, given the challenges of modern manufacturing.</p>
<p>Another company, Serenity Kids, which promises “clean nutrition” on its baby and toddler food, received a <a href="https://advocacy.consumerreports.org/research/cr-letter-calling-on-serenity-kids-to-comply-with-californias-heavy-metals-in-baby-food-disclosure-law/?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">letter</a> last year from Consumer Reports urging it to fully disclose to the public the results of tests for lead and other heavy metals from its baby food products. This year, the watchdog group Environmental Research Center <a href="https://oag.ca.gov/system/files/prop65/notices/2026-04473.pdf?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">alleged</a> that the company had products, including children’s-food pouches, that carried levels of lead warranting Proposition 65 disclosure. Swan had <a href="https://www.instagram.com/reels/DNtmO7CZHZK/?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">promoted</a> the company’s toddler formula on Instagram as one of the “cleanest options out there.”</p>

<p>A company representative previously <a href="https://www.consumerreports.org/health/food-safety/lesser-evil-serenity-kids-cassava-puffs-high-lead-levels-a2654657249/?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">told Consumer Reports</a> that it “is always working with our suppliers to reduce the heavy metal content of our products,” but that “heavy metals are prevalent in our food system.”</p>
<p>Warsh encouraged direct consumption of a different metal linked to harmful health effects by promoting Sovereign Silver, liquid drops featuring small particles of silver that the <a href="https://sovereignsilver.com/?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">company says</a> “supercharges your everyday wellness.”</p>
<p>Warsh said in an <a href="https://www.instagram.com/reels/DVJbhD9EfDz/?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">Instagram video</a> that the drinkable silver offers “natural immune support that fits into everyday life: school, travel, busy schedules, and real kids being kids.”</p>
<p>The National Institutes of Health said in an <a href="https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/colloidal-silver-what-you-need-to-know?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">advisory notice</a> updated in 2023 that colloidal silver isn’t safe or effective for treating any health condition and that it can cause serious side effects, including kidney, liver, or nervous system problems and can permanently change the skin’s color to a bluish gray.</p>
<p>Stephanie Kaul, a Sovereign Silver representative, said the company works with “thousands of health and wellness content creators from all walks of life,” including Warsh, and that its internal tests have shown that “there is no evidence establishing a causal relationship between Sovereign Silver and any reported adverse health outcomes.”</p>
<p>Dan Pearce, the chief executive of the brand’s creator, Sovereign Naturals, said in a statement that the company has received thousands of positive reviews from customers “who have made Sovereign Silver part of their wellness routines.”</p>
<p>Warsh did not respond to requests for comment, but he said in an <a href="https://www.instagram.com/reels/DVJbhD9EfDz/?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">Instagram post</a> that the product was “part of our family’s wellness routine.”</p>
<h2 data-component="Heading" data-contentid="BJMLLU54RVD5HIJWNFL5CJY5PA">“At your own risk”</h2>
<p>Government authorities have flagged some of the promoted products reviewed by The Post as potentially risky.</p>
<p>In January 2025, Clark, for instance, <a href="https://www.instagram.com/reels/DEoKaxovalc/?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">featured</a> White Oak Pastures, a regenerative family farm in Georgia that says its “grassfed” beef is raised without mRNA vaccines. The following year, the U.S. Department of Agriculture posted a <a href="https://www.fsis.usda.gov/recalls-alerts/fsis-issues-public-health-alert-ground-beef-products-due-possible-foreign-material?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">public health alert</a> about its ground beef and said it “may be contaminated with foreign material, specifically metal,” following consumer complaints.</p>
<p>Will Harris, the owner of White Oak Pastures, said in an interview that the alert was sent after metal birdshot pellets were discovered in beef from at least one of the cows raised on the farm’s 3,500 acres of land. He said that farm employees do not shoot the animals and don’t know where the birdshot had come from, and that this was the only time metal had been discovered in its beef over the decades he’s run the farm. Asked about the Clark promotion, he said the farm had no advertising budget but happily worked with anyone who could bring attention to their work.</p>
<p>In May 2026, Swan promoted a homeopathic medicine firm called Boiron that received a <a href="https://www.fda.gov/inspections-compliance-enforcement-and-criminal-investigations/warning-letters/boiron-inc-663402-09112023?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">warning letter</a> in 2023 from the FDA over its homeopathic eye drops and was <a href="https://centerforinquiry.s3.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/13113556/BoironAnswer-2022-09-08.pdf?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">sued in 2022</a> by the Center for Inquiry, a scientific watchdog group, for <a href="https://cdn.centerforinquiry.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/14130827/CFI-v-Boiron-Complaint-FINAL.pdf?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">allegedly using</a> “a carefully crafted scheme of misrepresentation” to offload “otherwise worthless products upon the unwitting, the ill-informed and the vulnerable.”</p>
<p>Boiron denied the lawsuit’s allegations and accused the group of trying to limit “the options of consumers to healthcare-related products,” according to a <a href="https://centerforinquiry.s3.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/13113556/BoironAnswer-2022-09-08.pdf?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">legal response</a>. A non-jury trial is scheduled for March, D.C. Superior Court records show.</p>
<p>Alissa Gould, a Boiron spokeswoman, said in a statement that the warning letter was one of the dozens of letters concerning eye-care products sent by the FDA to both conventional and homeopathic over-the-counter drugs and that the company discontinued a line of eye drops “due to the heightened FDA scrutiny of eye-care products and out of an abundance of caution.” She noted that the FDA has taken no enforcement action against Boiron for any of its products.</p>
<p>Boiron, Gould said, paid Swan to create a short Instagram video promoting an allergy medicine after she had posted on her own about her “positive experiences with Boiron products.” The company, she added, “selects potential partners based on their prior content, alignment with our company, and audience.”</p>
<p>Of the Center for Inquiry lawsuit, Gould said the company “looks forward to vigorously defending ourselves against the meritless allegations.”</p>
<p>Richard Conn, the general counsel for the Center for Inquiry, said in a statement that the lawsuit “is not about restricting a consumer’s ability to purchase a product; it’s about consumers’ rights to the truth about what they’re purchasing and putting into their bodies.”</p>
<p>Both Clark and Swan had promoted Primally Pure, which promises “mindfully made skincare” including “charcoal deodorant” and a “grass-fed tallow” balm and sunscreen. Clark was a frequent promoter, advertising Primally Pure more than 50 times in less than two years.</p>
<p>In September 2025,the Better Business Bureau’s National Programs’ National Advertising Division, an industry self-regulatory group, <a href="https://bbbprograms.org/media/newsroom/decisions/primally-pure?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">referred the company</a> to the FTC for “review and possible enforcement action,” claiming the company had failed to substantively respond to the group’s requests to substantiate its claims about its Primally Pure Sun Cream.</p>
<p>Phyllis Marcus, the industry group’s vice president, told The Post that officials there had been concerned about the company’s claims that the Sun Cream was “better and safer” than conventional sunscreens, which it said were “poison” and made of “toxic” ingredients that can hurt people’s health.</p>
<p>Primally Pure did not respond to requests for comment but says on its website that its sunscreen does not contain “<a href="https://primallypure.com/products/sun-cream-spf30?variant=46653686481066&amp;country=US¤cy=USD&amp;utm_medium=product_sync&amp;utm_source=google&amp;utm_content=sag_organic&amp;utm_campaign=sag_organic&amp;source=aw&amp;sv1=affiliate&amp;sv_campaign_id=301377&amp;tw_source=awin&amp;tw_campaign=301377&amp;awc=112758_1793940284_089dbd6f664207dfca9623d2579cecc3&amp;itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">industrialized seed oils</a><u>,</u>” which it calls “unstable, inflammation-prone ingredients,” and says its product meets the “<a href="https://primallypure.com/products/sun-cream-spf30?variant=46653686481066&amp;country=US¤cy=USD&amp;utm_medium=product_sync&amp;utm_source=google&amp;utm_content=sag_organic&amp;utm_campaign=sag_organic&amp;source=aw&amp;sv1=affiliate&amp;sv_campaign_id=301377&amp;tw_source=awin&amp;tw_campaign=301377&amp;awc=112758_1793940284_089dbd6f664207dfca9623d2579cecc3&amp;itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">least toxic</a>” criteria. In its website <a href="https://primallypure.com/pages/terms-of-service?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">terms of service</a>, the company writes, “Any reliance on the material on this site is at your own risk.” Neither Swan nor Clark’s spokesperson directly addressed their endorsement of Primally Pure.</p>
<p>The FTC declined to comment.</p>
<p>In one case, the federal government penalized a supplement company for false claims. In April, Warsh shared an <a href="https://www.instagram.com/p/DXH12gcAclc/?img_index=4&amp;itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">Instagram</a> post promoting TruHeight, the maker of a supplement it had once advertised on social media could “<a href="https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/TruHeight-Complaint.pdf?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">help your child grow taller</a>,” one day after the FTC <a href="https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/04/ftc-takes-action-against-truheight-deceptive-unsubstantiated-advertising-supposed-height-enhancing?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">announced a $4 million judgment</a> against the company, alleging it had been deceptively advertising for years about its products’ effectiveness in boosting people’s height. The FTC <a href="https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/07/ftc-approves-final-order-against-truheight-deceptive-unsubstantiated-advertising-supplements-kids?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">announced</a> in July that the company would pay $750,000, based on its “inability to pay the full amount,” and would be barred “from making false or unsupported health claims and using fake or incentivized consumer reviews.”</p>
<p>The company primarily based its promises on a single company-sponsored study of 32 people that “showed no meaningful differences between the treatment and control groups,” the FTC <a href="https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/blog/2026/04/protecting-kids-deceptive-unsubstantiated-health-claims-ftcs-case-against-truheight?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">alleged</a>, and thousands of its glowing reviews were written by employees or bot accounts. Warsh has promoted the supplement at least 12 times since the judgment was announced.</p>
<p>TruHeight co-founder Eden Stelmach said the company “immediately accepted responsibility and implemented a series of changes to improve our advertising practices” after the FTC opened its investigation of TruHeight’s ad claims in 2024.</p>
<p>Stelmach said the company agreed to the non-monetary provisions of the FTC settlement last year, before it was announced in April, and that the paid promotional campaign with Warsh was commissioned and scheduled long before the settlement announcement, calling the timing “only a coincidence.”</p>
<p>Stelmach said the company no longer claims to “make children taller” but instead offers products that “can help children reach their natural growth potential by meeting their nutritional needs during the growing years.”</p>
<p>Stelmach said the company has had a paid promotional agreement with Warsh for its Kids Essentials Kit, a package of its “bone growth” gummies, protein powder and sleep drops, that requires all of his content be submitted for review before publication. He added that the company has “no political or partisan agenda” and that it does not view Warsh as a “MAHA influencer.”</p>
<p>“To us, he is a highly qualified, experienced pediatrician who has taken the time to learn about TruHeight’s products,” Stelmach said.</p>
<p>Warsh did not respond to requests for comment. A testimonial on <a href="https://www.truheightvitamins.com/?itid=lk_inline_enhanced-template">TruHeight’s website</a> quotes Warsh saying he is “glad to be working with TruHeight because they understand something I tell families every day: nutrition is one of the most powerful and overlooked tools we have in supporting kids’ growth, development, and long-term health.”</p>
<p>Sharfstein, the former principal deputy FDA commissioner, said influencers’ promotion of overhyped or disreputable products could mislead the consumers who saw them as unconventional truthtellers and trusted they could guide them to a better life.</p>
<p>“What troubles me is that this part of the movement seems to be defining itself in opposition to the kinds of standards that have helped separate ineffective treatments from products that actually work,” Sharfstein said.</p>

<p>Methodology</p>
<p>The Post analyzed 37,000 social media posts, podcasts and newsletters from 18 figures affiliated with the MAHA movement, from 2024 through late October 2026, to find posts promoting products or brands with whom the person had an apparent commercial relationship.</p>
<p>The Post started by selecting a group of more than 80 people who identified as supporting “MAHA” or Kennedy’s movement in posts and had at least 100,000 followers on a major social media platform, including prominent MAHA advocates who discussed topics like nutrition, pesticides and vaccines as well as MAHA-identifying administration officials. The Post winnowed the list to solely include the 18 whose Instagram follower count increased by 50 percent or more in the nearly two years from August 2024, before MAHA’s rise to prominence, to June 2026. The Post then reviewed the posts and podcasts from those 18 to find promotions. We excluded accounts by Robert F. Kennedy Jr. because he became secretary of Health and Human Services.</p>
<p>Identifying ads is difficult because the Federal Trade Commission’s requirement that influencers disclose any financial relationships with endorsed companies is sparsely enforced and often ignored, though the FTC has issued warnings against influencers and their clients before. Some influencers also may endorse products without being paid to do so or may criticize products, especially mainstream pharmaceutical and food companies, that they dislike – so mere mentions of a brand aren’t enough to assume a relationship.</p>
<p>Using Claude Haiku 4.5, we identified posts that mentioned an apparent commercial relationship with a brand, including explicitly disclosing a sponsorship relationship or gifted products, posting an influencer-specific promo code, promoting a product sold on their own web store or thanking a brand for “support.” This AI identification process had two steps: first, identifying any with an apparent potential monetization avenue (this had an accuracy rate of 94 percent when measured against a hand-labeled set of 320 posts), then further classifying whether posts included clear disclosure of sponsored content. This second step, using GPT 5.4 Mini, had an accuracy rate of 95 percent when compared to a hand-labeled set of 196 posts; we then manually reviewed posts showing each influencer-brand relationship and corrected the errors that we found – ensuring that our data includes zero false positives.</p>
<p>Finally, we included in our analysis any posts where there was a clear commercial relationship between the MAHA proponent and the brand – even if they did not disclose that relationship in a specific social media post. For instance, influencers whose podcast is sponsored by a specific brand would have any social media posts mentioning that product included in our dataset.</p>
