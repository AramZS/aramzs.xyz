title: Bitter Clash Over Fossil Fuels Derails U.N. Climate Talks

<p>European and vulnerable nations rejected a proposed agreement after Saudi Arabia and other oil producers blocked any mention of phasing out coal, oil and gas.</p><figure><img alt="The audience in an auditorium watches a large screen showing Darragh O’Brien in a blue suit." src="https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/11/22/multimedia/22cli-cop-final2-bpvk/22cli-cop-final2-bpvk-articleLarge.jpg"/><figcaption>Darragh O’Brien, Ireland’s climate, environment and energy minister, speaking at COP31 in Antalya, Turkey, on Sunday. Khalil Hamra/Associated Press</figcaption></figure><p>The United Nations climate talks were on the brink of collapse on Sunday aslong-building geopolitical tensions paralyzed the negotiations, pitting nations seeking to wind down the era of fossil fuels against those aiming to prolong it.</p><p>European nations, as well as many smaller countries vulnerable to climate disasters and rising sea levels, rejected a proposed agreement they said stripped out crucial protections and favored countries that export oil, gas and coal. A group of oil producers, led by Saudi Arabia, had blocked any mention of fossil fuels, the root cause of global warming.</p><p>That the talks were derailed by something so elemental shows the vast challenges facing the global process for addressing climate change. After nearly two weeks of jockeying in a conference center in Turkey, sleep-deprived negotiators fought two days past the talks’ official deadline, rebooking flights, bargaining in closed-door meetings, to see if Russia, Saudi Arabia and other Arab nations might loosen their opposition to language about fossil fuels.</p><p>“It fails to meet the moment,” said Darragh O’Brien, Ireland’s climate, environment and energy minister, speaking on behalf of the European Union. Mr. O’Brien and Wopke Hoekstra, the European climate commissioner, said all efforts to negotiate a compromise in Antalya had been blocked. “We will not accept a COP that greenwashes fossil fuel,” Mr. O’Brien said.</p><p>He called it “unconscionable that we would have a climate pact that makes no reference to fossil fuel.” As diplomats spoke, COP31  became the longest U.N. climate summit in history, surpassing one held in 2019 that went nearly 45 hours into overtime.</p><p>Negotiators from more than 190 countries meet every year to advance the goals of the 2015 Paris Agreement.</p><p>There have been just two previous times in three decades of annual global climate talks when nations failed to reach agreement. Coming at a low point for global cooperation, the standoff deepened doubts about whether the United Nations process  is up to the task of confronting an overheating planet.</p><p>Even in its best moments, that process hasn’t been easy. With its lawyerly fights over wording and long history of making pledges that under-deliver, many have long questioned whether the process is viable.</p><p>It has been further complicated by the exodus of the United States under Mr. Trump from the global effort to fight climate change.  Some participants say that in that vacuum, other countries have staked out harder positions, leaving the process gridlocked.</p><p>Countries in 2023 had clinched a celebrated agreement to “transition away” from coal, oil and gas — what organizers called the “beginning of the end” of the fossil fuel era. But in three COPs since then, no final agreement has directly mentioned fossil fuels. Canada is fast-tracking an oil pipeline. Australia has approved new projects that would last for decades. And the United States, under Mr. Trump, has used trade policy to promote fossil fuels abroad.</p><p>“Fossil fuel-producing countries are banding together, no matter which part of the world they come from,” said Lynda Tabuya, Fiji’s climate and environment minister.</p><p>That’s left countries that favor a transition to wind, solar and other clean energy to work outside the formal U.N. system, with a series of conferences and domestic pledges that gained some momentum in Antalya. Denmark, Spain and Portugal were among the nations that announced new plans this month.</p><p>At the same time, a proposal in the proposed text had called to increase the deployment of technology to capture and store carbon dioxide emissions.</p><p>A group of Arab countries led by Saudi Arabia that sought such language argued that it would be impossible to keep global temperatures at safe levels without carbon removal. Critics called it a license for fossil fuel developers to continue burning coal, oil and gas rather than shift to renewables.</p><p>A Saudi delegate, in a closing plenary, described the proposed text as a “package of solutions that would have advanced climate solutions.”</p><p>The talks, known this year as COP31, came two months after the United Nations acknowledged failure to meet the world’s most ambitious climate goal: limiting temperature increase to 1.5 degrees Celsius above preindustrial levels. Some diplomats said the conference’s credibility would depend on mounting a response to that breach and to the intensifying heat waves, droughts and floods that will come with it.</p><p>Holding temperatures to the “lowest possible overshoot” would require swift and immediate cuts to greenhouse emissions, the United Nations has said.</p><p>Mr. O’Brien Mr. Hoekstra said they and the leaders of negotiating groups representing of about 130 countries repeatedly tried to incorporate language that acknowledged a global effort to transition away from fossil fuels but were “blocked” by oil producing nations.</p><p>“There were no measures that were going to bridge that gap and get us back down toward 1.5,” Mr. Hoekstra said.</p><p>Governments have been focused on bringing down the soaring costs of fuel, which have been driven sharply higher by the United States and Israeli war in Iran, disruptions in the Strait of Hormuz and refining bottlenecks.</p><p>While climate negotiators worked in Antalya, President Trump, President Xi Jinping of China and President Vladimir V. Putin of Russia met at a lakeside villa in Shenzhen, China, and agreed to “<a href="https://www.whitehouse.gov/briefings-statements/2026/11/u-s-russia-china-heads-of-state-declaration-toward-peace/">increase the production and supply</a>” of petroleum. The United States, China and Russia represent three of the four largest emitters.</p><p>In an unusual twist, two countries this year had a hand in the event, with Turkey hosting but an Australian minister in charge of shepherding negotiations. But as talks went past the deadline, it became hard for delegates to tell who was doing what. Organizers didn’t convene all nations together to hash things out. Public communication stopped. Groups came in and out of backrooms. “It’s not effective and it can’t build consensus,” said Xolisa Joseph Ngwadla, a negotiator from Botswana. “The process is basically mismanaged.”</p><p>The impasse at COP31 comes amid concerns that the consensus-based process is no longer functioning. Johan Rockström, the director of the Potsdam Institute for Climate Impact Research in Germany, wrote in an online post that the unanimity requirement was the “real Achilles' heel” of the process and said a handful of countries were deliberately putting “spanners in the wheel.”</p><p>Former Vice President Al Gore, who was in Turkey for the beginning of the talks, said it was “insane” that any individual country could “block the entire country.” Mr. Gore suggested supermajority voting.</p><p>Since the landmark Paris Agreement in 2015, the world has edged away from a worst-case scenario: a temperature increase of 4 degrees Celsius, or 7.2 degrees Fahrenheit. But even the current trajectory, of around 2.8 degrees, or 5 degrees Fahrenheit, is a “recipe for catastrophe,” António Guterres, the United Nations secretary general, has said. Nations have fallen well short of their emissions-cutting pledges, but have gotten a boost from the plummeting cost of solar and wind energy, and from Chinese manufacturers supplying the world with electric vehicles.</p>
