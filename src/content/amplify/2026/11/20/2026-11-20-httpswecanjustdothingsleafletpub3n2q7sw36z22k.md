<p>There is something slightly disappointing about Bluesky introducing view counts.</p>
<p>Not because knowing how many people see your posts is inherently bad, but because it feels like an unexamined import from a model of social networking that deserves much more scrutiny.</p>
<p>The feature allows users to see how many times their posts have been viewed. The numbers are private: only the author can see them. Bluesky says it has responded to requests from users who want to understand the reach of their posts.</p>
<p>That's a reasonable explanation. People want to know whether their contributions are reaching an audience.</p>
<p>But the fact that people want a feature doesn't necessarily mean introducing it is good for the network. View counts aren't just a vanity metric. They risk changing the behaviour and culture of the network itself.</p>
<p>I think this is a questionable direction for a social network that has an opportunity to do things differently. I would like to see Bluesky asking a more fundamental question:</p>
<p>What kind of behaviour does this encourage, and what kind of social network does this help to create?</p>

<h2 data-index="13"><a data-canvas-passive="true" href="https://wecanjustdothings.leaflet.pub/3n2q7sw36z22k#metrics-change-the-thing-they-measure">Metrics change the thing they measure</a></h2>
<p>A view count appears to be a neutral piece of information. It tells you how many times a post has been displayed. Nothing more. But measurements rarely remain neutral once people begin using them to evaluate their own performance.</p>
<p>Introduce a number and people start interpreting it. Is it good? Is it bad? Why did this post get more views than the last one? Is someone else's content reaching a larger audience? What did I do differently? What should I post next time?</p>
<p>Gradually, numbers become targets. And once it becomes a target, it starts influencing the behaviour it was originally intended to measure.</p>
<p>A 2021 experimental study [<a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2021.106940">Emotional responses to likes and comments regulate posting frequency and content change behaviour on social media: An experimental study and mediation model</a>] examined how likes and comments influence subsequent posting behaviour. Researchers found that receiving more engagement than expected was associated with positive emotional responses and intentions to post more frequently. Receiving less engagement than expected was associated with negative emotions and changes in subsequent content choices.</p>
<p>The research concerned likes and comments rather than view counts but it illustrates an important principle: social feedback doesn't simply tell us how our contributions performed. It can influence what we choose to contribute next. View counts introduce another feedback mechanism into that relationship.</p>
<h2 data-index="20"><a data-canvas-passive="true" href="https://wecanjustdothings.leaflet.pub/3n2q7sw36z22k#the-attention-economy-doesnt-need-to-be-public">The attention economy doesn't need to be public</a></h2>
<p>Bluesky's decision to keep view counts private is a useful distinction. It avoids the obvious problem of turning every post into a public popularity contest. Users cannot inspect someone else's numbers and compare their reach directly.</p>
<p>But privacy doesn't remove the underlying incentives.</p>
<p>If I can see that one post received 10,000 views and another received 500, I now have information that invites comparison and interpretation. I might begin to favour the subjects, opinions, formats or styles that generate higher numbers. I might become more conscious of my audience, more calculated in what I publish, and less willing to share something that I suspect will receive little attention.</p>
<p>None of this requires an algorithm to tell me what to post. The metric itself can become a source of behavioural pressure.</p>
<p>This is one of the more interesting aspects of social media design: a platform doesn't necessarily need to make competition visible to introduce competitive behaviour. Sometimes giving people a way to measure themselves is enough.</p>
<h2 data-index="27"><a data-canvas-passive="true" href="https://wecanjustdothings.leaflet.pub/3n2q7sw36z22k#reach-is-not-the-same-as-value">Reach is not the same as value</a></h2>
<p>There is also a fundamental problem with treating views as a meaningful indicator of success. A view tells us very little about the experience that follows it. It doesn't tell us whether someone read the post, understood it, agreed with it, learned something from it or found it worthwhile.</p>
<p>A thoughtful contribution seen by 50 people might generate a genuinely interesting discussion. Another post might receive 50,000 views and leave almost nothing behind. Both have reach. Their value may be entirely different.</p>
<p>Social networks are not simply publishing platforms. Their value comes from the relationships, exchanges, discoveries and communities that develop through participation. If we measure the success of that experience primarily through exposure, we risk confusing the distribution of content with the quality of social interaction.</p>
<p>A 2025 study [<a href="https://doi.org/10.1093/pnasnexus/pgaf062">Engagement, user satisfaction, and the amplification of divisive content on social media</a>] examined engagement-based ranking on Twitter. Through an algorithmic audit, the researchers found that engagement-based ranking amplified emotionally charged, hostile content about political out-groups compared with a reverse-chronological feed. They also found that users did not necessarily prefer the content selected by the engagement-based ranking system.</p>
<p>This study examines algorithmic amplification, not private view counts, but it demonstrates the broader danger of treating measurable engagement as a reliable proxy for what people actually want or what makes a healthier online experience.</p>
<p>What performs well according to a metric and what contributes positively to a community are not necessarily the same thing.</p>
<h2 data-index="35"><a data-canvas-passive="true" href="https://wecanjustdothings.leaflet.pub/3n2q7sw36z22k#the-measures-we-choose-matter">The measures we choose matter.</a></h2>
<p>We should be careful about introducing metrics that turn participation into performance.</p>
<p>Visibility isn't connection. Popularity isn't quality. Attention isn't value.</p>
<p>Bluesky doesn't need to reject every conventional social media feature, but it should be more deliberate about the design philosophy it adopts, especially given the history of social networks.</p>
<p>It has an opportunity to prioritise meaningful participation, good conversations and healthy communities over familiar measures of attention.</p>
<p>My concern is that Bluesky is importing the same assumptions that have shaped other platforms: that more reach = more success, more engagement = more value, and that everything worth doing should be measurable.</p>
<p>The culture of a network is shaped by what gets measured, rewarded and encouraged. I'd rather see Bluesky question the conventions it has inherited than reproduce them simply because they're familiar or because people ask for them.</p>
<p>There is a difference between building a platform that helps people communicate and one that encourages them to measure how well they're performing at communicating.</p>
<p>Bluesky has an opportunity to challenge the attention economy, not quietly reproduce it. The question isn't how many views a post gets, but whether the interaction it creates actually matters.</p>
<p>What might it look like if we prioritised meaningful conversations, community, curiosity and the quality of contributions over quantity of eyeballs?</p>
<p>We don't need another place to count how many people looked at us. We need better places to connect with one another.</p>
