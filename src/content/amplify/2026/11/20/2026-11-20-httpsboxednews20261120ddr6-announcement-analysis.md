<h2>The new DDR6 standard, with its significantly faster speeds, could maybe offer a pressure-release valve on one of the toughest areas in computing.</h2>
<p><strong data-line="2">Hey, don’t tell Sam Altman, but</strong> … new, faster RAM is on the way.</p>
<p>This week, the Joint Electron Device Engineering Council, a.k.a. the primary standards body for microelectronics, <a data-line="4" href="https://www.jedec.org/news/pressreleases/jedec%C2%AE-previews-next-generation-ddr6-dram-higher-performance-greater-capacity">pulled the curtain off of the emerging DDR6 standard</a>. It comes with a few impressive upgrades beyond what we’re already used to.</p>
<p><strong data-line="6">» Speeds starting at 12.8 gigatransfers per second,</strong> a level of memory bandwidth nearly 30% higher than DDR5’s 9.2 GT/s maximum. (Top speeds should go up to 14.4 GT/s, a 44% uptick from DDR5.)</p>
<p><strong data-line="8">» Up to 128 gigs of memory on a single DRAM die,</strong> with an emphasis on putting more RAM in less space. <em data-line="8">(Hey, if the RAM is smaller, maybe it’ll be easier to acquire?)</em></p>
<p><strong data-line="10">» Better support for different memory form factors,</strong> including the emerging <a data-line="10" href="https://www.xda-developers.com/camm2-will-replace-ram-sticks-in-your-next-laptop/">CAMM standard</a>. (As you might remember, laptop-grade DDR5 wasn’t as fast as desktop-grade DDR5, which is part of the reason so much RAM is soldered in 2026. So hopefully more CAMM might help!)</p>
<p>How much of an improvement is this? For context: DDR4, first announced in 2014, generally topped out around 4.26 GT/s, so that’s about triple the speed in 12 years.</p>
<p>Of course, <em data-line="14">faster</em> RAM isn’t worth very much if that RAM isn’t accessible. But on the other hand, odds are high that these newer kinds of RAM are likely to take the pressure off of consumer-focused varieties of RAM, eventually. (Especially if, as some reports are implying, DDR6 will make <a data-line="14" href="https://hothardware.com/news/jedec-previews-ddr6-4x-bandwidth-smaller-sticks-1tb-modules">1TB of RAM in a single system</a> feasible.) After all, there’s a reason why DDR4 is way more price-accessible than DDR5.</p>
<p>Eventually, we’ll all benefit from the <a data-line="16" href="https://tedium.co/2019/06/04/used-workstation-computer-buying-strategy/">upgrade abritrage</a>.</p>

<h3 data-line="1">Forget Jensen: It’s OK to feel excited (and weird) about the RTX Spark boxes</h3>
<p>I’ll admit, first off, that Boxed News is not where you go for your hot takes on Jensen Huang, even if there have been <a data-line="56" href="https://www.reuters.com/world/us-secs-nvidia-probe-is-examining-potential-accounting-issues-sources-say-2026-11-20/">some news stories</a> about him and his company this week. Instead, we’re focused on the devices themselves. And the earliest RTX Spark desktop reviews came on strong this week, with <a data-line="56" href="https://www.howtogeek.com/msi-edgemesa-n-ai-rtx-spark-mini-pc-review/">mostly</a> <a data-line="56" href="https://www.tomshardware.com/desktops/mini-pcs/msi-edgemesa-n-ai-plus-review">positive</a> <a data-line="56" href="https://www.windowscentral.com/hardware/asus/asus-proart-gr1x-nvidia-rtx-spark-desktop-review">reviews</a>.</p>
<p>All the reviews kind of landed in the same category: The port selection is kind of rough (like most Windows-on-ARM devices, no USB4 yet), they cost a lot, and Windows on ARM is just not all that amazing. But the devices itself are fast, compact, and can do really cool stuff.</p>
<p>As cool as they are, it would not be surprising if this made you feel a little hollow. The key point of discussion here comes <a data-line="60" href="https://www.pcworld.com/article/3288499/im-furious-that-i-love-nvidias-rtx-spark-so-much.html">from <em data-line="60">PC World</em>’s Brad Chacos</a>, who more or less nails down why these machines are simultaneously exciting and deeply annoying:</p>
<blockquote data-line="62"> <p data-line="62">It’s awesome. This is the sort of weird, experimental computing that made me fall in love with PCs in the first place. I can dream up some ridiculous project, throw a mountain of hardware at it, and see what happens. No permission required! And no annoying cloud API meter ticking away every time I get curious.</p> <p data-line="64">And I hate that this wonderful new frontier is emerging from the same AI gold rush that’s making ordinary computers so damned expensive.</p> </blockquote>
<p>The appeal of mini PCs, as well as the single-board computers that came before them, was that they allowed experimentation without a lot of extra cost. Now, we’re seeing a lot of energy towards mini PCs that cost as much as a used car. That’s inevitably going to leave a lot of folks out in the cold.</p>
<p>I’m with Brad. That’s bad for users, and it’s bad for computing.</p>

<h3 data-line="1">WTF Is A Gigatransfer?</h3>
<p><strong data-line="55">You might have read the RAM story up above</strong> and thought to yourself, “wait, I’ve never heard of a gigatransfer before.”</p>
<p>It’s the common term for referring to signal transmission rates across computing interfaces, with an emphasis on components like PCIe cards and memory. It’s a measurement of how fast a pipe can actually transfer data, rather than the number of bytes actually bing shared. Gigatransfers (and its kissing cousin, megatransfers) are measured in thousands of bits by a factor of 10, rather than as a multiple of a gigabyte or megabyte, which is measured by a factor of 8.</p>
<p>Confused yet? To put it another way, it’s a reference to the number of data transfers per second that a computing device is capable of, which isn’t a 1:1 match with a processor’s clock speed. Modern-day RAM, which pushes a lot of data around a computer at once, can greatly outpace what a processor is capable of.</p>
<p>Found this confusing? Well, probably not helping was that transfer speed is often incorrectly described as megahertz or gigahertz, rather than transfer rate. It became enough of a pedantic problem that Microsoft <a data-line="61" href="https://www.techpowerup.com/322246/microsoft-is-switching-from-mhz-to-mt-s-in-task-manager-for-measuring-ram-speeds">actually changed it in the Task Manager</a> a few years ago. Most people don’t get hung up on this, but as late tech-reviewing giant Gordon Mah Ung noted in 2024, <a data-line="61" href="https://www.pcworld.com/article/2328096/this-windows-11-change-will-finally-make-nerds-less-agro.html">tech nerds do</a>.</p>
<p>(This is one of those things that you probably need to know about for your inevitable CompTIA certification test. I mean, that’s what you’re buying all these mini PCs for, right?)</p>

<h3 data-line="1">This Kangaroo mini PC could bounce into your pocket</h3>
<p>If you’ve ever found yourself looking longingly at a <a data-line="54" href="https://www.khadas.com/mind">Khadas Mind</a>, a mini PC designed to be extremely pocketable, know that Khadas was not first to that model. Far from it, in fact.</p>
<p>In the mid-2010s, devices like the Intel Compute Stick aimed to make it possible to plug a full-fledged computer into the back of anything with an HDMI port. Think the Chromecast, but for Windows. That trend didn’t <em data-line="56">quite</em> blow up the way that Intel presumably hoped, but other competitors definitely saw an opportunity in the same space.</p>
<p>Which brings us to the InFocus Kangaroo Mobile Desktop, launched around 2016. Essentially working as something of a combination of a Compute Stick and a Raspbery Pi, the device was a two-chunk block of aluminum that could be used as a full computer. The larger block featured the computer itself, which had a fairly modest Intel Atom processor, along with a battery.</p>
<p>The smaller one? It was a dock, and rather than using USB-C, it relied on a proprietary connector that looks not unlike a 30-pin iPhone connector.</p>
<p>This was a unique setup even for its era, when pocketable computers were kind of having a moment. You could literally set up a computer with a mini server, have it connect to Wi-Fi, and it could just live on battery. Or you could plug it into a TV, or a monitor. <a data-line="62" href="https://www.pcmag.com/reviews/infocus-kangaroo-mobile-desktop">As <em data-line="62">PC Magazine</em> noted in an Editor’s Choice review</a>, the device seemed to be designed for managing remotely.</p>
<blockquote data-line="64"> <p data-line="64">If you stand the module upright, you’ll see an LED indicator on the top left that tells you whether the Kangaroo Mobile is on (blue) or charging (red). On the right side is a fingerprint reader and the Power button, along with an action switch, which uses Virtual Network Computing (VNC) software so you can remotely control the Kangaroo. Sliding the action switch enables Wi-Fi Direct mode, making the computer available to incoming connections, so you can control your Kangaroo with other devices running a VNC solution, such as another PC or mobile device. For example, you can hook up the Kangaroo with a remote mouse and keyboard on your phone and control it using the RemoteMouse app. Simply download the app to your phone (the remote client) and download the app to the Kangaroo (the host client), hop on the same Wi-Fi network and step through the setup process, and you’re able to control the Kangaroo using your phone.</p> </blockquote>
<p>On top of its more minimal use cases, the device actually borrowed some of the tricks of the Khadas Mind, with InFocus <a data-line="66" href="https://www.zdnet.com/article/infocus-kangaroo-notebook-gives-you-two-swappable-windows-10-pc-modules-in-one-299-laptop/">selling an 11.6-inch laptop shell</a> that you could plug the tiny computer into.</p>
<p>To be clear, this is not a particularly powerful computer—the Khadas Mind has laptop-grade components that are actually quite fast, all things considered—but it is quite cheap.</p>
<p>And that means that, if you’re in the market for some small, cheap computers that maybe run a container in a constrained environment, <a data-line="70" href="https://ebay.us/HkbfdV">you can find these on eBay</a> in the $50 to $75 range.</p>
<p><a data-line="72" href="https://infocus.com">InFocus</a> gave up this market long ago, instead favoring its traditional market of projectors and interactive displays. But the Kangaroo Mobile was a genuinely good idea for its time, and one that probably deserves a bit of a comeback in 2026.</p>
