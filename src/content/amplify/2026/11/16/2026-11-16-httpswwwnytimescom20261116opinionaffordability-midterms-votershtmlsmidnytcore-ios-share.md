title: Affordability or Antifascism Is a False Choice

<figure><img alt="An illustration of a fist atop people’s legs." src="https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/11/16/opinion/16taylor/16taylor-articleLarge-v5.jpg"/><figcaption> Nathaniel Russell</figcaption></figure><p>As we process the Democratic Party’s  midterm victory, a widely agreed-upon narrative is emerging: This was an affordability election. From South Carolina to Ohio to California, voters rallied behind Democratic candidates who spoke to kitchen-table concerns.</p><p>But it would be wrong, and ultimately dangerous, to interpret voters’ preference for Democrats exclusively as a reaction to rising prices.</p><p>This election was also a profoundly antifascist one.</p><p>Americans desperately want and need costs to come down, and delivering on that should be the Democratic Party’s first priority. But the landslide that redrew political maps, particularly in the South, cannot be explained solely by anger at grocery and gasoline prices. It was also powered by a deep sense of moral revulsion and indignation that has only gained force since 2024.</p><p>The tone of these midterms was set by ordinary people who stuck their necks out by bravely opposing Elon Musk, immigration enforcement, data centers and the carnage in Gaza well before those positions were safely mainstream.</p><p><strong>Let’s remember how</strong>, in the early days of the second Trump administration, an atmosphere of fear descended over the nation as many elite institutions — universities, law firms, media outlets and tech companies — bent the knee, convinced that a permanent proto-fascist “vibe shift” had arrived. Thankfully, not everyone was so eager to acquiesce.</p><p>Soon after the inauguration, <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2025/06/03/business/elon-musk-trump-tesla-protests.html">Tesla Takedown</a> protests popped up at Tesla dealerships and spread widely, much to the irritation of Elon Musk, who was then gleefully beginning to gut the federal work force. People kept showing up even after President Trump threatened to classify those who vandalized Tesla cars as “domestic terrorists” and send them to Salvadoran prisons.</p><p>In April 2025, according to organizers of the events, millions of people at 1,400 sites across the country mobilized during the Hands Off protests in outrage over Mr. Trump and Mr. Musk’s decimation of public services and the mass firing of federal employees. The aim, in the words of an Indivisible founder, Leah Greenberg, was “piercing that sense of authoritarian inevitability.” I, for one, recall being able to breathe more freely knowing so many people were taking a fundamental lesson from the history of fascism to heart: Do not obey in advance.</p><p>Soon after, the No Kings protests brought even bigger crowds into the streets, culminating in what may have been the single largest day of protest — Oct. 18, 2025 — in American history. Research shows that the demonstrations reached deep into Trump country. It was an early sign that MAGA was vulnerable on red turf.</p><p>When Stephen Miller unleashed ICE and the Border Patrol, the grass-roots resistance evolved to meet the threat. From Chicago to Charlotte, concerned citizens, many of whom had never been activists before, received training in civil disobedience at churches. In Minnesota, ICE’s violent Operation Metro Surge was met with courage and defiance. After Renee Good and Alex Pretti were killed by federal immigration agents, the weekslong showdown between protesters and ICE became a moral referendum for the nation as the public witnessed what “mass deportation” actually entails.</p><p>Public opinion on immigration enforcement turned even more intensely against the administration, with one YouGov poll <a href="https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/54225-support-for-abolishing-ice-reaches-50-percent-february-27-march-2-2026-economist-yougov-poll">finding</a> that half of respondents supported abolishing ICE. This month, Michigan and Maine elected progressive senators on platforms pledging to do just that. ICE had become an avatar of Republican authoritarianism, propelling Latino voters to swing toward Democrats at the polls.</p><p>It’s notable, for example, that in a <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2026/10/28/polls/times-siena-mn-poll-toplines.html">Times/Siena poll</a> of likely voters in Minnesota, “the state of democracy/corruption” was the most important issue after the economy in their decision about which congressional candidates to choose, perhaps because of their intimate experience of state violence. Nationally, the cost of living was the <a href="https://maristpoll.marist.edu/polls/u-s-midterm-elections-october-2026/">top issue</a> for 34 percent of voters, but “preserving democracy” was a close second.</p><p>What exactly do people mean when they cite democracy as a key concern? It’s impossible to know for sure, but it’s hard not to imagine some respondents connecting that prompt to everything from disgust with the federal government’s blatant disregard for the rule of law to repudiating racist attacks on voting rights and D.E.I. It could also be rage against the president’s self-enrichment and embrace of the tech oligarchs and their anti-human vision of A.I.-control.</p><p><strong>In 2026, being concerned</strong> about democracy meant something deeper than it did two years prior, when Democrats tried and failed to position themselves as defenders of a status quo imperiled solely by Mr. Trump’s recklessness. Some of this month’s winning candidates took a far different tack, echoing anti-oligarchy messaging long promoted by the democratic socialist left. Abdul El-Sayed’s slogan in Michigan, “Money out of politics, money in your pocket, Medicare for all,” states it plainly: Democracy as Americans know it is broken by the outsize power of the superrich, and this dynamic has steadily eroded “affordability” while preventing sensible and popular political reforms.</p><p>Many commentators have framed the Democratic Party’s pivot to anti-oligarchy messaging as a turn away from so-called culture wars and identity politics. But that’s too simplistic. Democrats have gotten better at framing the experience of marginalization, which often falls along racial and gender lines, in ways that underscore more broadly shared concerns, including the desire for dignity and security and a more responsive government.</p><p>In Tennessee, Victoria Broderick, the Democratic nominee in the Fourth Congressional District, was initially inspired to run because of her anger over the dismantling of abortion rights. She campaigned as a proud working-class mother of two, openly queer, pro-Palestine, Medicare-for-all supporting ally of trans youth who may yet flip her highly gerrymandered district (votes are still being counted). That she spent under $100,000 on the race only lends credibility to her call to get money out of politics.</p><p>That’s what real economic populism is: a democratic revolt against the capture and corruption of politics by moneyed interests, which is more than just a call for lower living costs. It is also fully compatible with a politics that refuses to pull punches about the far right’s authoritarian designs, and is inseparable from universal human rights.</p><p><strong>Mr. Trump’s post-election elevation</strong> of Stephen Miller to chief of staff signals that the White House, however weakened, remains undeterred, even if the president has acknowledged that the ICE crackdowns may have been “too tough” and turned voters off. Mr. Miller, of course, is a promoter of white nationalist material who is an architect of the administration’s war on immigrants, an avowed enemy of international law, and a firm believer that might makes right. In short, the administration is doubling down on fascism.</p><p>This is not a moment for leftists, who make up a substantial number of Democratic voters, to shrink our ambitions or to soft-pedal the hideousness of the Republican agenda. The people occupying the White House, and their Big Tech enablers and political allies, have shown us that they would install themselves as our permanent overlords if they could. My co-author, Naomi Klein, and I call their politics “end times fascism,” an apocalyptic and kleptocratic movement that has little to offer its base except bigotry, destruction and data centers.</p><p>To deliver a decisive blow against Mr. Trump and his billionaire backers, we need to build on the antifascist organizing that has proved so effective thus far at beating them back. Mr. Trump and MAGA are flailing; if we let up now, the next iteration of the far right may be even more extreme.</p><p>Ultimately, affordability and antifascism are complementary: Insecurity often makes people more susceptible to far-right appeals, which means addressing affordability is part of a smart antifascist policy response, though not an adequate one on its own.</p><p>The midterms showed that a growing number of people, from all walks of life, want to be able to make ends meet. They also showed that ordinary people understand that the stakes are much more existential. This is a fight for a livable democratic future that we can’t afford to lose.</p><p>Astra Taylor is an organizer, a filmmaker and an author, with Naomi Klein, of “<a href="https://us.macmillan.com/books/9780374621384/endtimesfascism/">End Times Fascism: And the Fight for the Living World</a>.”</p><p><em>The Times is committed to publishing </em><a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2019/01/31/opinion/letters/letters-to-editor-new-york-times-women.html"><em>a diversity of letters</em></a><em> to the editor.
