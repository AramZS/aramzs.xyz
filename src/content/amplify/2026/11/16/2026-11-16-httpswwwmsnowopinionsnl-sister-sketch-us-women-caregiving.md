<p>The <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/newsgraphics/card-story/saturday-night-live-ad-parodies">commercial parodies</a> on “Saturday Night Live” are often good for a chuckle, but the parody ad “<a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-X9dxL17t1k">Sister</a>,” which aired during last weekend’s episode, may spark more than a few family arguments this holiday season. The ad opens with a man, played by host John Mulaney, resting a loving hand on the shoulder of his aging father, played by Tommy Brennan. “My dad means the world to me,” Mulaney intones, adding “I didn’t want to put him in a nursing home, but a private health aide could cost thousands a week. I didn’t know where to turn. That’s when I heard about Sister.”</p>
<p>“Sister,” a voiceover explains, “is a live-in 24/7 eldercare provider that is completely free and also your sister.” A smiling Mulaney adds, “Now I don’t have to worry about Dad getting the care and attention he needs, because my sister will do it, and I can get back to my life and the stuff I like to do.”</p>
<figure><div class="rw-embed-wrapper"><embed src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/-X9dxL17t1k?feature=oembed" type="video/mp4"/></div></figure>
<p>The sketch has already <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-X9dxL17t1k">racked up millions</a> of views and <a href="https://www.instagram.com/reels/DfwDTErvgHN/">likes on social media</a>, with commenters saying “This is me. I am sister,” “Cannot wait to send this to brother,” and “This is going to [expletive] up a lot of Thanksgivings and I am here for it.”</p>
<p>Why? Because “Sister” hits on a fundamental (and fundamentally messed-up) reality: that women are the closest thing the U.S. has to a social safety net.</p>
<p>Women in the U.S. <a href="https://thegepi.org/publications/free-time-gender-gap/">spend twice as many hours</a> on unpaid care as men. According to the Department of Labor, women over 55 are the largest group of people <a href="https://www.dol.gov/sites/dolgov/files/WB/WBIssueBrief-OlderWomenAndUnpaidCaregiving.pdf">providing unpaid elder care on any given day</a>. Research <a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8996302/">has also shown</a> that these divisions <a href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jomf.12951">take a toll on sibling relationships</a>, even when siblings were previously very close.</p>
<p>Take, for example, one of the women I interviewed for my book, “<a href="https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/697130/holding-it-together-by-jessica-calarco/">Holding It Together: How Women Became America’s Safety Net</a>.” I’ll call her Virginia to protect her privacy. Not only is Virginia the <a href="https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/23780231211038783">default parent</a> and the person who manages most of the housework and <a href="https://press.princeton.edu/books/hardcover/9780691245386/whats-on-her-mind?srsltid=AYCX9vL_Anhcgkb2IzYIxzxJMOhKCh-_4HwE5z49F3hENwoaKdo0Gx81">mental load</a> for her own family, she’s also the default caregiver for her aging parents, even though her brother lives nearby. Virginia told me how, after her mom was first diagnosed with Alzheimer’s disease, her brother gave Virginia a hug and told her, “I’m sorry this had to happen, but you’re the matriarch now. It’s all on you, and I see you holding the world up.” That conversation left Virginia itching with resentment, but ultimately she had no way to force her brother to help care for their parents, and she couldn’t see any easy way out. “There’s a real weight to being a woman in a family,” she told me resignedly.</p>
<figure><blockquote><p>The politics of personal responsibility incentivize all of us to push the work of care downstream, from men to women, and from women in more privileged positions to other women — often Black, Latina and immigrant women.</p></blockquote></figure>
<p>What SNL’s parody omits, though, is that caregiving inequities don’t start or stop with men. Rather, as I show in my book, the politics of personal responsibility incentivize all of us to <a href="https://rooseveltinstitute.org/publications/the-care-cascade-interviews-from-our-broken-care-system/">push the work of care downstream</a>, from men to women, and from women in more privileged positions to women — often Black, Latina and immigrant women — in positions more vulnerable than their own.</p>
<p>You see, the core idea of <a href="https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3579128">personal responsibility politics</a> — or what we might more wonkily call <a href="https://rooseveltinstitute.org/publications/the-care-cascade-interviews-from-our-broken-care-system/">neoliberalism</a> — is that the <a href="https://global.oup.com/academic/product/the-great-risk-shift-9780190844141">government shouldn’t take care of people</a>, because people can either take care of themselves or pay someone to take care of their needs instead. The problem, though, is that many — including young, sick and elderly people — need hands-on, 24/7 care. This kind of care is extremely labor intensive; under a privatized model, each caregiver’s salary and benefits have to be covered by a tiny number of clients. Home health aides typically only have a handful of patients in their caseload, and some elderly patients have multiple home health aides taking shifts for round-the-clock care.</p>
<p>These low recipient-to-caregiver ratios are essential to ensure high-quality care, but they also make care so expensive that most people who need it can’t <a href="https://www.aarp.org/caregiving/financial-legal/long-term-care-affordability-report/">afford it</a>.</p>

<p>Rather than acknowledge that care is incompatible with personal responsibility politics, U.S. policymakers have tried to ignore the problem by keeping <a href="https://www.nbcnews.com/business/economy/eldercare-workers-job-growth-pressure-rcna258820">eldercare</a> and <a href="https://www.americanprogress.org/article/still-underpaid-and-unequal/">childcare</a> wages as low as possible, and by creating <a href="https://www.phinational.org/a-new-federal-proposal-would-further-undercut-home-care-workers-labor-rights/">loopholes</a> that allow care sector employers to get away with denying <a href="https://cscce.berkeley.edu/publications/report/from-unlivable-wages-to-just-pay-for-early-educators/">health insurance</a> and <a href="https://www.bostonfed.org/news-and-events/news/2023/07/child-care-early-educators-retirement-challenges-and-opportunities.aspx">retirement benefits</a> to their employees. These <a href="https://crr.bc.edu/better-pay-and-benefits-for-direct-care-workers-could-help-care-recipients-and-their-families/">low wages and lack of benefits</a> keep the costs for paid care services low enough that some families can afford them, albeit sometimes only by <a href="https://kffhealthnews.org/aging/dying-broke-facing-financial-ruin-as-costs-soar-for-elder-care/">taking on substantial debt</a>.</p>
<p>Faced with the prospect of financial ruin, many U.S. families decide to insource care rather than outsourcing it. That’s where the hot potato starts getting thrown around. Which sibling should, as the “Sister” ad put it, “quit her job, break her lease and move halfway across the country to take care of” the parent?</p>
<p>What the “Sister” ad aptly calls “internalized sexism” tends to make it seem “natural” for this work to fall to women. <a href="https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/23780231211038783">Gender pay gaps and gendered work pressure</a> make it seem “logical” for women to do this work too. “Whatever pays the most wins,” another woman, whom I’ll call Erica, explained to me. “My husband’s job is very demanding, and they talk a lot about flexibility, but at the end of the day if [his boss] sets a meeting, he sets a meeting. You can’t not go if you want to keep your job.”</p>
<figure><blockquote><p>We could treat care as public good, the way that many other high-income countries have. </p></blockquote></figure>
<p>But forcing care downstream isn’t the only solution to the care crisis, despite what conservative policymakers might want us to believe. Instead, we could treat care as public good, the way that many <a href="https://archrespite.org/what-long-term-care-looks-like-around-the-world/">other high-income countries</a> have. The Netherlands, for example, is a <a href="https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/health-at-a-glance-2025_8f9e3f98-en/full-report/long-term-care-spending-and-unit-costs_59f07562.html">global leader in eldercare</a>, coupling its <a href="https://english.zorginstituutnederland.nl/about-us/healthcare-in-the-netherlands">universal healthcare</a> program with a tax-subsidized <a href="https://www.nber.org/papers/w31823">public insurance program</a> that guarantees access to low-fee long-term care services, including both nursing home placements and in-home personal care.</p>
<p>These public programs haven’t closed the gender care gap entirely. Women in other countries still spend more time doing <a href="https://www.statista.com/statistics/521908/time-spent-work-unpaid-countries/">unpaid care</a> than men do. But <a href="https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691202402/making-motherhood-work?srsltid=AYCX9vLAtcpot2sEFjZZMedtfPoqpzE9c-uc8E9pR9w6bZUyq6RGwrSS">sociologist Caitlyn Collins has found</a> that public investments in care programs — particularly when coupled with a rejection of sexist beliefs about women’s “natural” fit for care work— can lift some of the weight on women without simply shifting it to men. Dutch women, for example, spend less time on unpaid care than do U.S. women. But Dutch men also spend less time on unpaid care than do U.S. men. And not surprisingly, the Dutch are <a href="https://ourworldindata.org/happiness-and-life-satisfaction">happier</a> than Americans too.</p>
<p>The “Sister” ad, then, could have had a slightly different ending. The current version notes cheekily, “Offer void if Sister has a good therapist.” But given that the U.S. is already (and for related reasons) facing a severe <a href="https://www.kff.org/other-health/state-indicator/mental-health-care-health-professional-shortage-areas-hpsas/?currentTimeframe=0&amp;sortModel=%7B%22colId%22:%22Location%22,%22sort%22:%22asc%22%7D">shortage of mental healthcare</a>, there aren’t enough good therapists for all the Sisters, Wives, Mothers and Daughters who need them. And therapy, operating at the individual level, can’t fix the broken systems that are breaking people’s mental health.</p>
<p>Instead, “Sister” might have ended with a “Coming Soon!” new product called Billionaire. The pitch is simple: “Sick and tired of individualized and privatized solutions to your care crisis? Try taxing Billionaire to help fund high-quality, universal public systems of care!”</p>
