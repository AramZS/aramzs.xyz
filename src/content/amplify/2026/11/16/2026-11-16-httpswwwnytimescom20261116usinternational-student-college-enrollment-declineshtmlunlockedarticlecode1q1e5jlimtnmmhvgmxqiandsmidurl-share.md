title: International Students Are Turning Away From U.S. Universities
---
<p>For a second year, Trump administration policies appear to be scaring away international students, disrupting the finances of the American colleges that depend on them.</p><figure><img alt="A brick tower is seen in the distance against a gray sky. Two people walk along a path under trees with green, orange and yellow leaves." src="https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/11/16/multimedia/16nat-international-students-hp-promo/16nat-international-students-tvmb-articleLarge.jpg"/><figcaption>Overall international enrollment is down about 10 percent at Michigan State University in East Lansing.  Nick Hagen for The New York Times</figcaption></figure><p>The enrollment of new international students at American universities dropped once again this fall, fueled by deep uncertainty about visas and job prospects.</p><p>A survey released Monday found that new international student enrollment plunged by 15 percent, a figure that was particularly worrisome for higher education executives following a precipitous 18 percent decrease last year.</p><p>Including preexisting students, international student enrollment declined 9 percent this fall, according to the survey, adding to last year’s drop of 3 percent.</p><p>For years, American colleges and universities had attracted growing numbers of international students, who often paid full tuition, indirectly subsidizing tuition for domestic students. But President Trump’s crackdown on immigrants, which has occasionally swept up foreign students, along with confusing visa policies and a trade war with China, one of the top suppliers of international students, have stoked fears that the United States is no longer a welcoming place to study.</p><p>Ted Mitchell, president of the American Council on Education, a large education trade organization, characterized the combined two-year downturn as “likely historic,” noting that international students had been a growth sector for higher education.</p><p>He pointed to departures among international students who were already enrolled as a worrying sign. “These are people who’ve already started, and they’ve said, ‘No, the risk is too high. I’m not going back,’” Dr. Mitchell said.</p><p>To make up for lost revenue, universities around the country have cut budgets, shuttered programs and laid off employees. The University of North Texas said it was eliminating or merging about 70 academic programs, citing a decline in international students. Pennsylvania State University partly blamed a drop in international enrollment for a recent <a href="https://budgetandfinance.psu.edu/fy-2026-27-eg-budget-update">belt-tightening</a>. And Boston University, which laid off employees last year, said that a drop in international students is <a href="https://www.bu.edu/articles/2026/transformation-higher-education-finances/">contributing to financial pressures</a>.</p><p>The fall 2026 data, based on a survey of more than 800 universities, was released by the Institute of International Education.</p><p>The organization also released its annual Open Doors report, a detailed and more comprehensive look at international enrollment for the 2025-26 school year. The I.I.E., a nonprofit group, is affiliated with the State Department.</p><p>A separate analysis by a group that promotes international education, NAFSA: Association of International Educators, said that this fall’s decline would cost the U.S. economy 31,000 jobs and $2.4 billion in revenue.</p><p>Despite the declines, the United States remained the top destination for international students, with more than a million enrolled last school year.</p><p>The report from I.I.E. showed variations among the types of students coming, however. Graduate students, which make up the largest segment of international students, declined by 13 percent last year, driven by a drop in students pursuing master’s degrees.</p><p>A New York Times analysis of more recent student visa data collected across all institutions by the U.S. Department of Homeland Security shows that those trends appear to have continued this year. The number of visas held by students seeking master’s degrees dropped 15 percent year over year, according to the Times analysis. The number of visas held by students seeking bachelor’s degrees was roughly the same this October versus last, and the number held by international students pursuing doctoral degrees was up about 6 percent.</p><figure><img alt="A person wearing a knit V-neck sweater stands on a paved path outside of modern multistory buildings." src="https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/11/16/multimedia/16nat-international-students-01-hmlc/16nat-international-students-01-hmlc-articleLarge.jpg"/><figcaption>Elena Li, a sophomore from Wuxi, China, blames the Trump administration’s immigration policies for a decline in international student enrollment. Nick Hagen for The New York Times</figcaption></figure><p>Many international students once viewed an American college degree as a way into careers with U.S. companies. But some now fear that a U.S. diploma no longer provides such a guarantee, as the Trump administration restricts long-term stays and work visas.</p><p>Elena Li, a Michigan State University student, said that many of her Chinese friends had decided against coming to the United States to study because they worried about securing visas to stay and work after graduation.</p><p>“I think the main reasons have been a lot of changes in immigration policies,” said Ms. Li, a sophomore from Wuxi, China, who is studying supply chain management.</p><p>At Michigan State, overall international enrollment is down 10 percent this fall, according to university data.</p><p>Some students whose visas have expired are remaining in the United States for fear that, if they go home to renew them, they won’t be readmitted, according to Scarlett Geng, a Michigan State sophomore from Beijing.</p><p>“They want to complete their degrees,” she said.</p><p>Ms. Geng, a communications major, said that new restrictions that the Trump administration was placing on a program called curricular practical training, a type of internship, were creating additional anxiety.</p><p>“It means it will be much harder for us to find a job in the United States,” she said.</p><figure><img alt="A person in a black coat sits on a concrete bench outdoors. Plants and a brick building appear in the background." src="https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/11/16/multimedia/16nat-international-students-02-hmlc/16nat-international-students-02-hmlc-articleLarge.jpg"/><figcaption>Scarlett Geng is a Michigan State sophomore from Beijing. She worries about finding a job after recent policy changes.  Nick Hagen for The New York Times</figcaption></figure><p>University leaders said they were feeling the impact. At Ohio State University, overall enrollment grew but international enrollment declined by 13 percent this fall, including a 20 percent drop in students from China.</p><p>In an interview, Ravi V. Bellamkonda, Ohio State’s president, said the university relies on tuition from international students to subsidize in-state tuition.</p><p>“There’s both a financial impact and a larger impact,” said Dr. Bellamkonda, who first came to the United States as an international student from India, currently the largest source of international students to the United States.</p><p>“There’s an impact to the economy and the country for the loss of these bright people, who might stay in Europe or Australia or somewhere else,” he said.</p><p>The University of Illinois, Urbana-Champaign, a campus known as a top destination for Chinese students, said 9 percent fewer of them had enrolled.</p><p>Wojtek Chodzko-Zajko, dean of the graduate college at Illinois, came to the United States as an international student from England.</p><p>“I think international students are weighing their options, and they do have options,” he said.</p>
