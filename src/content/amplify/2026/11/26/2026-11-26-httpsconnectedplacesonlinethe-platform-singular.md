<p>Here’s a list of design practices that the European Commission wants to ban on social media for young people. It wants to ban content that plays automatically, as well as notifications not directly triggered by their own activity, or rewards for sharing content. And it also wants to remove incentives to engage “at regular times or with greater frequency, including through penalties or loss of benefits” for staying away. And the European Commission also does have very explicit ideas of what it wants social networks to do instead. It wants them to give “priority and primary weight to explicit user-stated preferences”, and also wants platforms to switch off engagement-based suggestions by default.</p>
<p>This list is surprisingly close to the reasons people give for why they prefer fediverse software over centralised platforms. Almost every feature on this list is something that software like Mastodon have explicitly chosen not to build, and the absence of these features is a major reason why people use the fediverse.</p>
<p>But at the same time, it is almost impossible for the fediverse to meet all the requirements that the Act requires. A fediverse server operator currently cannot verify the age with the certified systems that the Act requires. And more importantly, it cannot give adults normal treatment as they currently get on the fediverse without these accounts having gone through these age verification systems. Nor can it tell other servers which accounts belong to children, because the Act explicitly forbids that. So the strange thing is that the design of the EU KIDS Act rewards the values of social network design that the fediverse also has, but then puts highly stringent requirements on those same fediverse server operators that are impossible to meet.</p>
<p>Some context for the people who do not follow European regulation, which, side note, highly recommended, good for your sanity. The EU KIDS Act is a legislative proposal published by the European Commission this fall, and is currently being discussed by the European Parliament and the member states. It makes significant proposals. Such as that nobody under 15 may have their own account on a social network, and 13 and 14-year-olds may only use an account when it’s supervised by their parent. This goes for every social network, every video platform, online games, AI chatbots, app stores; virtually every social online service is in scope and has to meet a long list of safety-by-design requirements.</p>
<p>The thing that matters here for the fediverse is that the Commission explicitly states that “small and micro enterprises are not exempted from this Regulation, since they may equally provide harms to minors. It would undermine the objective of this proposal to exclude them from scope.” This makes the EU KIDS Act different from the Digital Services Act, which spares small platforms from most of the obligations that it puts on the largest big tech platforms. This proposed law deliberately and explicitly also includes all small providers. And the Commission helpfully concedes in that same document that it cannot estimate what it will cost the “potentially large number of small and micro providers” who were not covered before under the DSA. A Mastodon server run by a volunteer admin for 100 community members has to meet the same requirements as Instagram does.</p>
<p>The EU KIDS Act, much like the other European platform laws like the DMA and the DSA, are written with a very particular kind of viewpoint: that there is a single party that the law can address, that controls the entirety of the service, and that European regulators can write a law that targets those specific parties. The problem, of course, here is that the fediverse is deliberately built to avoid constructing such a single party.</p>
<p>Imagine for a moment you’re the regulator trying to enforce the new EU KIDS Act. You want to make sure that the providers know of the obligation in Article 12(3), that says “by default, other recipients of the service not previously accepted by the minor shall not be able to access account information of the minor or content uploaded or shared by the minor on the service”. So you draft a letter reminding the providers of this and asking how they are going to comply.</p>
<p>If the service in question is Instagram, you can send this letter to Dublin, where Meta has set up a large compliance department, whose entire job it is to answer this kind of letter. Instagram is also the kind of company that can actually do what the Act requires. And it can do so because, as a centralised platform, it both builds the app, runs and controls the servers, and controls the algorithms. So if Instagram decides a teenager’s posts are hidden from strangers, they are indeed hidden from everywhere on the network, because Instagram controls all parts of the app.</p>
<p>Centralised platforms have become so normalised that it actually becomes hard to see that this request by the regulator contains three different things. And that a centralised platform fuses these three things together, but that does not naturally need to be so. Namely, there has to be an address. The recipient has to be able to do what is required. And doing what is required is also enough to make the desired outcome actually true. The EU KIDS Act is written with these type of big tech platforms in mind.</p>
<p>But the thing is, for the fediverse, they come apart. The first problem is: where is the regulator actually going to send this letter to remind the provider of his obligations? There’s tens of thousands of fediverse servers. So this one letter becomes tens of thousands of letters. And it is very unclear who the operators actually are for the majority of servers. This is not an insurmountable problem, however. It is expensive and slow, but theoretically, the regulator can send a letter with the reminder of obligations to every fediverse server operator.</p>
<p>But the second problem is what the recipients of this letter can actually do. A fediverse server operator controls their server, but not the software it runs or the apps that people use to read it. Some of the requirements in the letter are able to be done by the server operator, but not all of them.</p>
<p>The third problem is that, let’s suppose a fediverse server operator does everything that they can do with their server to meet the requirements of the KIDS Act. This means that a teenager’s posts are restricted to accepted followers, and that requests from anybody else are refused. But the posts are not hidden in the way that the Act requires, because the way that the fediverse works is that a copy of that post was delivered to the server of every follower, and these servers belong to other people. So if you are a fediverse server operator that has done everything they can to meet the requirements set out in the Act, it still has not made the outcome true that was required, namely that nobody else can read the posts.</p>
<p>This is not a problem that previous European internet platform regulations had in the same way. The Digital Services Act, in its version of this letter of requirements, asks for the much broader and vaguer “appropriate and proportionate measures” to protect minors. So a platform can answer those requirements, and answer honestly, by simply describing all the actions that they’ve taken to try to comply. The EU KIDS Act takes that same article of the Digital Services Act and specifies it in its own words. But in practice, this means that the appropriate measures are now replaced by a list of things that must be true. To the letter of a regulator asking for appropriate measures, a fediverse server operator could reply with all the efforts that they’ve made. But for that same request regarding the KIDS Act, the only honest reply is that they could not comply.</p>
<p>In the previous section, the regulator sent a letter asking about compliance as if it were one single thing. But the EU KIDS Act asks about compliance with a dozen different requirements: age checks, default settings, parental controls, complaint procedures, making sure children cannot receive notifications at night, and so on. Apply that to a fediverse server and its operator, and it quickly becomes clear how hard this actually is to implement.</p>
<p>Part of the list of requirements is simply work for the operator of a server. If your server is based outside the EU, you have to name a legal representative inside it: a person or a company who answers for the server in a member state, so that the EU knows which person in Europe to send its complaints to. The server also has to handle reports from young users first, and handle them fast. It needs a procedure for age checks that went wrong, with a free way to contest them. And finally, the operator can face complaints from children, parents and organisations, filed with the regulator in the country where the person complaining lives, and then passed on to that legal representative.</p>
<p>It needs an organisational structure, it needs money, and it needs effort. If you are a social media platform run by a medium-sized or large company, you can, in theory, hand it to a department, give it a budget and move on. If you have any familiarity with how fediverse servers actually run, you know how very much that is not the case. Outside of a few servers run by large companies, like Meta’s Threads, there is no server run by a company with a department and a dedicated budget for regulatory requirements. Servers are largely run by one volunteer in the evening. As <a href="https://about.iftas.org/trust-safety-services/2026-social-web-operations-report/">IFTAS’s 2026 survey</a> showed, most of them are run by volunteers who are not paid for their work, and donations cover less than half of what it costs to run the servers, with the operators paying for the rest themselves. The cost of implementing all of these requirements does not get any lower for that.</p>
<p>So this part of the list is about time and money. That is very hard to fix, especially given how server operators actually work in the fediverse, but it is not impossible.</p>
<details> <summary>Legal text</summary> <p><strong>Art. 24(1):</strong> Providers “which do not have an establishment in the Union but which offer services or systems in the Union, shall designate, in writing, a legal or natural person to act as their legal representative in one of the Member States where the provider offers its services or system.”</p> <p><strong>Art. 19(3):</strong> Providers shall ensure “that reports submitted by minors … are processed and addressed as a priority and without undue delay.”</p> <p><strong>Art. 29(5):</strong> Providers “shall ensure that recipients have access to an effective internal complaint-handling mechanism enabling them to lodge, by electronic means and free of charge, complaints against the outcome of the age assurance.”</p> <p><strong>Art. 21(2):</strong> Minors, guardians and qualifying non-profit bodies “shall have the right to lodge a complaint alleging an infringement of this Regulation … with the competent authority … of the Member State where the recipient of the service … is located or established.”</p> <p><strong>Art. 41</strong> adds the Act to the annex of the Representative Actions Directive (EU) 2020/1828, opening it to collective consumer action.</p> <p><strong>Explanatory memorandum:</strong> “It is not possible to conclusively determine the costs of these adjustments and their incidence, especially for the potentially large number of small and micro providers of online social networking services … that were previously not subject to article 28 of the Digital Services Act.”</p> </details>
<p>The more challenging part is that most of the list is actually not something that an operator does, but something the software does, the software that the server operator runs. Things like checking a person’s age at sign-up, being able to tell adults and minors apart, setting safe defaults, setting up a system that refuses service to children at night, and building a parental control system for parents over their children’s accounts. So a fediverse server operator that uses software that offers these features, they would just update the software and be done with it. But then the problem is: none of the fediverse software, like Mastodon or PieFed or Lemmy or Pleroma, does this so far. And the things listed are massive features. And even if you’re the type of person who believes we can just throw an AI at it, part of it actually depends on updates to the protocol as well. So that’s not something you can easily just throw an AI at either.</p>
<p>The same goes for the apps, because so far we’ve been talking about the fediverse server software, but lots of fediverse users never use the website that comes by default with the server software. They use third-party clients like Tusky, Ivory, Phanpy, or a dozen others. And a whole lot of requirements from the EU KIDS Act are specifically about how the app works. So things like how a timeline scrolls, whether a video plays by itself, and whether a phone is allowed to buzz at night. This is all done in the app.</p>
<p>So the Act targets the people who operate the software and holds them responsible, but they are largely dependent on the developers of the server software and the developers of the apps, and those do not have any responsibility under the Act.</p>
<details> <summary>Legal text</summary> <p><strong>Art. 29(2):</strong> For the under-fifteen rule, providers “shall rely exclusively on an EU age verification solution using an EU proof of age attestation, provided by a third party, certified as conforming with the EU Age Verification Scheme.”</p> <p><strong>Art. 8(1):</strong> Providers “shall design those services and systems in accordance with the requirements laid down in this Chapter by default and shall only derogate from those requirements after they have established that the recipient of the service or the user of the system is an adult, by making use of age assurance.”</p> <p><strong>Art. 11(1):</strong> Providers “shall, by default, turn off at least the following settings: (a) geolocation and other tracking features; (b) access to microphone and camera; (c) recommendations of other accounts and synchronisation of contacts; (d) push notifications.”</p> <p><strong>Art. 9(3):</strong> Providers “shall put in place effective measures to ensure: (a) time-limited access for minors on their service; (b) interruption of usage by minors on their service. Such measures shall be designed in a way that protects school time and core sleep hours of minors.” <strong>Recital 27</strong> defines core sleep hours as “at least eight consecutive hours between 22:00 and 08:00 local time.”</p> <p><strong>Art. 20(1)–(2):</strong> Tools for guardians must be “tailored to the age of the minor”, “easy to use, access and activate”, “ensure that changes can only be made with the same degree of authorisation that is required for the initial activation”, and be “effective and cannot be easily circumvented”; and “minors are informed when any tool for guardians … is being used.”</p> <p><strong>Art. 9(2)(a)</strong> treats as addictive design “enabling automatic play of content and uninterrupted content consumption without effective and regular interruption moments.” <strong>Recital 27</strong> names “autoplay, autoscroll, autoreplay, infinite scroll.”</p> </details>
<p>But even if the server developers and the app developers build everything the EU KIDS Act requires, we still wouldn’t be there. Because the Act requires for two things at the same time. The first is that it wants the child to be protected wherever her posts are. And the second part is that nobody gets told that the account is of a child, because the result of an age check cannot be shared with anybody else. This works fine on a centralised platform like Instagram, because Instagram controls where the post ends up, and Instagram is the one who does the age check. So it doesn’t really have to tell anybody. But on the fediverse, the posts of her end up on the server of every follower that she has. And those servers need to know if the account that made that post is of a child, to treat the account as such. But telling the other servers means sending a list of children’s accounts to strangers. And for pretty good reasons, that is not allowed by the Act. So in a decentralised network, you can only have one of the two options: either the entire network knows an account belongs to a child and can treat it as required by the law, or a child’s account has the anonymity that the law also requires, but then the rest of the network cannot treat it as the law also requires.</p>
<details> <summary>Legal text</summary> <p><strong>Art. 12(3)(a):</strong> “by default, other recipients of the service not previously accepted by the minor shall not be able to access account information of the minor or content uploaded or shared by the minor on the service.”</p> <p><strong>Art. 28(1)–(2):</strong> “Age assurance solutions shall not enable the identification of the recipient nor locate, track, target, advertise to or profile recipients for any purpose.” Providers “shall not further process, share or combine this information with any additional data.” The only exception is operating systems passing on an age signal they already hold, with the user’s consent (Art. 29(6)).</p> <p><strong>A rule no website can meet.</strong> Art. 12(3)(e) requires that “other recipients of the service shall not be able to download or take screenshots of … any content uploaded or shared by minors on the service.” No service that can be opened in a web browser can promise this, Instagram included.</p> </details>
<p>So in short, some of what the Act asks is work that operators could do, if they suddenly had a magic button that gives them a whole lot of money and time. Some of it is things that developers can do, again provided there’s a budget and time for it, even though they’re not required to by law, because the law doesn’t target them. And some of it is just not possible at all, in how the structure of the network is set up.</p>
<p>So why do fediverse server operators actually have to deal with this now? When it comes to European internet regulation, you sort of get three types of answers. It’s a protect-the-children argument. It’s Brussels wants control of all of the internet. And European regulators are stupid and they don’t understand how the internet works. I think there’s something to all three of these arguments. But there’s also a more boring answer.</p>
<p>The job of the European Commission is to turn many different national laws that cover the same thing into a single European law. 7 countries have introduced their own draft laws in the last 2 years, and there’s more being proposed, with age limits ranging somewhere between 13 and 16. And the Jutland Declaration from last year had 25 member states also asking the European Commission for strengthening online child safety.</p>
<p>The frustrating part is that these laws are pretty popular with the regular voting base, and popular laws tend to happen, whether they work or not. If you’re the type of person that reads this type of newsletter, it’s probably not a very big surprise that the implementation so far of online age verification laws in Australia and the UK has been mixed, to put things very optimistically.</p>
<p>But Europe simply has one way of how it actually does platform laws: it has one hammer, and to that hammer the entire digital world looks like a nail. This is now the third time I’m writing about that hammer hitting the fediverse: first with the <a href="https://connectedplaces.online/federation-has-a-european-legal-problem/">Russmedia ruling</a>, then with <a href="https://connectedplaces.online/the-algorithm-singular/">Coyote</a>, and now with the EU KIDS Act. So the entire law gets shaped around the structure of: find who runs the service, and give them a whole long list of requirements they have to meet. Because implicit in that is the assumption that, oh, this is only going to be Twitter and TikTok and the Mark Zuckerberg Cinematic Universe. And we don’t feel a whole lot of sympathy for them, so we can put very stringent requirements on them. The requirements are written to be harsh enough for Mark Zuckerberg. But they land on somebody who runs a server in the evenings, for free.</p>
<p>So the fun part about writing such an article, and getting you to read a whole lot of words about what the impact of this new proposed regulation is, is that they all come with the assumption of how all the servers can conform with this regulation.</p>
<p>And the simple answer here is, for the fediverse, most simply won’t.</p>
<p>There’s going to be a whole lot of fediverse servers that are going to put their middle fingers in the air and say, fuck you, European Commission, we’re not going to do this. This is part of the ethos for a significant group of the fediverse. The idea of being independent, of running your own server, also comes with the idea of being responsible yourself and not getting told what to do by government regulators. And there’s simply precedent for it that goes far beyond the fediverse as well. Most websites on the internet don’t implement GDPR and cookie banners properly, and everybody knows at this point that you can just kind of get away with it.</p>
<p>The ones that are going to ignore this are largely hobby and personal servers run for a small community. They might actually not even technically be covered by the law, because the law applies to services normally provided for remuneration. Which leads to the slightly absurd situation that the moment you put up a donation page and ask your users to help pay for the server, you’re more likely to fall under this law. Ignoring it is not a risk-free strategy, and this is very much not legal advice, because a single complaint from a parent can reach the server operator on a personal level. So there will definitely be server operators who will hedge by closing sign-ups or going invite-only.</p>
<p>Then there are the servers who really cannot ignore this. There’s the few large servers, like mastodon.social. They are simply too visible. But there’s also a category of servers that are visible even though they’re small, because they’re tied to an organisation that people know in the real world. And their choice comes from the rule that everybody is treated as a minor until shown otherwise. So they have to either check the age of everybody, or simply put all unchecked accounts into a minor mode, with posts hidden, no follow suggestions, etc.</p>
<p>For me, the most notable part here is not so much what happens with the current fediverse servers, but that it makes the adoption path of new fediverse servers much harder. One popular story of how the fediverse has grown over the last few years has been the idea of: hey, the fediverse is a service of communities, and all sorts of communities that already exist in the real world can set up a fediverse server for them. Whether that’s a broadcaster or a city or a media organisation, or a university actually is also a strong comparison. It’s those type of organisations that already have a strong presence in the real world who cannot afford to just go the middle-fingers-in-the-air approach that the hobby servers do. They ask themselves, okay, it’s a fun idea to open a Mastodon server, but what does our legal compliance department say? And then they look at the legal compliance department and the legal people start screaming.</p>
<p>Because the law puts a very high price on open sign-ups by people you don’t know. Even the organisation behind the largest server, Mastodon GmbH, is a small team of fewer than 20 people.</p>
<p>And that’s, for me, the part that makes this also incredibly frustrating. These servers are exactly the way that Europe hopes to get digital sovereignty and independence from the big tech platforms. And it’s these servers that get hit the hardest.</p>
<p>The EU KIDS Act is still a proposal, however. And the European Parliament and the member states will rewrite it before it becomes law. An exemption for small server operators, similar to how the DSA has, would fix a lot of the problems already for the fediverse. But even then, the deeper problem remains that the European regulators want to deal with singular entities. That control their entire platform. And that’s the one specific thing the fediverse deliberately does not do.</p>
<p>That’s just the sad thing of it all, really. There’s a very widespread agreement in society that it’s probably not a very good idea to have all of our children use social media controlled by Mark Zuckerberg. And we keep adding legislation and regulation, now with the EU KIDS Act, but also with the rulings I wrote about earlier this year. We keep making it harder for teenagers to escape Mark Zuckerberg.</p>
