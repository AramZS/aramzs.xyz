title: Bradford Cox Wants to Be Redeemed
---
<p>The polarizing frontman of Deerhunter and Atlas Sound largely disappeared for seven years. He just released a surprise album inspired by mercy and the death of a close friend.</p><figure><video controls="" poster="https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/11/24/25cul-bradford-cox-24217-cover/25cul-bradford-cox-24217-cover-master315.jpg"><source src="https://vp.nyt.com/video/2026/11/24/184314_1_25cul-bradford-cox-24217_wg_1080p.mp4" type="video/mp4"/></video></figure><p>Faulkner fell asleep before the veterinarian could slip the needle into his leg.</p><p>Bradford Cox had adopted the white-and-tan dog in the summer of 2014, when his band Deerhunter was between albums. The two became so close that Faulkner soon showed up in promotional photos. Their bond intensified in the years after the pandemic, when Cox neither traveled nor released new music.</p><p>But Cox recorded in his Atlanta attic almost every night, with Faulkner snuggled nearby. They developed a daily ritual — a 9:30 p.m. nap, while “<a href="https://elianeradigue.bandcamp.com/album/trilogie-de-la-mort">Trilogie de la Mort</a>,” by the French composer <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/02/24/arts/music/eliane-radigue-dead.html">Éliane Radigue</a>, played.</p><p>On a visit to a veterinarian in April for a routine allergy shot, the doctor noticed Faulkner had lost weight. Blood tests indicated advanced leukemia. Three weeks passed before Cox took the dog for a last evening at their favorite dog park, where two dozen people came to say goodbye. Cox played Radigue from a Bluetooth speaker; Faulkner knew it was time to nap.</p><p>“That was the time when I realized there was something I loved so much more than myself or material or artistic achievement,” Cox said, “but there was nothing I could do to give him one more day.”</p><p>Almost exactly seven months later, Cox strode into the Ace Hotel in Manhattan and beamed. It was minutes after midnight on a Friday, and he had just surprise-released “<a href="https://atlas-sound.bandcamp.com/album/thanatos">Thanatos</a>,” an album under his solo project Atlas Sound, that was precipitated, in part, by Faulkner’s death.</p><figure><img alt="Bradford Cox sits on a red couch with a golf motif, playing a miniature piano. Behind him sits a painting of a peacock." src="https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/11/25/multimedia/25cul-bradford-cox-wqcv/25cul-bradford-cox-wqcv-articleLarge.jpg"/><figcaption>Bradford Cox hadn’t released new music, or ridden in a plane, in seven years before his new album “Thanatos.”</figcaption></figure><p>It is the first Atlas Sound record in 15 years, ending Cox’s seven-year retreat from the music industry since Deerhunter’s 2019 album. After spending so long by Faulkner’s side at home, and taking his first plane ride in seven years to do his first interview in the same time span, Cox was eager to see what had changed in New York, and in the musical world he’d left behind.</p><p>“I’ve not been the guy from the band for seven years, and I’ve enjoyed the break,” Cox, 44, said the next night, after a trip to Brooklyn to buy hats and records and eat fried catfish. “I don’t have any clout with young people from Brooklyn. I would imagine that I’ve been made … not obsolete, but more like an interesting legacy. I hate to say it, but maybe I’m a myth?”</p><p>When Deerhunter and Atlas Sound emerged two decades ago, Cox was the clearly brilliant chameleon, able to shift among honey-sweet psychedelic rock, hypnotic abstraction and radiant pop. He had a striking look and backstory — tall and rail thin, having endured a series of grueling surgeries to mitigate the effects of Marfan syndrome, an inherited condition that can warp the skeleton and damage the circulatory system. His interviews could extend for hours, with Cox talking about surviving Southern poverty, being a sober asexual virgin and why so many fellow artists seemed so boring.</p><figure><img alt="Cox plays a guitar onstage with three other men. He is shot from below and a green spotlight glows behind him." src="https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/11/25/multimedia/25cul-bradford-cox-pqmv/25cul-bradford-cox-pqmv-articleLarge.jpg"/><figcaption>“I’ve not been the guy from the band for seven years, and I’ve enjoyed the break,” Cox said of his absence from the band Deerhunter. Michael Nagle for The New York Times</figcaption></figure><p>Back then, he <a href="https://www.theguardian.com/music/2015/dec/07/deerhunters-bradford-cox-retracts-smashing-pumpkins-billy-corgan-statement-altercation-claim">feuded publicly</a> with Billy Corgan, showed up onstage in slinky dresses and waltzed onto late-night television <a href="https://vimeo.com/257309971">dripping blood from his bandaged fingers</a>. In a moment when nascent professionalism tempered indie rock’s antagonistic origins, Cox evoked Andy Warhol and even Henry Rollins, his idiosyncratic collision of art and life demanding a visceral response.</p><p>“I have always felt like Bradford is the last rock star of the modern era — this person who wasn’t afraid to give a combative interview, to be himself in a way that most artists aren’t willing to do,” said <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2022/05/10/arts/music/kevin-morby-this-is-a-photograph.html">the songwriter Kevin Morby</a>, who has covered Deerhunter and spent a memorable night at Cox’s house in 2013. “Bradford walked the walk in this way where the art really came first. He didn’t compromise that at all.”</p><p>After being diagnosed with autism during the pandemic, though, Cox recognized he had sometimes overcompensated. “I didn’t understand that I was unable to stop talking, that I was in this neurodivergent state,” he said en route to Phil’s Stationery, a 60-year-old Midtown Manhattan reliquary of office supplies. “It explained my rambling and my sensory obsessions, things that people find charming when they want to and, when they don’t, are annoying.”</p><p>Cox had slept until 1:30 p.m., then woke up worried he wouldn’t make it to Phil’s before closing time. He called and asked if they might stay open a bit longer. The clerk agreed.</p><p>He went silent the moment he walked inside, spellbound as he strolled the cluttered aisles, marveling at half-century-old packets of green graph paper and gazing at a bag of loose pencil erasers like one might examine a satchel of jewels. “I don’t know why anyone would want to have sex, when you can have <em>this</em>,” he said, almost whispering. “I know I don’t.”</p><p>Cox sauntered to the register and smiled at Frank, who had taken his call. “I am in heaven, sir,” he said, his voice now town-crier loud. “On the phone, you were like, ‘Why do people think this is so great?’ I’m autistic, and this is heaven.”</p><p>For much of his life, people in Cox’s orbit — or even fans with parasocial relationships — labeled him intense or insufferable, words that make him grimace. He was opinionated, loud and stubborn, prone to fill silence with banter because it made him uneasy. He now tells strangers he is neurodivergent early in conversation. He believes it puts them at ease, letting them off the hook for their responses to how insistent and enthusiastic he can be.</p><figure><img alt="An old soccer ball leaves Cox’s hands. He is seated in a room with low ceilings and there is a black dog in front of him in this black and white image." src="https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/11/25/multimedia/25cul-bradford-cox-gjzt/25cul-bradford-cox-gjzt-articleLarge.jpg"/></figure><p>Those disclosures also function as a note-to-self, reminding him that he doesn’t owe anyone the brash opinions or fresh gossip that frequently got him into rock ’n’ roll trouble, as with Corgan. He often mentioned the concept of “quiet dignity” as his ultimate aspiration. “I want to be less verbose and, at the end of my life, leave no one’s feelings hurt.”</p><p>Cox has accepted what a challenge that will be. Two dozen times during a day, for instance, he said something sharp about another musician, family member or even a long-dead celebrity, then fretted over the impact of his words. In the past, he reckoned, he wouldn’t have cared. “I had a very emotional and spiritual self-analysis since Faulkner died, thinking about things I regret. I don’t resent or have ill will towards anyone. I’ve had it, but it’s gone,” he said. “I want to be redeemed.”</p><p>Faulkner’s death also helped prompt “Thanatos,” which gently considers questions of redemption and maturity, or of surviving long enough to have a shot at either. “Calling my friends / We’re all still learning,” he sings in “<a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mEwRoTH9XCQ">Akron,</a>” a song about institutional decay and personal endurance, “to cope with ourselves / And the power of the Lord.”</p><p>Karen O of the Yeah Yeah Yeahs was first stunned to hear he’d just released a new album, then again at what she heard. “He’s such a natural that it feels like throwaway talent, but it isn’t if you look at the depths to which he plunges in seeking some kind of truth,” she said. “That’s the tough thing, making it feel effortless, almost like a little spring bubbling.”</p><p>For years, Cox had rejected every offer to perform, largely because he didn’t want to leave Faulkner with anyone else. But in late August, four months after Faulkner’s death, Cox’s longtime booking agent emailed to say that <a href="https://stereogum.com/2512091/bradford-cox-plays-all-new-songs-in-first-show-in-seven-years-at-making-time/news">the experimental Philadelphia festival Making Time</a> had made yet another offer. Cox had already turned down the festival’s invitation for this year, but another artist had canceled. The festival founder Dave Pianka asked Cox to reconsider.</p><p>“How can I sit here and just pray for a change or something to ground me after losing Faulkner?,” he said back at the hotel, as the Radigue piece played from an orange Bluetooth speaker. “I thought, ‘What do I have to take care of now? Where do I put my love and attention?’”</p><figure><img alt="Cox, wearing a brown sweater and tan pants printed with small dogs, stands to the right of an old staircase where a white and tan cat rests." src="https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/11/25/multimedia/25cul-bradford-cox-ftbh/25cul-bradford-cox-ftbh-articleLarge.jpg"/><figcaption>In his time away from the public, Cox had accumulated more than 1,500 new tracks, often working through the night in his attic alongside Faulkner.</figcaption></figure><p>Cox had less than two weeks to prepare, but he knew he didn’t want to step back onstage with old material. In his time away from the public, he had accumulated more than 1,500 new pieces, often working through the night in his attic alongside Faulkner and his TASCAM 8-track, improvising lyrics over meticulously built rhythms. Those songs, though, were scattered across years of unlabeled SD cards, lost between couch cushions and behind bed frames in a house overstuffed with used records and books and assorted gewgaws.</p><p>As Cox retrieved the SD cards, he was shocked by how many of the tunes he loved, how they surprised him. They were like illuminated diary entries, gracefully chronicling his states of mind. He cut the nervy “<a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JXe2Kvd2XKo">Passing Strange</a>” at the threshold of a panic attack, opting to channel his feelings into sound rather than a breakdown. The cracks in his voice are real, true to that moment’s terror. The surreal soul of “<a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SgW94kpm2c4&amp;list=RDSgW94kpm2c4&amp;start_radio=1">Come and See</a>” reflected all the physical vulnerabilities around him, from his own body’s ailments to the tree branch that punched a hole in his roof, which a raccoon and possum soon climbed through.</p><p>Cox suddenly realized that he had not only a live set but also a new album. He finished the music in late October and the cover — a death mask of his own face — two days before its release on Nov. 20, urging his label to issue it digitally as quickly as possible and worry about selling physical copies later. He might back out otherwise. And maybe, he thought, someone else might need to hear the songs in the same way he had needed to make them.</p><p>“The reason I really pushed to do this now is because, when Faulkner passed away, I was shown a lot of support and love and mercy. Faulkner was, too,” Cox said. He wanted to return the favor. “My true hope is that this music will find the person, wherever they may be, it is meant to find, who it has been sent to help.”</p><p>Back at Phil’s Stationery, after an hour of slack-jawed browsing, Cox lugged three armloads of office supplies to the counter — antique pencils, enormous stencils, aged inks. Frank was helping another late arrival, who was buying sheets of old pink paper. “That’s exquisite,” Cox said, picking up a matching envelope. “It’s like dirty ballerina slippers.”</p><p>The other customer nodded and grinned. Recognizing a fellow aficionado, Cox pulled out the leather pencil case he stows in his jacket pocket near a bag of Faulkner’s fur. He told her he was neurodivergent. She told him she graded diamonds. He asked how she might grade him. Before she could answer, he laughed.</p><p>“Oh, I know,” he exclaimed. “I’ve got it: I am flawed, but I am <em>pretty</em> good.”</p>
