<p>Chevron's Gorgon gas project vented massive methane plumes to ease pressure on its troubled carbon capture and storage (CCS) system, investigators say.</p>
<p>The finding was prompted by <a data-component="Link" data-uri="coremedia://article/107288658" href="https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-10-23/large-methane-plumes-above-chevron-gorgon-project-investigation/107288658">a United Nations alert</a> sent in August, when huge clouds of the greenhouse gas were detected soaring above the $81 billion facility off Western Australia's Pilbara coast.</p>
<p>Five consecutive satellite observations between November 2025 and August 2026 estimated methane emissions averaging 4.9 tonnes an hour — nearly five times the reported rate at Gorgon over the past two years.</p>
<p>But the <a data-component="Link" data-uri="coremedia://article/107313078" href="https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-10-27/wa-regulators-investigate-methane-plumes-chevron-gorgon-project/107313078">WA's Department of Water and Environment Regulation (DWER)</a> says August's emissions event was planned, based on information from Chevron and gathered during a site inspection earlier this month.</p>
<p>"DWER concluded that the emissions were associated with venting of reservoir carbon dioxide through approved facility infrastructure during periods where constraints affected operation of the CCS system," Environment Minister Matthew Swinbourn said in a statement.</p>
<p>"DWER has found no evidence that the emissions detected on 29 August 2026 were associated with an uncontrolled leak, upset condition, incident, or unauthorised emissions source."</p>
<figure data-component="Figure" data-print="inline-media" data-uri="coremedia://imageproxy/107423574"><img alt="Matthew Swinbourn wears a suit and tie as he delivers a media conference" data-component="Image" data-lazy="true" sizes="100vw" src="https://live-production.wcms.abc-cdn.net.au/4471f1fb6ebd8e200071c018270161d2?impolicy=wcms_crop_resize&amp;cropH=892&amp;cropW=1338&amp;xPos=5&amp;yPos=0&amp;width=862&amp;height=575"/><figcaption>Matthew Swinbourn says the emissions were part of the venting of reservoir carbon dioxide. (ABC News: Lauren Smith)</figcaption></figure>
<p>Located on Barrow Island, about 60 kilometres from the West Australian mainland, the Gorgon complex remains the country's most expensive resources venture.</p>
<p>A Chevron spokesperson told the ABC the company had worked "constructively" with DWER and was committed to responsibly managing its emissions, including methane.</p>
<p>"Gorgon operates within some of Australia's most rigorous environmental and regulatory frameworks, with extensive requirements governing how emissions are managed, monitored and reported," the spokesperson said.</p>

<h2 data-component="Heading">Whistleblower: Emissions 'deliberate'</h2>
<p>In claims tabled by WA Greens MLC Sophie McNeill before state parliament overnight, a whistleblower working on Barrow Island accused Chevron of "deliberately releasing" the methane rather than slowing down production.</p>
<p>The three-point statement made to Ms McNeill reiterated that the emissions had not been a leak and blamed Chevron's CCS.</p>
<p>Reporting by Chevron shows its production of liquefied natural gas (LNG) at Gorgon has regularly outstripped its stated 15.6 million tonne per annum design capacity.</p>
<p>According to the multinational's own compliance reports to DWER, it has surpassed 15.6 million tonnes by close to 8 per cent each year since 2021.</p>

<p>Ms McNeill said the emissions amounted to a violation of Gorgon's environmental approvals, which stipulated there might be "no routine venting or flaring policy" except where normal operations were unavailable.</p>
<h2 data-component="Heading">Carbon capture scrutiny</h2>
<p>The revelations coincide with new data calculating the shortfalls of Gorgon's CCS.</p>
<p>Chevron and its joint venture partners are obligated to sequester 80 per cent of the carbon dioxide (CO2) released by Gorgon's particularly carbon-rich gas fields.</p>
<p>But engineering issues ensured the CCS system did not operate for the first three years of gas exports, and it has lagged behind the 80 per cent benchmark in the time since.</p>
<figure data-component="Figure" data-print="inline-media" data-uri="coremedia://imageproxy/107423584"><img alt="A woman and a man in Chevron branded hi-vis and hi-hats consult clipboards surrounded by gas infrastructure." data-component="Image" data-lazy="true" sizes="100vw" src="https://live-production.wcms.abc-cdn.net.au/2e70a26e53718f914271f31286a32c3f?impolicy=wcms_crop_resize&amp;cropH=2431&amp;cropW=3647&amp;xPos=1215&amp;yPos=561&amp;width=862&amp;height=575"/><figcaption>Technicians working on Gorgon's CCS system. (Supplied: Chevron)</figcaption></figure>
<p>The alert-triggering methane plumes appear to originate near the plant's three acid gas removal units, which strip unprocessed natural gas of CO2 and other impurities.</p>
<p>When operating as intended, the CO2 is then piped to a separate facility for injection into Barrow Island's sandstone underlayer.</p>
<p>A Chevron spokesperson acknowledged that challenges had "constrained injection rates".</p>
<p>"We're progressing projects to optimise the system, with work well underway," the spokesperson said.</p>
<p>Chevron documents published this month say that about 5.54 million tonnes of reservoir CO2 was separated and only 1.45 million tonnes (26 per cent) was buried during the past financial year.</p>
<p>Energy researcher Tim Baxter said it was the smallest share since Gorgon's CCS began, meaning more than 4 million tonnes of CO2 had been vented into the atmosphere and <a data-component="Link" data-uri="coremedia://article/107390220" href="https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-11-19/critics-chevron-gorgon-plumes-expose-greenhouse-gas-monitoring/107390220">required offsets</a>.</p>

(Supplied)</figcaption></figure>
<p>It is not clear why methane, the main component of natural gas, is also being released, but Mr Baxter said DWER's conclusion demonstrated "downstream impacts" affecting Gorgon's environmental performance.</p>
<p>"What we have from there is a cascading series of failures," Mr Baxter said.</p>
