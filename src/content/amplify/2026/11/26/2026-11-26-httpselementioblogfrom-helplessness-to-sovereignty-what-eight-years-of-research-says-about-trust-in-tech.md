<p>When people say they care about privacy but hand over their data anyway, the usual explanation is that they're trading it for convenience. Dr Denise Almeida, Head of Policy and Compliance at Element, has spent eight years researching how tech workers think about privacy, and she reckons that explanation gets it backwards. What her participants described wasn't a trade. It was the absence of a choice.</p>
<p>Denise did her PhD at UCL part-time, alongside her day job. That gave her an unusually long view: she spoke to more than 200 tech workers across five rounds of surveys and interviews, starting just after the GDPR came into force and running through Brexit and the COVID-19 pandemic. Interviewing some people several times meant she could watch their attitudes shift.</p>
<p>At the Matrix Conference 2026 in October, she shared what she found, and why she thinks open infrastructure like Matrix is part of the solution.</p>
<figure><div class="rw-embed-wrapper"><embed src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/BkTbcy7Oz_g?rel=0" type="video/mp4"/></div></figure>
<h2>94% of tech workers want rules, but few see them enforced</h2>
<p>In the first survey, run just after the GDPR came into enforcement, 94% of participants strongly agreed that companies should have to follow rules on data. That cuts against a popular idea at the time: that regulation is a burden nobody wants. People were happy to implement it, as long as they had the time and resources to do so.</p>
<p>They were even clearer about who shouldn't write those rules. Across three surveys, 90 to 93% disagreed that it should be up to each company to decide. Tech workers simply don't trust organisations to regulate themselves.</p>
<p>The trouble starts with enforcement. Only 38% said they knew the consequences of mismanaging data, and many others didn't believe there was any enforcement at all. Support for regulation was strong; faith that it would live up to its promises was not.</p>
<h2>The privacy paradox assumes a choice people don't have</h2>
<p>The privacy paradox is a well-established theory in academic research: people report concerns about privacy, then share their data anyway (Barth and de Jong, 2017). The gap is usually read as irrational, or as a fair trade for convenience. Denise finds that framing patronising, because it assumes people had a real choice in the first place.</p>
<p>Her participants described something more nuanced. Many built their own layers of conditional trust: happy to give accurate details to their bank, say, but inventing a date of birth for a social network. Others described reluctant acceptance. As one put it, "In the end I felt I had no choice but to accept the conditions."</p>
<p>The alternative to accepting was often being cut off from parts of everyday life. That's where the sense of helplessness comes in, alongside the distrust, overwhelm and privacy apathy other researchers have documented: a feeling of being unable to escape certain products, and of wondering what the point is when your data is already out there.</p>
<blockquote>A paradox requires a choice, and that choice isn't always available to people.</blockquote>
<p>If consent isn't freely given and properly informed, Denise argues, we should stop calling the result a paradox.</p>
<h2>Fear of fines faded</h2>
<p>In the early days of GDPR, the fines did a lot of the talking. "Everyone has heard of the massive fines associated with GDPR, so we had to adapt accordingly," one participant said in 2019.</p>
<p>But the big fines largely never came, and participants mentioned repercussions less and less over time, possibly because enforcement was lower than anticipated (Ruohonen et al., 2022). Even when fines do land on the largest companies, Denise points out, they become a cost of doing business: a budget line that's already accounted for. That matches research in cybersecurity, where fear changes behaviour in the short term but the change rarely lasts.</p>
<p>What did last was a sense of moral obligation. Reported on average by 58% of participants, it grew steadily across the study. And change stuck when people had four things: autonomy, time, resources, and leadership they trusted. That means leaders who believe that this is the right thing to do, not that the company will get fined if it doesn't.</p>
<h2>You can't be accountable for a black box</h2>
<p>The helplessness people feel as users doesn't stay at home. It follows them into work.</p>
<p>Participants regularly had to use, implement and build on proprietary tools without fully understanding how those tools worked, while remaining responsible for their compliance. If something went wrong, it was their fault. Yet they couldn't see what the vendor was doing with the data, or how decisions about it were made. People in marketing and SEO raised this particularly often.</p>
<p>It doesn't help that the services organisations rely on for hosting, marketing and day-to-day operations are mostly proprietary and concentrated in a handful of large providers. A black box can't be audited by the person who is accountable for it. So people end up doing what they're told, without really knowing what their tools are asking of them.</p>
<p>One participant's answer was for people to "reclaim their autonomy through decentralised or federated, end-to-end encrypted services and platforms". Without naming it, they had more or less described Matrix. But that answer puts the burden on individuals, and Denise's argument is that the bigger change has to happen at the level of infrastructure, so that better individual choices become possible.</p>
<h2>Three principles that answer helplessness, and how Matrix applies them</h2>
<p>There's been plenty of talk about digital sovereignty lately, and Denise isn't trying to reinvent the wheel. Her point is that we need a shared language for it, grounded in the problems people actually describe. She proposes three principles, each answering one of the frustrations above.</p>
<table><tr><th><p>Principle</p></th><th><p>What it solves</p></th><th><p>How Matrix applies it</p></th></tr><tbody><tr><td><p>Transparency by design</p></td><td><p>Systems people can't see into or audit</p></td><td><p>An open specification, open-source homeservers and clients, and audited cryptography</p></td></tr><tr><td><p>Participatory governance</p></td><td><p>The absence of a voice (Kinchin, 2024) and of agency</p></td><td><p>A public spec change process, with the protocol held by the independent Matrix.org Foundation</p></td></tr><tr><td><p>Interoperability and autonomy</p></td><td><p>Being unable to escape certain products</p></td><td><p>Federation by default: run your own homeserver and still reach everyone else, with no vendor lock-in</p></td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>None of this is theoretical. Matrix already underpins public-sector communications across Europe, including Tchap for the French government, BwMessenger for the Bundeswehr, TI-Messenger for Germany's national healthcare system, the openDesk public-sector office suite, LuxChat for government-to-citizen communications in Luxembourg, and NATO Allied Command Transformation's Ni²CE Messenger. Matrix works as a case study that other technologies and sectors can learn from.</p>
<h2>Open code gives little control to someone who can't read it</h2>
<p>Denise is clear that Matrix isn't a finished answer. Transparency without accessibility only helps people who are technical enough to read a spec or a codebase. As she put it in the Q&amp;A, "If you put a lot of code in front of me, that's really transparent, but I still don't know what's happening." Regularly commissioned independent audits and plain-language disclosures would close that gap.</p>
<p>Then there's sustainability. Suppliers who repackage Matrix without contributing upstream weaken the maintainers the whole sovereignty case depends on. Open source only delivers independence if the software it relies on is sustainable.</p>
<p>Finally, sovereignty itself is often reduced to where a supplier is based, or to buying European. For Denise, the critical point is self-hosting and control over data: who runs the server, who holds the keys, and who decides where the data goes.</p>
<h2>Sovereignty is built collectively</h2>
<p>Individuals can push back against helplessness, and it starts with asking the right questions about any product or service:</p>
<ul><li>Who is building it?</li><li>Who is funding it?</li><li>What am I exchanging by using a free service? (Usually, your data.)</li><li>Can I trust this organisation with my data?</li></ul>
<p>But individual action only goes so far. Collective action and changes to infrastructure are what actually build trust and sovereignty, and Denise's challenge is aimed at everyone who uses the protocol, from volunteers to organisations and governments:</p>
<ul><li>Commission independent audits regularly.</li><li>Make the spec process accessible to non-technical people.</li><li>Pair sustainable procurement initiatives with funding for governance infrastructure, since funding is often the biggest obstacle open-source projects face.</li><li>Agree on a definition of sovereignty based on self-hosting and control over data.</li><li>Replace incumbent proprietary and centralised solutions with open alternatives.</li></ul>
<p>Asked how to help people get more comfortable with these ideas, Denise's answer was education. Not to make everyone stop using everything, but so that consent can finally be informed and freely given.</p>
