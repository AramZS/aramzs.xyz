title: 'Trapped on the Tarmac: 3-Hour Flight Delays Spike Under Trump'
---
<p>Long delays have risen to levels not seen in more than 15 years, with no enforcement in sight.</p><p>One of the most unpleasant experiences in flying has been on the upswing under the Trump administration.</p><p>Passengers on domestic flights are being trapped on an airplane between the gate and the runway for more than three hours at rates not seen in more than 15 years. This year is on pace to be the worst since 2009, according to data from the Bureau of Transportation Statistics.</p><p>In 2010, the Transportation Department began enforcing a sweeping set of new passenger protections that included stiff fines for what are known as <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2009/12/22/business/22passengers.html">extended tarmac delays</a>. Three-hour tarmac delays fell by 94 percent in two years, and the department levied fines every year from 2011 to 2024.</p><p>The delays began to creep up slowly over that time, but accelerated during the second Trump administration. The rate of long tarmac delays this year has nearly doubled compared with 2024. Since Sean Duffy became transportation secretary in 2025, the department has yet to issue a fine for extended delays. In fact, it has not fined any U.S. airline for any consumer protection violation in nearly two years.</p><figure><img alt="United Airlines airplanes rest on a runway apron. In the distance, the New York  cityscape stands against a clear blue sky." src="https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/11/25/multimedia/up-flight-delays-01-mqgv/up-flight-delays-01-mqgv-articleLarge.jpg"/><figcaption>The airports in the New York area tend to be the worst offenders. Mario Tama/Getty Images</figcaption></figure><p>A spokesperson for the Transportation Department said in a statement that it “continues to investigate extended tarmac delays and will take enforcement action where warranted by the facts and the law.” The department’s approach “focuses on airlines fixing the root causes of travel disruptions,” the spokesperson said, adding that fines do not address the reasons for delays and cancellations.</p><p>Though still relatively rare, there are on average 2.7 extended tarmac delays every day in 2026, out of over 19,000 daily domestic flights. New York City area airports are the worst offenders.</p><p>The tarmac delay rule was enacted after a series of high-profile incidents, including a 2009 <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2009/08/15/opinion/15sat3.html">Continental Express flight</a> in which 47 passengers were stuck on a parked plane for six hours overnight with little food or water and one bathroom. The rule <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2025/04/09/travel/plane-tarmac-delays-passenger-rights-options.html">requires airlines</a> to provide snacks and water after two hours and to allow domestic passengers to deplane within three hours — four hours for international — except for safety, security or air-traffic-control-related reasons.</p><p>Ray LaHood, who served as transportation secretary during the first Obama administration, called it a “no-nonsense approach.”</p><p>“I think the flying public appreciated that rule that we put out,” Mr. LaHood said in an interview. “That is not the way you treat people when they’re paying a lot of money for a ticket to have to sit on a tarmac for more than three hours.”</p><p>The latest government data doesn’t include the Nov. 13 <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/13/us/airports-stranded-travelers.html">nationwide ground stop</a>, when more than 30 percent of that day’s flights (domestic and international) were canceled. But preliminary data on the 30 largest U.S. airports, shared by the aviation data company Flightradar24, estimates between 57 and 101 domestic passenger flights that day experienced extended tarmac delays.</p><figure><img alt="An air traffic control tower rises above an airport terminal. Airplanes and construction vehicles sit on the tarmac below." src="https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/11/25/multimedia/up-flight-delays-02-mqgv/up-flight-delays-02-mqgv-articleLarge.jpg"/><figcaption>The Dallas Fort Worth International Airport on Nov. 13, when flights were grounded nationwide.  Tony Gutierrez/Associated Press</figcaption></figure><p>“When we pulled up, there were just planes everywhere,” said Lisa Price, a traveler who was caught in a four-and-a-half-hour tarmac delay at Dallas Fort Worth International Airport after her flight landed during the outage. “I think all those rules went out the window.”</p><h2>What causes extended delays</h2><p>Even with this year’s surge, most days go by without a single flight spending more than three hours on the tarmac.</p><p>When extended delays do happen, they’re usually in clusters initially caused by severe weather and made worse by runway congestion or air traffic delay issues.</p><p>Before the Nov. 13 ground stop, the worst day for tarmac delays this year was July 18, when the New York region was affected by <a href="https://www.nj.com/news/2026/07/nj-flash-flooding-and-thunderstorms-trigger-water-rescues-closed-highways-and-newark-airport-ground-stop.html">thunderstorms and flooding</a>. The <a href="https://www.foxbusiness.com/economy/united-passengers-say-were-stuck-newark-tarmac-7-hours-during-severe-weather-delay">next</a> <a href="https://www.nj.com/news/2026/05/severe-thunderstorms-left-frustrated-travelers-stranded-at-newark-airport-for-hours.html">four</a> <a href="https://www.atlantanewsfirst.com/2026/03/07/frustration-grows-hartsfield-jackson-amid-flight-cancellations-delays/">worst</a> <a href="https://www.businessinsider.com/new-york-snowstorm-jfk-passengers-spend-3-hours-tarmac-2025-12">days</a> were all related to thunder or snowstorms.</p><p>The worst days for delays are often during the summer months, though all seasons now have more delays. The best month last year, October, had almost as many long delays as the worst month a decade ago.</p><p>Convective weather caused by rising warm and moist air, like thunderstorms, is intensifying and extending later in the year with climate change, said Michael McCormick, a professor of air traffic management at <a href="https://erau.edu/">Embry‐Riddle Aeronautical University</a> and a former air traffic controller. This type of weather creates hazards for aviation, such as increased turbulence, icing and changes in wind speed and direction. </p><p>Once bad weather arrives, the airports themselves can<strong> </strong>become a big factor in delays. The three New York airports, for example, experience significantly more extended tarmac delays than any other airport in the country. They are “very constrained in space” and operate close to their capacity in flights scheduled, said Alexandre Jacquillat, an associate professor of operations research and statistics at M.I.T.’s Sloan School of Management.</p><p>In contrast, Los Angeles International Airport hasn’t had a single three-hour delay through the first eight months this year. The three New York airports each have had at least 79 such delays this year alone, even before the recent ground stop.</p><h2>How extended delays can be prevented</h2><p>When the rule was enacted, airlines argued that they were being forced to cancel flights more aggressively to avoid extended tarmac delays.</p><p>Robert Mann, an aviation consultant and former airline executive, noted that cancellations are far worse for an airline than long delays because it means lost revenue and stranded passengers. He also pointed out that it could be a worse passenger experience to return to the gate after a shorter tarmac delay, only for the flight to be canceled.</p><p>Airlines for America, an industry trade group, said in a statement that long tarmac delays often had “multiple factors,” some within an airline’s control and others that are not, such as weather, air traffic control issues and construction.</p><p>“Airlines are aware of the increase in long tarmac delays in the past three years and are actively working to better anticipate potential hot spots and mitigate impact to passengers,” the statement said.</p><p>Aside from canceling flights, airlines have other levers they can pull to mitigate tarmac delays, like scheduling fewer flights during busy periods of the day, Mr. Jacquillat said. They can also build more resilience into their schedules by leaving extra turnaround time for flights prone to delays, he added.</p><p>But aviation experts said the lack of enforcement could also have something to do with the spike in tarmac delays.</p><p>“Limited enforcement means little accountability,” Mr. McCormick said. “There’s no disincentive.”</p><p>The second Trump administration has been characterized by a gentler approach to regulating the aviation industry, including attempts to <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/08/travel/airlines-flights-passenger-rights-trump.html">water down passenger protections</a> governing issues such as how airfares are advertised and fees disclosed.</p><p>Consumer advocates fear that airlines are being given free rein to mistreat travelers.</p><p>“In the olden days, now and again there would be enforcement actions with tarmac delays,” said Teresa Murray, consumer watchdog director at the <a href="https://pirg.org/">U.S. Public Interest Research Group</a>. “That’s not even happening anywhere. So no harm, no foul, move on.”</p>
