<p>This month, voters in four states with <a data-cms-ai="0" href="https://www.chronicle.com/article/here-are-the-states-where-lawmakers-are-seeking-to-ban-colleges-dei-efforts">anti-DEI laws</a> changed who will enforce them. In Texas, Democrats won the governor’s office, the lieutenant governor’s office, the attorney general’s office, and, for the first time in more than two decades, control of the State House. They <a data-cms-ai="0" href="https://www.texastribune.org/2026/11/09/texas-legislature-90th-session-bill-filing-begins/">quickly</a> began filing bills to repeal the state’s ban on DEI offices at public universities, though the bills face long odds in the Republican-held Senate. Democrats also flipped the governor’s and <a data-cms-ai="0" href="https://www.beneschlaw.com/insight/election-2026-reshapes-the-state-attorney-general-landscape-what-companies-should-expect/">attorney general’s offices</a> in Ohio and Iowa, and the attorney general’s office in Kansas.</p>
<p>DEI-ban repeals may not come, but universities don’t need to wait for the legislative process to play out. Much of the damage these laws have done on campus comes from how universities have interpreted them, which has often been far more restrictive than the letter of the law requires. As new officials take over enforcement, universities should revisit their view of these laws.</p>
<p>In 2025 and early 2026, we <a data-cms-ai="0" href="https://edworkingpapers.com/ai26-1515">interviewed</a> 103 faculty and staff at 81 public colleges and universities in 38 states. One longtime administrator at a flagship university has had to certify three times that his unit does no “DEI work.” “It’s kind of Orwellian,” he told us. No one defined what that work was. “We’re doing something that seems like it’s OK, but then we’re not sure if it is, and then making the call is difficult,” he said. The university’s lawyers “tend to be risk-averse. They would tend to say we probably shouldn’t do it.” The certifications began after a state senator warned university leaders, “We catch you guys trying to rebrand stuff or change it, or whatever, we’re not having that.”</p>
<p>Rules without definitions have left universities guessing what enforcers will consider a violation, and worrying that a single complaint could put state funding at risk. And choosing to play it safe out of abundances of caution has led the sector to frequently read the worst into a legislator’s warning or a lawyer’s caution. The result: Too many institutions have given up programs and practices that were <a data-cms-ai="0" href="https://www.insidehighered.com/opinion/views/2026/06/03/how-universities-expand-reach-anti-dei-laws-opinion">still legal</a>. The education law scholar Liliana Garces and her colleagues call this dynamic “<a data-cms-ai="0" href="https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00091383.2025.2471242">repressive legalism</a>,” in which perceptions of the legal environment, shaped by legislators, officials, and activists, “narrow understandings of what is possible.”</p>
<p>At one flagship, a department chair in an interdisciplinary field recalled the vice president for legal affairs insisting that the university had interpreted its state’s law “to the letter of the law.” In the chair’s view, “As draconian as they could have interpreted the law, they interpreted it.” The Trump administration’s executive orders call for an end to “<a data-cms-ai="0" href="https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/01/31/2025-02097/ending-illegal-discrimination-and-restoring-merit-based-opportunity">illegal DEI</a>” without specifying which programs or practices qualify. We spoke to an administrator and scientist who runs programs for graduate students at a research university. While negotiating a National Science Foundation grant, her team was asked to attest to language that “was so vague and weird that we didn’t know what it meant.” Neither the university’s lawyers nor the grant program’s officers could clarify. They “were just told, ‘We have to ask you this.’”</p>
<blockquote>I don’t think you can comply enough.</blockquote>
<p>Today’s rules also give officials wide discretion to decide what counts as a violation. A ban on “illegal discrimination” sounds like common sense. But officials who promised to root out DEI get to decide whether a scholarship, a mentoring program, or outreach to a local school counts as “illegal discrimination.”</p>
<p>Universities have their own lawyers and long experience with civil-rights law, but officials outside the institution can overrule them. Under Iowa’s <a data-cms-ai="0" href="https://www.legis.iowa.gov/docs/code/2026/261J.1.pdf">2024 law</a>, even an office at a state university that exists to ensure legal compliance may stay open only if the attorney general certifies that its “sole and exclusive mission” is “ensuring legal compliance.” In January, a new attorney general will decide how to exercise that authority.</p>
<p>Many universities did not wait to see how that authority would be used. They made concessions before anyone demanded them. “We have, in my opinion, overcomplied,” a senior administrator at one flagship told us. After the Supreme Court’s 2023 ruling ended race-conscious admissions, she was in the room when her university’s top leaders decided to apply it to scholarships too. When she pointed out that the ruling “doesn’t speak to scholarships,” the answer was, “Well, but it’s going to get there.” The hope, as she described it, was “if we overcomply, then they’ll leave us alone.” But, she added, “I don’t think you can comply enough.”</p>
<p>In another case, a professor’s university was already practicing what she said it called “proactive compliance” even before her state passed one of the nation’s strictest anti-DEI laws. The university had hired her several years earlier for her research focus and expected her to win outside funding to earn tenure. Now, its research office told her she could no longer apply for a foundation grant because of that same focus and asked whether she could “just take Black out.” She refused to “control-F the word.” “My mom marched so that I could do these things that I’m doing,” she said. Even in states where no anti-DEI law had been introduced, universities overcomplied, then hid that it had been their choice.</p>
<p>At one flagship, a professor who has spent her entire career there was called into meetings and told to scrub DEI language from her program’s website. When she emailed to ask whether she had to make the change, she got an immediate phone call chastising her off the record: “What the hell are you doing?” Administrators, she said, “don’t want that anywhere it could be traced.” A written order at a public university is public record. It can be requested and challenged, and it would show that the university, not the law, required the change.</p>
<p>Some of the universities we studied refused to make unnecessary sacrifices. At one, administrators told researchers privately, “Don’t preemptively comply. Do not change what you’re planning to do. We’re going to negotiate everything.”</p>
<p>The elections make that stance easier to take. In Texas, Ohio, Iowa, and Kansas, some of the officials responsible for enforcing these laws are about to change. Universities in those states should now do what the laws have always allowed, reading them constructively and rebuilding programs they’d never prohibited.</p>
<blockquote data-hide-quotes="">Universities everywhere still face federal rules no one can define.</blockquote>
<p>What could come back? Cultural centers, mentoring programs, scrubbed research, and the ethnic- and gender-studies programs that some Texas universities have consolidated or phased out. Much of it never had to go. <a data-cms-ai="0" href="https://legiscan.com/TX/text/SB17/2023">Texas’ 2023 law</a>, for example, explicitly exempts course instruction, research, student groups, and recruitment and student-success programs open to all students. With new enforcers, universities lose their best excuse for the cuts.</p>
<p>Some laws could even be read the other way. <a data-cms-ai="0" href="https://legiscan.com/TX/text/SB37/2025">Texas’ 2025 law</a> giving regents more control over the curriculum was followed by some universities setting new limits on teaching about race and gender. But the same law says general education should “transmit culture” and requires courses “necessary to prepare students for civic and professional life,” a goal that is hard to meet without teaching about race and gender.</p>
<p>Universities everywhere still face federal rules no one can define. In 2025, a federal judge blocking one Education Department directive noted that it <a data-cms-ai="0" href="https://www.npr.org/2025/04/26/nx-s1-5375904/trump-dei-schools-education-courts">“does not even define what a ‘DEI program’ is.”</a> Many law professors have <a data-cms-ai="0" href="https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=7669498">explained</a> why common diversity programs remain lawful under federal civil-rights laws. Universities can refuse to prohibit more than the law requires, and challenge rules so vague that <a data-cms-ai="0" href="https://www.aclu.org/press-releases/aclu-and-nea-sue-u-s-department-of-education-over-unlawful-attack-on-educational-equity">lawyers at the ACLU</a> argue they are unconstitutional.</p>
<p>For decades, universities interpreted civil-rights law in service of the public. They should take that power back.</p>

<p>Caleb Dawson is an assistant professor of sociology at the University of California at Santa Barbara. He is part of a team at the Higher Education, Race &amp; the Economy (HERE) Lab conducting a national study of anti-DEI policy in public higher education.</p>
