<p>I am a 42-year-old man. I have no desire to have to explain to you the meaning – or absence thereof – contained in the phrase “52 bomboclat pussywagon chicken nuggets…” but due to the unhinged nature of British political discourse that is precisely what I am now going to have to do.</p>
<p>In the early stages of this week’s confected row about a silly lipsync video posted outside the door to Number 10 Downing Street by the Tiktoker Max Balegde after a reception for social media influencers, it was the Jamaican patois word “bomboclat” that caused most consternation and comment.</p>
<p>Originally meaning either an arse rag or a sanitary towel, depending on who you ask, it has, thanks to the linguistic process called semantic drift, come to be a general purpose expletive indicating strong surprise at a turn of events. It’s bad enough that I’m providing that definition, but on Sky News’ <em>Cathy Newman Show</em> last night, the nominated etymologist was… <a href="https://x.com/SkyNews/status/2125341796653834672">Michael Gove</a>.</p>
<p>The use of ‘bomboclat’ in the meme has led many a bad faith commentator to suggest that Balegde was engaged in mockery of women’s menstrual cycles or some act of all-purpose misogyny.</p>

<p>In fact, he was just jumping on a sound that was popular at that moment on TikTok – a trending audio. There’s another linguistics term that’s useful here – semantic bleaching – which means when a word becomes less extreme over time. That’s also happened to ‘bomboclat’ which has been taken into meme culture as a word that gets posted along with two sequential images. The beginning of that trend was a tweet from 2019 featuring a mouse-character called Winslow from the animated series <em>Catdog</em>.</p>
<p>This all probably sounds very stupid. And it is. But it’s become rather more serious since the British political and media class began parroting a hard-right conspiracy theory that floated up on the swamp gasses of Elon Musk’s X yesterday. We now have to move on to the words ‘chicken nuggets’ in the viral song. On X, multiple posters have claimed – largely on the basis of a 2010 user-submitted definition from the site Urban Dictionary – that ‘chicken nuggets’ is a term for “a girl seen as ‘cheap white meat’”. This theory has then, inevitably, been tied to the grooming gangs scandal.</p>
<p>In reality, the phrase “52 bomboclat pussywagon chicken nuggets…” comes from a video made in 2025 by a literal child. That clip begins with them using the catchphrase of another influencer Kaisheps (“I can’t hit my protein”) before showing a series of mostly empty McDonald’s chicken McNuggets packets and a large stack of chicken nuggets. Sometimes a cigar is just a cigar. This time, chicken nuggets are just chicken nuggets.</p>

<p>All of this would be simply a rather grim line of argument on X were it not for the fact that prominent journalists and politicians have laundered the conspiracy theory into the mainstream.</p>
<p>This morning on BBC Radio 4’s flagship news and current affairs show, <em>Today</em>, the host Justin Webb <a href="https://bsky.app/profile/brokenbottleboy.bsky.social/post/3n34syhjhzc2n">repeated the false theory</a> as unquestioned fact: “… including the words chicken nuggets [used to describe] cheap white meat, in other words, poor white girls.”</p>
<p>In the <em>Times</em>, its sketchwriter, Tom Peck, wrote “‘chicken nugget’ apparently means “white girl”, which they don’t make clear on Deliveroo” and the same claim was pushed out by <em>The Spectator’s</em> political reporter Noa Hoffman in a <a href="https://spectator.com/article/kemi-badenoch-eviscerates-andy-burnham-over-no-10-influencers-stunt/">piece</a> for its website: “… chicken nuggets’ is a derogatory code for white girls.” Many other reporters and commentators joined in with the acceptance of this utterly fanciful but at the same time dangerously racially-provocative theory.</p>
<p>By the time that Prime Minister’s Questions had rolled around, the desperately online Leader of the Opposition, Kemi Badenoch, repeated the claim across the dispatch box telling Andy Burnham that the video had included: “Disgusting and pejorative comments about women’s private parts. Obscene language that referred to white women as cheap meat.” That is a lie and a lie that will now be recorded in Hansard. A hard-right conspiracy traded as fact in the House of Commons. It’s not the first one and it will not be the last.</p>
<p>During the opposition’s lunchtime lobby briefing, Kemi Badenoch’s official spokesperson responded to <a href="https://bsky.app/profile/adambienkov.bsky.social/post/3n35cri3jyc25">questions from <em>Byline Times’ </em>Political Editor, Adam Bienkov</a>, about the truthfulness of her statements in the chamber by saying: “Well, there are varying definition, but as I understand it, the use of chicken nuggets in used in certain communities to refer to white women as cheap meat.” Bienkov went on to ask if that definition was drawn from Urban Dictionary. The spokesperson said there “are multiple definitions” before later sending him an Urban Dictionary screenshot.</p>
<p>In the same briefing, another journalist asked the spokesperson about whether there was any evidence to back up what is clearly “a hard right trope”. The spokesperson said that Badenoch and the Conservative party are “well within our rights to say that it is what that influencer is referring to” and encouraged journalists to “get him to deny it”. Balegde doesn’t need to deny it. The evidence of the original video from which the meme stems is right there. <a href="https://brokenbottleboy.substack.com/p/when-is-a-chicken-nugget-just-a-chicken">It took me 10 minutes to confirm it yesterday</a>, time that is seemingly not available to the Conservative Party or numerous apparently professional journalists.</p>
<p>Why this all matters is because we are seeing a British ‘pizzagate’ come together in real time.</p>
<p>Just as right-wing conspiracists in the US took references to “cheese pizza” in Democratic Party emails during the 2016 US presidential election cycle and spun up a complex and entirely false story about an elite child-trafficking sex ring, the UK political and media class are making a foolish meme into proof of ‘anti-white’ racism.</p>
<p>What began as a rather silly story about a tasteless post by an influencer is becoming a serious one about how quickly the British media system picks up and spreads misinformation. For people in their teens and twenties particularly, this is also a hilarious but also horrifying example of how detached and out of touch people with power and influence can be. I asked my 16-year-old step-daughter whether she’d heard about the story and she just rolled her eyes and said, “Yes, it’s very stupid.”</p>
