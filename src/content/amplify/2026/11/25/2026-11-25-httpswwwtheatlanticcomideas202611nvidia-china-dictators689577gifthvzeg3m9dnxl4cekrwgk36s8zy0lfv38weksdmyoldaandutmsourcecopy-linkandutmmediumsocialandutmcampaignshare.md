<p>Autocracy Inc. enters the microchip business.</p>

<p>Last summer, a Russian drone came crashing down in the Ukrainian city of Zaporizhzhia, killing three people. Ukrainian engineers <a data-event-element="inline link" href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/08/24/world/europe/russia-drones-autonomous-ai-kill-ukraine-war.html">examined</a> the machine afterward and learned that it had been guided by minicomputers made by an American company. The Ukrainians then found the same minicomputers made by the same American company on a new missile that the Russians seemed to be testing in Ukraine.</p>
<p>The American company is the chipmaker Nvidia. When its Jetson Orin minicomputers started showing up inside AI-guided weapons designed to kill people in Ukraine, an Nvidia spokesperson issued a disclaimer. These were not military-grade products, he told <em>The New York Times</em>, but rather “consumer-grade products sold to students, developers and start-ups.” Nor, the company declared, does it sell the equipment directly to Russia. But it’s not hard to work out where Russia got this technology: The circuit boards inside the inspected drones and missiles were stamped Made in China. More sophisticated products, including the advanced AI chips that the Trump administration allowed Nvidia to sell to China, could eventually make their way along the same route to the Ukrainian front line. Some Chinese companies are <a data-event-element="inline link" href="https://wirescreen.ai/insights/the-malaysia-hong-kong-russia-chip-pipeline">reportedly offering</a> restricted chips to Russian customers already.</p>
<p>The evolving scandal surrounding Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang has many facets. It is a sex scandal, a spy scandal, and a corruption scandal. President Trump’s holdings of Nvidia stock, estimated at up to 370,000 shares, <a data-event-element="inline link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/nov/24/donald-trump-nvidia-stock-jensen-huang-stock-market">rose</a> by perhaps $15 million after Huang was reinstated; Cantor Fitzgerald, the financial firm controlled by the sons of Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, has <a data-event-element="inline link" href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/15/us/politics/trump-lutnick-ai-chips-nvidia-cantor-fitzgerald.html">business ties</a> with Southeast Asian companies that work with China and rely on Nvidia chips too. But this is also a special kind of national-security scandal: An American company, enabled by a greedy American president, has been selling what could become lethal technology, not just to China, but to the entire autocratic world.</p>
<p>By <em>autocratic world</em>, I mean a group of countries I’ve <a data-event-element="inline link" href="https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/archive/2021/12/the-autocrats-are-winning/620526/">described</a> in the past as Autocracy Inc.: a loose conglomerate of regimes linked not by ideology but by corrupt financial ties, institutional brutality, and a common aversion to democracy and human rights. Iran and North Korea are partners in this informal alliance. So are Russia’s partners in Africa—the Central African Republic, Mali, Niger, and warring factions in Libya and Sudan—as well as Iran’s partners in the Middle East. Cuba and Nicaragua are included, along with Belarus, Azerbaijan, and a handful of others. As chip technology evolves, along with AI warfare, authorities in the United States have to expect that anything China acquires can end up in the hands of these autocratic regimes. Eventually, the technology will kill Americans, and American allies as well.</p>
<p>If anyone in the Trump administration understands what is at stake, they haven’t yet spoken up. Trump himself is earning too much to care. Besides, he likes and admires Vladimir Putin, whom he calls a “friend,” and he appears to be in awe of Xi Jinping; he is learning fast from both of them. He shares some of their instincts: the same dislike of the language of democracy and human rights, disdain for the rule of law, a belief that the powerful should never be held accountable. His intervention to have Jensen Huang reinstated, though extraordinary in American history, would have been an ordinary event in Russia or China, where the head of state regularly makes or breaks the fortunes of individual companies for political or personal gain.</p>
<p>That’s not the kind of government Americans were voting for a mere three weeks ago. But Trump isn’t very interested in what the voters want either, or in what might be good for them in the long term, or in what might keep them and their allies safe in the next decade. National security, to Trump, is an abstraction. Only money, and the captivating feeling of personal power, is real.</p>
