<p>At least 43 Flock cameras were vandalized across Houston from April through September, according to records obtained from the Houston Police Department by <i>Houston Public Media</i>.</p>
<p>The first reported incident of vandalism took place in April 2026 near the University of Houston, when police reported that five cameras had "been damaged and tampered with" throughout the area.</p>
<p>Every other incident of vandalism mentioned in the documents occurred between late July and the end of September. Records show dozens of cameras were damaged across the city, including along the edge of downtown, along the western edge of Loop 610 and in southwest Houston near NRG Park.</p>

<p>Flock is a surveillance technology company that specializes in automated license plate readers. Over the past year, the surveillance company has faced growing scrutiny both locally and nationally for its use by local police departments to track individuals’ whereabouts.</p>
<p>The network of Flock cameras across Houston is operated by multiple governmental agencies, including the city and Harris County, and the police reports did not specify who operated the 43 vandalized cameras. The city began a more than $6 million contract with Flock in 2022.</p>
<p>Houston City Council Member Mary Nan Huffman, who represents part of west Houston in District G, said the cameras can be a critical asset for law enforcement.</p>
<p>"When I put in the Flock cameras in District G, I thought it would be to protect District G, but really, it’s doing a citywide service," Huffman said. "In the French Quarter shooting on New Year’s Eve in Louisiana ... less than 12 hours later [<a href="https://www.houstonpublicmedia.org/articles/news/houston/2025/01/02/509541/who-was-shamsud-din-jabbar-the-houston-man-accused-in-the-deadly-new-orleans-truck-attack/">the suspect</a>] was here in the city of Houston, and they were picked up on a Flock camera."</p>
<p>According to police records, at least three Flock stations in District G — one of which contained four individual cameras — were destroyed, amounting to at least $5,000 in damages.</p>
<p>Most of the reported cases of vandalism resulted in at least $2,500 in damages, according to police records. Texas law considers property damage over $2,500 a state jail felony, punishable by up to two years in prison and a maximum fine of $10,000.</p>
<p>Councilmember Joe Panzarella's District C, which covers sections of west Houston inside the loop, had at least five Flock camera stations put out of commission in his area. He said police officials told him Flock cameras are a "useful tool in solving crimes faster, but we still need to make sure that the correct data guardrails are in place to protect citizens from misuse of Flock."</p>
<p>"We don’t support vandalism of any type, and Houstonians really are looking for permanent tangible changes to the policy of the use of Flock, to the data privacy guardrails that are missing right now," Panzarella says. "Folks want long-term solutions, not vandalism."</p>
<p>No arrests were mentioned in the incident reports, and HPD did not respond to questions.</p>
<p>Beyond vandalism, the nationwide reckoning over Flock cameras has prompted policy changes across the Houston area.</p>
<p>Last month, Harris County commissioners delayed <a href="https://www.houstonpublicmedia.org/articles/news/harris-county/2026/10/15/564485/harris-county-commissioners-delay-action-flock-cameras-law-enforcement/">taking action on the county's use of Flock cameras</a> after Precinct 4 Commissioner Lesley Briones announced she would be taking down all of the cameras in her precinct.</p>
<p>During the midterm election earlier this month, <a href="https://www.houstonpublicmedia.org/articles/galveston-county/2026/11/05/566420/league-city-covers-more-than-50-flock-cameras-after-election-results/">League City voters approved a resolution</a> to cover the city's more than 50 Flock cameras. The city council added the resolution to the November ballot after residents voiced concerns about how the cameras could be misused.</p>
<p>City council public safety committee chair Amy Peck, who represents District A in Northwest Houston, argued that “regardless of your position on Flock cameras, everyone still needs to follow the law — so tearing those down is not an effective way to protest the cameras."</p>
<p>"We want to make sure we have those safeguards in place so that this tool isn’t abused," Peck said, "but when used correctly, we have seen it be very successful in helping to solve crime."</p>
<p>Flock did not respond to a request for comment.</p>
