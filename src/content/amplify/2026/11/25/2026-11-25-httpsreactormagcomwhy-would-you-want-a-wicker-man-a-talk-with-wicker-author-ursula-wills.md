<p>The folk romance <em>Wicker</em> has been one of <a href="https://reactormag.com/movie-review-wicker-is-less-scandalous-than-suggested-but-full-of-heart/">the most surprising additions to this autumn’s films</a><strong>—</strong>a love story that’s adult in every possible way, while also working as a cutting commentary on gender, sexuality, and modern society. Despite feeling like <a href="https://reactormag.com/wicker-and-the-deceptive-simplicity-of-the-fairy-tale/">a classic fairy tale</a>, the film is actually based on a much more modern short story by Ursula Wills, which will be included in Wills’ debut short story collection that’s coming out from Tordotcom next spring.</p>
<p>Reactor’s Leah Schnelbach had the opportunity to speak with Wills over Zoom, where the author talked about her writing process, the film adaptation, and the state of modern folklore.</p>
<p><em>This interview has been edited for clarity and brevity.</em></p>
<p><strong>Leah Schnelbach: I read </strong><a href="https://slate.com/culture/2026/10/wicker-movie-2026-alexander-skarsgard-olivia-colman-ending-short-story-ursula-wills.html"><strong>an interview of yours</strong></a><strong> where you told the story of</strong> <strong>the inception of “The Wicker Husband”, and I thought it was delightful. Could you tell it again for our readers—the story about visiting the Wicker People in Cornwall?</strong></p>
<p><strong>Ursula Wills:</strong> “[The Wicker Husband]” is like twenty years old. The original idea came from a place I visited called <a href="http://www.cornwalltour.co.uk/melinsey_mill.html">Melinsey Mill</a>, this old water mill that had been done up and was actually owned by a man who had been a basketmaker. They had a wicker art trail around the mill pond, there was a massive wicker spider hanging out of a tree, a full-size wicker stag—and then you get to the end and there was a wicker man fishing in the pond.</p>
<p>So I was like, “Why would you want a wicker man? What would you want one for?” That was the origin of the story. And it’s crept forward slowly in a three-steps-forward, two-steps-back kind of way ever since. I put it on a website in 2008, and then I think in 2011, <a href="https://playbill.com/article/before-wicker-the-movie-did-you-know-there-was-a-wicker-husband-musical">Rhys Jennings contacted me</a> and said “I’d really like to turn this into a theater piece”, and in 2013 he got musicians to work with him and they started taking that forward… and it went up on stage in 2020… before getting kiboshed by the COVID lockdowns… but then, in 2021, I got contacted by a film producer, and it took five years from that initial contact, and conversation with the film producer to the film actually being out and hitting cinemas.</p>
<p>The short story wasn’t published until the trailer went crazy viral… there was a lot of “publishers don’t like short stories…” but literally the weekend after the trailer dropped, I had a publisher in my inbox at like 9:30 on the Monday morning! Then I had another major publisher a <em>day</em> dropping into my inbox asking “Has anyone bought the rights to this?” [<em>laughs</em>] It’s been crazy times since the trailer dropped, really, and of course the film’s doing really well now. But it’s been a long and winding trail.</p>
<p><strong>LS: It’s so cool that it gestated for so long, and now it’s getting a new life.</strong></p>
<p><strong>UW: </strong>It’s not done yet! I saw the list of countries it’s going to—it’s still adding new countries. I think it’s not done on its journey yet.</p>
<p><strong>LS: I know you said you wrote “Wicker” quickly, but what is your writing process usually like?</strong></p>
<p><strong>UW: </strong>A couple years prior to that trip to Melinsey Mill, I attended a writing course. I’d been trying to write a novel, and one of the things I realized in the course was that I wrote <em>far</em> too many words, all the the time. I was getting really annoyed with myself, and the ability to contain the verbiage that I produced on the page…</p>
<p>That was why I started writing the short stories, and also why when you read “The Wicker Husband”, the style is quite “this happened, and then that happened”—there’s not loads of “what’s my motivation” for every character. It was part of me training myself not to produce ridiculous amounts of words every time I tried to tell a story.</p>
<p>But people ask “what’s your writing process?” And I’m like, “eh… I open the document and start writing!”—but that’s not strictly true. I tend to cook things in my head for quite a while before I sit down to write. I think “Wicker” was quite unusual in that I had the idea and was like “I must start writing immediately!” I was on holiday, I was camping, so I didn’t have my laptop with me—I went back to the campsite and started scribbling the start of it in a notebook.</p>
<p>But everyone works differently. And then you see things on the internet like FIVE THINGS YOU MUST DO TO MAKE YOUR NARRATIVE A SUCCESS. [<em>laughter</em>] No, just ignore those things. I’m not a great consumer of other people’s writing advice, I’m quite bullheaded like that, I suppose.</p>
<p><strong>LS: I know you have more stories coming out in the collection, could you talk a bit about them? Does “Wicker” fit in with them or are they each their own world?</strong></p>
<p><strong>UW: </strong>There are three or four other stories that I wrote around a similar time that I wrote “Wicker” and in some ways I think there’s some internal logic that strings them together. Some of them take place in sort of fairy tale universe, some of them take place in a sort of Victorian time and space, and some of them take place in the modern world… I think they all occupy similar spaces in that there’s three-quarters of an ordinary world, or maybe <em>8/10s</em> of an ordinary world, and then the remainder is something weird and magical.</p>
<p>I think in some ways there is a sort of through thread, but you won’t get thirteen monster stories or thirteen romances or thirteen fairy tales, but I think the common thread is that in all my stories nature and humans sort of slide into each other, without very clear boundaries.</p>
<p><strong>LS: And there are thirteen total?</strong></p>
<p><strong>UW: </strong>Yes there’s thirteen, two longer stories which are basically novellas, one’s called “Bentham River” and it’s set in a modern space about a woman who buys a new-built house in a field that’s flooded before, and her house is haunted by the water. The other novella is set in the 1940s, and It’s about a guy who comes home from war to find he’s inherited a crumbling mansion house—there’s a stone circle called The Seven Suitors on the grounds, and there’s a story about a woman who got through six husbands, until Satan came along to be the seventh, so it’s sort of a story within a story.</p>
<p>Everything occupies that same space on the borderline between the real and the unreal, regardless of where they’re set in time or geography.</p>
<p><strong>LS: What are your biggest folklore influences do you think? Are there particular stories or particular subgenres of folklore?</strong></p>
<p><strong>UW: </strong>I think I kind of grew up on those classic 1970s British kids books that were just slightly a bit too frightening for kids because ppl didn’t really think so much about that back then. Things like <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susan_Cooper">Susan Cooper</a>’s Dark is Rising series, <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Garner">Alan Garner</a>, that kind of thing. That was very much my childhood literary influence, but I’ve hung on to loving that kind of stuff as an adult. In a way it’s been a problem for me because [my work] doesn’t narrowly fit into one genre. It’s not exactly fantasy or drama, romance or comedy—it’s got elements of all those things because I read all those things. I don’t have one huge stash of sci-fi or one huge stash of romantasy that I’m working my way through. I’m quite a magpie.</p>
<p><strong>LS: I think for writers it’s good to be a magpie!</strong></p>
<p><strong>UW: </strong>Everyone creative I know is a bit of a magpie!</p>
<p><strong>LS: Do you have any theories about why folk horror seems to be having a bit of a moment, and now, folk romance—why do you think that’s speaking to people right now?</strong></p>
<p><strong>UW: </strong>I think that as organized religion sort of—maybe not in the United States—but in Europe, it’s in retreat in a lot of ways. The older things that are linked to peoples’ daily existence, and the land, resurge, like mushroom spores that have been underground. I think that’s partly it, and I think also people live lives where they’re often very urban, and move through urban space s all the time, and maybe don’t have as much nature in their lives as they’d like so things that are rooted in the seasonal and the natural give them that feeling of connection that maybe that they lack a bit… science is leading us that way as well, in terms of realizing that people <em>need</em> greenery and interaction with nature… we’re seeing humans as part of an ecosystem. It’s not seen as something kooky anymore, but more about dealing with those interrelationships.</p>
<p><strong>LS: For the film, what was the adaptation process like? Did you work on the script, or hand off the story? And were there any hard lines of things you didn’t want?</strong></p>
<p><strong>UW: </strong>My first question was “Are you going to CGI this?” because it’s quite a hard thing to make this wicker person who moves and emotes and does all sorts of things—and that was my main worry because I don’t really like over-CGI’d films. And they were like “No, we think it’s going to be an actor in costume”.</p>
<p>It was such a green flag for the whole project. That was really my one hard line, but it’s not like I had to fight about it. That was in 2021, and then it went quiet for a while, and then the next time we met up, Alex [Huston Fischer] and Eleanor [Wilson] had drafted the script. I was quite nervous reading it but as I read it I felt like they got my sense of humor, so I had a good vibe off the initial script… there were just literally too many words, so it was chipped down quite a bit from the original first draft. I think it’s been more of a process of cutting back from the original script rather than rewriting. And then there’s a whole chunk of the film, the whole story arc with the doctor that was added, but I felt it fit with the vibe of the story—the story I wrote is quite short, and the villagers are a bit of a Greek Chorus, they don’t have hugely differentiated personalities. For a feature film you needed them to have a bit more individual character. But I felt like it kept my original vibe quite faithfully, and it kept my ending, which I was really happy about.</p>
<p><strong>LS: And the way [the filmmakers] added in Tailor’s Wife trying to be a better doctor than her father, and then just being crushed the whole time.</strong></p>
<p><strong>UW: </strong>I was on set when they were film that. That was the second-to-last day of filming, it was getting really cold, and getting dark quite early, and there was a cubbyhole behind the room they were shooting in, with a screen and a heater… if you watch the bar scenes, and the scene with Richard E. Grant, if you see the back wall, I’m there, sat huddled up with a blanket and a screen behind the wall. I extra-ed for a couple days as well, so I’m in some of the crowd scenes.</p>
<p><strong>LS: I know the relationship between writers and their films can be fraught but it sounds like this was a really good experience.</strong></p>
<p><strong>UW: </strong>I think everyone on set was having a good time. Everyone got on, it was very chill, there was no screaming or ego tantrums or anything , and actually the day we finished I was saying thank you to some of the Hungarian production staff and they said, “Ah, you’ve been so chill!” And I was like is it normal for writers to turn up and throw their weight around on set? That doesn’t make sense to me because I’d seen the script—if I wanted to kick off about stuff in the script, six months ago would have been the time to do it! I asked “Do writers turn up and kick off?” and they were [<em>nods vigorously</em>].</p>
<p><strong>LS: I have two questions from friends: can the Wicker Baby keep being adapted by the Basket Maker, or is the baby a baby forever?</strong></p>
<p><strong>UW: </strong>A few people are wondering this! I don’t actually know the answer to this question. I guess the Basketmaker can make the baby larger, but I don’t know! I went down to see the people at Melinsey Mill, before the film came out—I wanted to have this conversation in person: “Do you want me to name you as the place where I got this idea? Because you might fans turning up on their doorstep!” So I had a chat with them, and the man who actually made the original sculpture—which is not there anymore, but he’s saying now that he’s going to make a new one—but I did ask him what the lifespan is of one of the wicker people, and he said about three years with good maintenance.</p>
<p><strong>LS: Oh man…</strong></p>
<p><strong>UW: </strong>So I don’t know…you can remake them, but I don’t know how long they’ll live.</p>
<p><strong>LS: OK I’ll report back to them! Oh and the other question they had, and I’m really interested in this too—</strong></p>
<p><strong>UW: </strong>Don’t ask about splinters, I don’t have any answer about splinters. [<em>Laughter</em>]</p>
<p><strong>LS: I wasn’t gonna go there! But thank you for bringing it up because I know our readers will want to know. I’m hoping they found a way around…</strong> <strong>that. Although, actually, that <em>does</em> kind of tie in—my friend was wondering if you could talk about the relationship between willow and blackthorn? I thought that was such a beautiful way to keep it within the realm of pure nature, but also have a real consequence inthe story.</strong></p>
<p><strong>UW: </strong>That’s something that literally came out of the landscape on that trip. What it’s like in Cornwall is it’s very windblown at the coast level, on those cliffs, and as you go inland, very quickly , within a mile or two it becomes very lush, very green in the valley, and it can be quite warm by British standards. The actual vegetation you get on the coast, and then inland, two miles away, can be quite different. At Melinsey Mill, where the willow trees were, was inland and sheltered, the climate is much warmer. You can grow palm trees in some places! But the blackthorn is something you’d get right on the coast, where the wind comes straight off the sea. [The plants] become very twisted, and its quite weird in that some of the branches do have a slight look of human limbs. But you can’t really make basketwork out of blackthorn—it’s incredibly hard, and has spines the length of human fingers. So I’d seen both these kind of trees and types of environment on that trip, and they knotted together.</p>
<p><strong>LS: I love that it’s so embedded in the landscape itself—the story is growing out of where you found it</strong>.</p>
<p><strong>UW: </strong>And I think also, because I was camping rather than staying in a cottage, you feel like you’re right out in that nature.</p>
<p><strong>LS: As you wrote it—you balanced the romance and the horror really well, and there is a fair streak of horror in the second half of the story. Were you conscious of trying to create that balance or was it growing out of the situation?</strong></p>
<p><strong>UW: </strong>I think it was growing out of the situation, and I think if you read some of the other stories I’ve written you’ll find that same balance between horror and romance a comedy. I think those are repeating elements of what I write.</p>
<p><strong>LS: I really appreciate because I do feel—and maybe this is just me being oversensitive—but I feel like people want everything to fit into a box.</strong></p>
<p><strong>UW: </strong>I like having different elements! This year I’ve become hopelessly addicted to watching Chinese dramas, and I’ve watched a few Bollywood movies, and they will quite happily throw in some romance, some action, comedy, family drama, a bit of supernatural something—they’ll throw it all into the pot. If I’m watching something that just comedy or just romance I’m a bit like, eh can we have a fight scene now? I like things that mashup all those different genres. In Europe I think we have to put these things in a box “Is it comedy? Or is it serious?” Where I like to get more than one element in all my stories.</p>
