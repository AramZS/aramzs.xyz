<p>Around 15 years ago, during the dog days of the Great Recession and its aftermath, rappers fell out of love with the word “like.” To up the oomph of their punchlines, MCs dropped the simile staple and replaced it with a charged pause. The results were… awkward.</p>
<p>“I’m a star… <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vtEL0uKO4tA&amp;ref=hearingthings.co"><u>sheriff badge</u></a>.” “Swimming in the money, come and find me… <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eDuRoPIOBjE&amp;list=RDeDuRoPIOBjE&amp;start_radio=1&amp;ref=hearingthings.co"><u>Nemo</u></a>.” “If she’s on my pipe, I will make her go down… <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JsBcprNEZL4&amp;ref=hearingthings.co"><u>Drano</u></a>.” I loved lots of the songs that these clunkers appear on, but the “hashtag flow,” as it came to be known, quickly wore out its welcome. The pauses inflated the bars that followed, overselling dopey one-liners as epic feats of lyricism.</p>
<p>The recession eventually ended, and this flow got absorbed into rap’s collective consciousness. “Like” returned, and the melodic rap of the 2010s unlocked new possibilities for wordplay. The slurred pronunciations, playful voice modulations, and layered vocals on songs like Migos’s “<a href="https://youtu.be/rjTVmv8p7vo?si=c5V5Ea2RLEzLz4Ez&amp;ref=hearingthings.co"><u>Hannah Montana</u></a>” and Rich Gang’s “<a href="https://youtu.be/nGt_JGHYEO4?si=r7X-q8Tvltx1O2xV&amp;ref=hearingthings.co"><u>Lifestyle</u></a>” make the punches land in unexpected places. “I’m on the top of the mountain puffing on clouds, and niggas still beginning,” Young Thug raps on “Lifestyle,” contorting “beginning” into a extended yawp that brings to mind both playground taunts and blues singing. Most rappers are not as nimble and inventive as Thugger, but melody on the whole made rap more fluid, more swingy. If punchlines in the hashtag era were all about impact—the spectacle of a haymaker connecting—those of the 2010s prized change-ups, combos, and slips that segued into liver shots.</p>
<p>In 2026, I noticed “like” fall from grace again. But for a different reason: de-emphasis. This year’s best shit-talking has been understated and sneaky, the punchlines more suggestive than flashy. Not saying “like” and minimizing pauses makes stunting sound like shooting the breeze. I call it shadow punching, and I love how it’s making wordplay more sly.</p>
<p>“<a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5iGemeSr9h0&amp;list=RD5iGemeSr9h0&amp;start_radio=1&amp;ref=hearingthings.co"><u>Nonchalant</u></a>,” a collab between rap’s youngest old man, <a href="https://www.hearingthings.co/tag/earl-sweatshirt/"><u>Earl Sweatshirt</u></a>, and chronically laidback Los Angeles spitter <a href="https://www.hearingthings.co/tag/cletus-strap/"><u>Cletus Strap</u></a>, could be a manifesto for this style. Cletus spends most of his latest record, <a href="https://www.hearingthings.co/juicy-j-spoken-word-jazz-album/"><em><u>Rappers Suck</u></em></a>, in a state of bemused composure. He lumbers behind producer Mad Max 4800’s massive bass kicks on “Nonchalant,” his sluggish flow offering just a smidge of bounce. But his rhymes are cogent and clever, taking stock of his peers’ petty relationship problems and goofy mob cosplay. “Moving nonchalant, they think I don’t really try,” he says on the hook, the misperception filling him with quiet confidence. That cool self-possession is the crux of shadow punching, which prizes both swagger and elision, slick talk and poise. For these rappers, downplaying a flex makes it even bolder.</p>

<p>Birmingham, Alabama’s 22 Pooruhn is even more deadpan on “<a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DTdRBw3cxQE&amp;list=RDDTdRBw3cxQE&amp;start_radio=1&amp;ref=hearingthings.co"><u>How Shit Go</u></a>,” a blitzkrieg of mutters and wisecracks set to floating snare rolls, spectral synth pads, and the frequent sound of blowing raspberries. His lines seem to have no end or beginning, the verses bleeding into ad-libs that often extend across measures, forming a latticework of run-ons. “Twin ass tweaked, he keep tripping ’bout that bitch, it’s not his ho/Go incognito, throw a button on that bitch, you call it a ghost,” he says at one point. The flexes are so muted they barely register as boasts.</p>
<p>Shadow punchers seem to have deeply internalized the numbness of benzos, <a href="https://www.hearingthings.co/a-history-of-hip-hops-obsession-with-lean/"><u>the lethargy of lean</u></a>, and the agitation of drill. They aren’t emotionless or monochrome, but they refuse to sweat, limiting their exertion as often as possible, making the listener work. That’s the case on “<a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pfAKCkNOxA4&amp;list=RDpfAKCkNOxA4&amp;start_radio=1&amp;ref=hearingthings.co"><u>IDK</u></a>” by Detroit’s <a href="https://www.hearingthings.co/tag/veeze/"><u>Veeze</u></a>, a groggy cut that sounds like it’s rusting in real time. Veeze’s nonplussed delivery and pronounced croak turn every bar into a shrug. “I’ma ball too hard, tear a ligament/I’m a dog, I could ball through an injury,” he says at one point, cartoonishly unbothered.</p>
<p>“<a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=y56D2WIeKxg&amp;list=RDy56D2WIeKxg&amp;start_radio=1&amp;ref=hearingthings.co"><u>Quebec</u></a>,” from the deluxe edition of <a href="https://www.hearingthings.co/tag/drake/"><u>Drake</u></a>’s <em>Habibti</em>, is just as cool-headed. Rapping in a calm murmur, the onetime poster child for hashtag rap floats across an airy beat, gliding from metaphor to metaphor with the slightest inflections. “Black skies, blue bills, my eyes turnin’ red/Purple stones encrusted, I can’t heal just yet/Yellow lookin’ orange now, I’m probably high off these meds/Strange things are happening, I swear that’s the feds,” he raps in a dazed singsong, each line feeding into the next. All the standard Drake paranoia and weariness is there, but he’s less guarded, less embittered and try-hard than he is on most of his albums from this decade. Relaxing his delivery and burying his wordplay uncoils his writing. Where the <em>Iceman</em> trilogy feels like a campaign cooked up by strategists, podcasters, and business advisors, “Quebec” is closer to a late-night workout: solitary, focused, a little sleepyheaded.</p>

<p>In terms of elaborate wordplay, battle and independent rap have always been reliable sources for abstraction, extended metaphor, and entendres. The shadow punchers’ tweak to this old tradition is to blur the swing and the hit, the wind-up and the strike. Their double-talk is tonal as much as lyrical, driven by casual changes in inflection and pitch that make barbs feel conversational without losing venom or sacrificing forward motion. It’s like they can bark and bite <em>and</em> yawn at the same time.</p>
<p>Karrahbooo’s recent mixtape <em>Not Da 2</em> is a masterclass in this aloof wizardry. The Atlanta rapper spends her debut dispatching pests, hangers-on, and foes with a clinically detached wryness. She catches every slight, escalates every beef, humbles every challenger—all while sounding deeply blasé. “I know these niggas is chicken, these niggas is tender, they fall off the bone,” she rattles off on “<a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=I5JYgle0QfU&amp;list=RDI5JYgle0QfU&amp;start_radio=1&amp;ref=hearingthings.co"><u>WYO</u></a>,” calm as a therapist. Her verses, almost all of them delivered as sighs, revolve around quick but cutting one-liners. “Nigga fanning, I call him Dakota.” “Bitches be worried ’bout me, I’m worried ’bout finding the lighter.” “Nigga broke, put his ass in some rice.” Karrahbooo constantly dismisses suitors and opponents as unworthy of her time, taunting them and swatting them away in the same disinterested breath.</p>
<p>“Out the way,” one of Karrahbooo’s pet phrases, captures her physical remove from bullshit and her emotional detachment. Her beat choices reference the triumphant trap of early 2010s Atlanta, which heightens her unrelenting cool. She floats through the pounding 808s, gothic church bells, and martial horns on “<a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=alFxwC-WbaI&amp;list=RDalFxwC-WbaI&amp;start_radio=1&amp;ref=hearingthings.co"><u>Pull a Luh Band Out</u></a>,” meeting the maximalism with insouciance. That bottomless conviction rebukes the <a href="https://www.billboard.com/music/rb-hip-hop/karrahbooo-responds-lil-yachty-ghost-writing-claims-1235761318/?ref=hearingthings.co"><u>sexist chatter about her former label boss Lil Yachty ghostwriting her music</u></a>, which is clearly driven by her own low-key finesse and charisma. “I’m not the two, I’m the one you do not wanna try,” she says at one point, supremely confident in her station.</p>

<p>AZ Chike’s major label debut <em>No Rest for the Wicked </em>is just as self-assured. The Los Angeles rapper, a scene mainstay whose profile rose following a standout verse on <a href="https://www.hearingthings.co/tag/kendrick-lamar/"><u>Kendrick Lamar</u></a>’s <em>GNX</em>, wastes no air. The album, which nods to several eras of West Coast rap, has a hushed grandeur that centers around Chike’s pliant snarl. He constantly modulates his delivery just so in order to set up and land his constant jabs. He differs from the shadow punchers above in that he’s generally expressive rather than deadpan, his verses built around sharp turns of phrase and steamrolling flows. On “<a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6gbnGDCR0TU&amp;list=RD6gbnGDCR0TU&amp;start_radio=1&amp;ref=hearingthings.co"><u>God’s Favorite</u></a>,” a leering and bouncy cut in the vein of SOB X RBE, every line prickles. “Lookie here, another nigga with his hand out/Hate me now, I remember when you was fanned out/Raise your hand if you God’s favorite, put your hand down/… Bitch, you don’t even pray,” he snaps. Notice all those commas, and the ellipsis—the pauses make his flows spring-loaded, obscuring when and where he’ll strike.</p>
<p>That tension drives “<a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_0KEfWTZ8wo&amp;list=RD_0KEfWTZ8wo&amp;start_radio=1&amp;ref=hearingthings.co"><u>Uncomfortable Conversations</u></a>,” a venting session that’s by turns confrontational and reflective. Chike dissects the ways loyalty can become exploitation, offering choice words to family, friends, and himself. “Back against the wall, still standing tall/Chin up, chest out, I know you wanna see me fall,” he huffs, steely but vulnerable. He’s not honed his singing enough to emote through melody, so he hyperfocuses on phrasing, wringing meaning out of every syllable. His lyrics can look very writerly on the page, but he understands that his snarl is his main instrument, and has fine-tuned it to tease and to taunt, to flirt and to flay. “Blow her back out, then I hit it while her back healing/Catch ’em out back, backdoor ’em, then I’m back chilling,” he says on “<a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=87gO7HMUHeo&amp;list=RD87gO7HMUHeo&amp;start_radio=1&amp;ref=hearingthings.co"><u>Look Like My Mama</u></a>,” each “back” hard as a snare strike. Chike doesn’t sweat, but he works every word.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.hearingthings.co/tag/rico-nasty/"><u>Rico Nasty</u></a> brings the same rigorous ethic to her self-released album <em>Rx</em>, a reunion with <em>Anger Management</em> producer Kenneth Blume. Across nine brisk and swagged-out tracks that slide between charm and menace, cartoon whimsy and debauched thuggery, Rico positions herself as rage rap’s premier imagineer. A student of Gucci Mane and Chief Keef, she’s long made music that’s as technicolor as it is hard, but on <em>Rx</em>, she ups the ante, punching in <a href="https://youtu.be/7NmtmA-Qoas?t=61&amp;ref=hearingthings.co"><u>gomu-gomu</u></a> blitzes that arc and whip across Blume’s Day-Glo beats. The record dials back the sonic and lyrical intensity that characterize rage, recasting the subgenre as candy-coated and radiant, with Rico at the center of the mosh. Rico’s rage doesn’t just thrash and throttle; it strobes and streaks—smearing words into the odd textures favored by the Opium boys without draining them of meaning. It’s as if she’s taken Playboi Carti’s sprawling, messy <em>Music</em> and compressed it into a pocket universe where drill and crank, plugg and bop, hyperpop and bubblegum trap all coexist.</p>
<p>Key to that smooth flux is her virtuosic rapping, which is casually artisanal. Like Chike, Rico’s not nonchalant or monotone. In fact, she’s outright theatrical, fond of slipping into playground cadences, Valley Girl impressions, and giggling like a supervillain. But these tics all serve to set up schemes or bridge ideas, turning the songs into hydraulic coasters that bounce and loop but always barrel forward. “Treat a bitch like a song I don’t want, I fuck around/Call her Pinocchi, that bitch nosey/Toss her around, ring a rosey, holy moly,” she raps on “<a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5RWIJ-qbpYM&amp;list=RD5RWIJ-qbpYM&amp;start_radio=1&amp;ref=hearingthings.co">Butthead</a>,” shouting each line while still having the control to harden and soften words for humor or menace.</p>
<p>Rico punches in a lot of ways. She’ll offer a compliment then withdraw it (“I would call you my sons, but you bitches ugly”), or make a statement and clarify it with negation (“Horses on my car, not my shirt, this ain’t rugby”). She loves puns (“Can’t clock shit while you pocket watchin’, go and pay a bill”), mocking comparisons (“My palms is itchin’, you pick at your crotch”), zingers (“Rockstar pussy and it got a mohawk”), and combinations of the above (“She not a baddie, her panties granny”).</p>
<p>She uses “like” and the hashtag flow, too, when she’s inclined, though never in predictable ways. She actually tends to naturalize these technical displays, not really showboating or calling attention to them; it’s like she’s in a perpetual flow state, and brilliant shit just happens. That repose heightens the feeling that this level of rapping is easy for her. She makes music about being young, rich, and Black, ultimately, her theatricality a means of conveying her fullness. For Rico, rage isn’t just darkness and mayhem; it’s passion, vim, and irritation that flares into pride. Controlling her fury, working the shadows and the light, is more freeing than simply unleashing it.</p>

<p>In all these songs, from the run-ons of 22 Pooruhn to the unamused sighs of Karrahbooo, I hear artists testing the limits of words—probing how far language can bend while retaining verve and depth. Shadow punching strikes me as a reclamation of “mumble rap,” a term that was an insult, then a rally cry, then a meme, but that increasingly feels avant-garde. It really is a provocation: What if rap—the genre of loud Blackness, of pronounced rebellion, of raw id and fuck-you-pay-me bluntness—embraced distortion? Rage and its offshoots have explored this question by splintering the voice and boosting the low-end to concussive levels. The shadow punchers seem to be pursuing another path, one in which the voice is steady, but words lean and lurch, dodge and deflect. The message is discernible to some but not all. And all speech takes on the opaque grammar of the inside joke. If you know, you laugh. If you don’t, you lean in closer.</p>
