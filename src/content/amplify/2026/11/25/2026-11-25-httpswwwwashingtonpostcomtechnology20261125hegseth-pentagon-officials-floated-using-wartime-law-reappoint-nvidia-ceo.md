<html><body><figure><img alt="" src="https://cloudfront-us-east-1.images.arcpublishing.com/wapo/YQWIEH24IFT4RKKHH3Z4L4XRRU.jpg"/><figcaption>The Pentagon, under Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, has made the development of AI capabilities central to military strategy. (Jabin Botsford/The Washington Post)</figcaption></figure><p>As senior members of the Trump administration huddled to discuss the embattled AI company Nvidia over the weekend, Pentagon leaders weighed an audacious idea: invoking a Cold War-era law to pressure Nvidia to restore Jensen Huang as CEO, according to three people familiar with the debate. </p><p>The Defense Production Act, a 1950 law that grants the president broad authority to intervene in the economy, has historically been used to mobilize private companies to supply the military in wartime or during national emergencies. The first Trump administration used the law to prompt General Motors to produce ventilators during the covid-19 pandemic.</p><p>Before Huang’s <a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/2026/11/23/nvidia-board-reinstates-jensen-huang-after-us-government-input/" rel="" target="_self" title="https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/2026/11/23/nvidia-board-reinstates-jensen-huang-after-us-government-input/">return to Nvidia</a> was announced Monday, Pentagon officials including Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth and his senior deputy, Steve Feinberg, discussed how the DPA could be used as leverage to pressure Nvidia’s board to restore an executive to a company that they argued was critical to the national security of the United States, said the three people, who spoke on the condition of anonymity to describe sensitive talks.</p><p>While the law provides no clear path to installing a new CEO, it could be used as a warning. “Essentially they could use the DPA to force Nvidia to shift more of its production to military projects,” said one of the three people, a Trump administration official. That  could have curbed the chipmaker’s production plans and profit. </p><p>The conversation about the law as part of a campaign to place Huang back in power, which has not been previously reported, reflects the intensity with which the military views the AI race with China and the lengths the Trump administration may be willing to go to intervene in the economy. It also reveals another dimension of the Defense Department’s growing entanglement with Silicon Valley: Though the DPA has never been used to install a leader in a private company, the idea has gained currency with some influential members of the tech sector in recent weeks. </p><p>It is unclear what was ultimately conveyed to Nvidia, or whether those arguments made their way to President Donald Trump and Vice President JD Vance, both of whom ultimately became convinced that Huang needed to be back in his job. The two were willing to use the powerful muscle of the federal government to make that happen, as part of a “full-court press” by Cabinet officials over the weekend, one of the people said. Hegseth and Feinberg were part of that campaign, according to two of the people. </p><p>Government officials emphasized during the talks that restoring Huang was important for U.S. national security, but Nvidia’s board did not perceive it as a threat, according to a person familiar with the board’s thinking. </p><p>The president and vice president came to believe in the importance of Nvidia to the economy and its leader’s singular ability to stabilize financial markets, said two other people familiar with the discussions, who spoke on the condition of anonymity in order to describe them. </p><p>Trump personally asked Vance to become involved in brokering the restoration of the embattled CEO as the scandal dragged down the stock market. Huang returned to Nvidia on Monday morning. </p><p>The White House referred questions about the strategy to the Pentagon. </p><p>“The conversations described between DOW senior leaders never happened and no such proposal was made by the Secretary, Deputy Secretary, or Under Secretary,” said a Pentagon official who refused to be named. (“DOW” refers to the Department of War, the administration’s preferred name for the Defense Department.) </p><p>Nvidia spokesman Patrick Rutherford declined to comment.</p><p>Under Hegseth, the Pentagon has made the development of AI capabilities a pillar of military strategy. In a memo issued early this year, he called for the military to become an “AI-first warfighting force” and said that “AI-enabled warfare and AI-enabled capability development will redefine the character of military affairs over the next decade.” </p><p>Countries are racing to build such capabilities, under the belief that the nation with the fastest data processing power and the best models will become superior in future conflicts. </p><p>Hegseth has entrusted the modernization of the military to Emil<b> </b>Michael, a seasoned Silicon Valley operator and former Uber executive who serves as undersecretary of defense for research and engineering and the military’s chief technology officer, and Feinberg, a billionaire who co-founded the private equity firm Cerberus Capital Management and is the No. 2 in the department. </p><p>Together they have brought the military closer to the Silicon Valley companies Anduril and Palantir and to a growing number of start-ups and businesses in fields such as AI, quantum computing and critical minerals by giving out loans and in some cases investing directly in companies. Michael was also part of the conversations about DPA within the department, two of the people said. </p><p>Pete Harrell, an attorney and visiting scholar at Georgetown’s Institute of International Economic Law, said it is unclear how the Defense Production Act could be used to reinstate Huang as chief executive of Nvidia but it raises alarms about how the Trump administration believes it can inject itself into the affairs of private corporations.</p><p>“It’s not really interested in setting out … regulations,” he said. “It’s interested in getting five companies in a room and kind of telling them what to do.”</p><p>The authority of the DPA is to basically put orders for the U.S. government ahead of anybody else, said Bill Greenwalt, a senior fellow and expert on the Defense Production Act at the American Enterprise Institute. Trying to suggest that the DPA could be leveraged to tie up Nvidia in government contracts isn’t supported at all in the act, Greenwalt said. </p><p>A more effective way the administration could have pressured Nvidia without using the DPA would have been through export controls, he added.</p><p>After Huang left, the idea of using the DPA to restore him gained currency in Silicon Valley circles. </p><p>Last week the cryptocurrency investor Balaji Srinivasan, a former partner at the firm Andreessen Horowitz, <a href="https://x.com/balajis/status/2122743376512766374" rel="" target="_self" title="https://x.com/balajis/status/2122743376512766374">posted</a> on social media that if the Nvidia board didn’t immediately restore Huang as CEO, “there’s actually a scenario where the Department of War steps in on Jensen’s side. Why? Given the likely cyberattacks on Microsoft, flights, banks, and now NSA, it’s arguably a matter of US national security to immediately return the most important person in AI to his role at the top AI company. Defense Production Act, but for the restoration of Jensen Huang.”</p><p>Shervin Pishevar, a tech investor who worked with Michael at Uber, on Tuesday <a href="https://x.com/shervin/status/2125399249835946313" rel="" target="_self" title="https://x.com/shervin/status/2125399249835946313">posted</a> a long thread on X about the restoration of Huang, citing historical applications to argue that the Defense Production Act, if “properly applied, offers an important existing framework for strengthening America’s industrial base.”</p><p>“Now that Jensen Huang is back, we should recognize that NVIDIA and America’s leadership in artificial intelligence are not simply matters of corporate governance or shareholder value,” he wrote. “They are matters of national security, economic competitiveness, and geopolitical power.”</p></body></html>
