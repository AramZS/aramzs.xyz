<p>After years of chasing their white whale—rural voters—North Carolina Democrats finally have something to show for it. With historic wins up and down the ballot, they delivered what George W. Bush once called a “<a href="https://www.nbcnews.com/id/wbna16279643">thumpin</a>’” after his Republican Party’s losses in the 2006 midterms.</p>
<p>The 2026 thumpin’ was evident in rural and urban parts of the state. After the new Congress is sworn in, Democrats will control the state’s three least densely populated congressional districts: the 1st, 3rd, and 11th. In the latter, Democratic Rep.-elect <a href="https://www.theassemblync.com/news/politics/elections/jamie-ager-democrat-nc11-jennifer-balkcom-mountains/">Jamie Ager</a> even received a majority of the vote in Swain County, which is at the far western edge of the state and more than an hour from the nearest Target.</p>
<p>But do those victories mark a turn in rural North Carolina’s long move toward Republicans, or did a Democratic wave simply flood every part of the state?</p>
<h2>The Long Road Back</h2>
<p><a href="https://www.theassemblync.com/news/politics/north-carolina-democratic-party/">Anderson Clayton</a> was elected chair of the N.C. Democrats at a low point for the party. In the 2022 election, Republicans won the U.S. Senate race, secured a supermajority in the state Senate, and flipped control of the state Supreme Court.</p>

<p>The party’s struggles were especially pronounced in rural North Carolina, where Democrats lost much of their historical foothold in state legislative and local offices. In the average rural county, Democratic U.S. Senate candidate Cheri Beasley received just 37.2% of the vote.</p>
<p>The Democrats’ decline was a slow process. In 1992, the average urban county was still slightly more Republican than the average rural county. As the graph below demonstrates, that relationship has reversed dramatically. In 2024, Donald Trump received about 63% of the presidential vote in the average rural county, compared with about 37% in the average urban county.</p>

<p>Clayton, a Roxboro native who was the Person County party chair, was elected state party chair in 2023 at least partly on her pitch that this rural decline was not inevitable—that Democrats could once again compete in rural areas. As <a href="https://ncnewsline.com/2026/05/29/nc-democratic-party-leader-anderson-clayton-runs-to-the-problem-engaging-voters/">she put it</a> in May 2026, “Rural North Carolina didn’t leave us overnight, but we’ve got to invest in it for the long haul.”</p>
<p>In 2026, Democrats got the election they had been waiting for. Roy Cooper beat Michael Whatley by almost 20 percentage points, the <a href="https://chriscooperwcu.substack.com/p/wow-just-wow">largest margin</a> for a Senate election in the state since 1974. Democrats are also poised to flip seven congressional seats, the largest number of Democratic seat gains in any state and two more than in California. They won control of the state House and gained four seats in the Senate.</p>
<p>Cooper improved on Beasley’s 2022 Senate vote share in all 100 counties. Some of the gains were truly eye-popping; in the average rural county, Cooper’s vote share was 9.8 percentage points higher.</p>
<p>It wasn’t even just the <a href="https://www.theassemblync.com/news/politics/roy-cooper-rural-voters/">Cooper juggernaut</a> that made Democrats’ rural fortunes rise. The party won in places like state House District 119, which covers Jackson, Swain, and Transylvania counties. Democrat Mark Burrows defeated appointed Republican incumbent Anna Ferguson by 4.8 percentage points. Two years ago, Republicans carried the district by nearly 11 points.</p>
<p>In the northeast, Sam Davis III defeated incumbent Bill Ward by 5.9 percentage points in House District 5, which Republicans won by 8.3 points in 2024. And in House District 7, covering Franklin and part of Vance County, Mark Speed won by 3.2 points in a district Republicans had carried by 13.2 points two years earlier.</p>
<p>Local elections weren’t immune, either. Democrats won county commission seats in unlikely places like Scotland and Washington counties. In <a href="https://smokymountainnews.com/news/news-latest/jackson-democrats-flip-county-commission/">Jackson County</a>, Democrats flipped all three county commission seats on the ballot, bringing the commission from unanimous Republican control to majority Democratic control overnight. In neighboring Transylvania County, Democrats Owen Carson and Bryan O’Neill <a href="https://er.ncsbe.gov/?election_dt=11/03/2026&amp;county_id=88&amp;office=ALL&amp;contest=1">won</a> both available commission seats.</p>

<p>Some of the Democratic Party’s newest stars also come from more rural areas. Ager may hail from relatively populous Buncombe County, but he’s a farmer with a demeanor that doesn’t exactly scream urban hipster; his victory party featured a bluegrass band and hay bales. Richard Ojeda, who ousted Richard Hudson in the 9th Congressional District, was seen as a <a href="https://www.politico.com/magazine/story/2018/03/02/richard-ojeda-west-virginia-blue-army-one-217217/">rising rural star</a> when he was a state legislator in West Virginia. He now lives in Moore County.</p>
<p>The rising profile of small-town mayors like <a href="https://www.theassemblync.com/news/politics/zeb-smathers-canton-mil-closure/">Zeb Smathers</a> also suggests that perhaps the promise of a competitive rural North Carolina has arrived.</p>
<h2>Remember the Context</h2>
<p>But before Democrats declare themselves the party of rural voters, they should consider what happened elsewhere: The same election that produced this year’s victories generated even larger gains in urban and suburban places.</p>
<p>Cooper gained 9.8 percentage points over the 2022 midterms in the average rural county, compared with 10.4 points in the average urban county and 12.4 points in the average suburban or regional-city county. His three biggest county-level gains came in Johnston, Cabarrus, and Gaston, all <a href="https://www.ncruralcenter.org/how-we-define-rural/">classified</a> as suburban or regional-city counties by the N.C. Rural Center.</p>
<p>And gaining ground is different from winning. Cooper received 47 percent of the vote in the average rural county. He still lost in 56 of 100 counties, including nearly two-thirds of the state’s 78 rural counties, compared with seven of 16 suburban or regional-city counties. He won every urban county.</p>
<p>Republicans won 65.7% of the rural county commission seats on the ballot this year and retained a majority of rural General Assembly districts.</p>
<p>The 2026 election may mark the end of the long Republican advance in the state’s less densely populated places. But one election, even one that delivered a thumpin’, cannot prove–much less sustain–a rural realignment.</p>
